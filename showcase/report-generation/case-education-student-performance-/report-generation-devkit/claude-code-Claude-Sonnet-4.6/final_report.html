Executive Summary

This report analyses academic outcomes for 1,000 Bangladeshi secondary students across eight administrative districts, examining how demographic, socio-economic, and behavioural factors interact with HSC results — the terminal upper-secondary examination used for university admissions.

Overall performance is strong and homogeneous. The average HSC GPA is 4.01 (on a 5.0 scale), 26.8% of students achieve Excellent (≥4.5), and — notably — no student falls below the 3.0 at-risk threshold. This near-absence of low performers is a striking finding that suggests either effective baseline support or a floor effect in this sample.

Socio-economic factors show weaker-than-expected effects. Family income, internet access, and private tuition each produce differences of less than 0.05 GPA points — far below policy-action thresholds. Parent education shows the largest differentiation: Secondary-educated parents correlate with the highest average HSC (4.05), while Primary-educated parents correlate with the lowest (3.96), a gap of 0.09 points.

Regional and institutional disparities are modest but real. Barisal leads districts at 4.11 average HSC; Rangpur trails at 3.91 — a 0.20-point spread. On school type, public schools marginally outperform private schools (4.02 vs. 4.01). The GPA Progression Delta of +0.018 indicates a system where students collectively hold steady rather than dramatically improve or decline.

Policy implication: The greatest leverage for improvement lies in addressing geographic gaps (particularly in Rangpur, Khulna, and Mymensingh) and ensuring the high SSC-to-HSC transition supports students whose SSC outpaced their HSC — 48.4% of students declined in GPA between their previous record and HSC.

● High Priority
1. Targeted HSC Transition Support in Rangpur & Khulna
With Rangpur averaging 3.91 HSC GPA and 20.3% Excellent rate — both lowest nationally — structured bridge programmes for SSC-to-HSC transitions are urgently needed. Khulna (3.95) also warrants district-level academic support teams. Allocate additional teacher training resources and diagnostic testing in these two regions.
● High Priority
2. Address the 48.4% GPA Decline Rate at HSC
Nearly half of all students experience GPA decline from their previous academic record to the HSC exam. Implement early-warning systems that identify declining students mid-year, and offer structured academic counselling focused on HSC-specific examination strategy. This affects students across all income levels and districts equally.
● Medium Priority
3. Parental Engagement Programmes over Income Transfers
Parent education level shows the largest socio-economic effect (0.086 GPA pts from Primary to Secondary). Income alone has negligible impact. Redirect resources from pure financial assistance towards structured parental literacy and education involvement programmes — particularly for families with only Primary-level education (n=235, lowest Excellent rate: 24.7%).
● Medium Priority
4. Investigate Dhaka Public School Excellence Model
Dhaka public schools outperform private schools by +0.234 GPA points — the largest district-level school-type gap in the dataset. A systematic qualitative investigation of what Dhaka public schools do differently (teacher quality, curriculum coverage, administrative practices) could yield transferable best practices for other districts.
● Medium Priority
5. Reframe SSC–HSC Curriculum Alignment
The 0.244-point drop from SSC (4.26) to HSC (4.01) suggests a systemic mismatch. Conduct a curriculum alignment study comparing SSC and HSC syllabi and assessment formats. Consider whether SSC-level coaching prepares students appropriately for HSC demands, or whether remediation strategies are needed between the two examination cycles.
● Contextual Priority
6. Reassess Tuition & Internet-Access Policy Assumptions
Private tuition (+0.012 GPA pts) and internet access (+0.017 GPA pts) both show negligible effects. Before scaling expensive tuition subsidies or connectivity programmes on academic-outcome grounds alone, conduct deeper analysis of usage patterns. The effectiveness of internet access appears contingent on parent education level — standalone connectivity investment without content guidance may not yield expected returns.

Data Limitations & Next Steps

This analysis is based on 1,000 student records. While structurally sound for pattern identification, the following limitations should inform policy decisions:

  • The near-zero at-risk rate (0%) may reflect sample selection bias rather than universal educational equity.
  • Key unmeasured variables include teacher quality, school infrastructure, peer effects, and student motivation.
  • Cross-sectional data prevents causal inference — correlations identified here do not establish causation.
  • District-level sample sizes (117–133 per district) are adequate for comparison but insufficient for sub-group analysis within districts.
  • Longitudinal tracking of the same students across Previous GPA → SSC → HSC would strengthen conclusions about progression patterns.
