3 Socio-Economic Factors and Their Impact on Performance

This is the most critical section for policy analysts. Analysis of how family income, parent education, internet access, and private tuition correlate with HSC outcomes.

📌 Critical Data Finding: After rigorous computation across all four socio-economic dimensions, no statistically meaningful relationship between any socio-economic variable and HSC performance is detected in this dataset. Family income (r = −0.008), parent education (max spread = 0.09 GPA), internet access (difference = 0.018 GPA), and private tuition (difference = 0.012 GPA) all show near-zero effect sizes. This is reported faithfully and directly from the data — any claim of strong socio-economic determination in this specific dataset would not be data-grounded.

💹

Income Quartile Findings

Mean HSC across income quartiles ranges only from 3.992 (Q2) to 4.025 (Q3) — a 0.033-point spread over a 10,000–59,767 BDT income range. No income advantage in HSC performance is detectable in this sample.

🎓

Parent Education Non-Linearity

Counterintuitively, Secondary-educated parents produce the highest mean HSC children (4.050), exceeding Graduate-educated parents (4.001). Primary-educated parents show the lowest mean (3.964) — but the 0.086-point range is not substantively meaningful.

📡

Internet Access: Marginal Edge

Students with internet access average 4.022 vs 4.004 for those without — a difference of 0.018 GPA points. With 478 vs 522 students in each group, this difference is practically negligible in this dataset.

✍️

Private Tuition: No Clear Advantage

Private tuition students average 4.019 HSC vs 4.007 for non-tuition students — a 0.012 GPA difference. This dataset does not support claims of tuition-driven performance advantages at HSC level.

⚠ Data Integrity Note: These near-zero effect sizes may reflect: (1) the dataset being a stratified sample where selection already controlled for SES variation; (2) Bangladesh's education system providing sufficient access-equalization at this level; or (3) sample size constraints limiting power to detect small effects. Analysts should treat this as a signal to expand data collection rather than conclude that socio-economic factors are irrelevant to Bangladesh education outcomes.

Key Behavioral Insights
Paradox — High Attendance, Lower HSC: Students in the 90–100% attendance bucket average 3.948 HSC, which is lower than students in the 80–89% bucket (4.082). This counter-intuitive pattern suggests attendance alone is not a reliable predictor of HSC performance in this dataset.
Study Hours Non-Linearity: Students studying 11–20 hrs/week average 3.986 HSC vs 4.025 for those studying 21–29 hrs/week — a marginal difference. Low-study students (1–10 hrs) actually average 4.034. Correlation of −0.022 confirms no meaningful linear relationship.
Policy Implication: In this dataset, neither attendance mandates nor study-hour prescriptions appear to drive HSC outcomes linearly. Quality of engagement, not quantity, may be the more relevant lever — though this would require richer data (e.g., classroom performance metrics) to confirm.

Policy Recommendations
  • 1
    High Priority

    Launch Targeted HSC Transition Support Programs in Rangpur and Khulna

    Rangpur (HSC mean: 3.909, delta: −0.057) and Khulna (HSC mean: 3.946, delta: −0.006) are the two districts where students are actively regressing between Previous GPA and HSC. Deploy HSC-specific academic support — remedial instruction, counseling, and enhanced teacher training — concentrated in these two regions. Rajshahi's positive delta (+0.078) provides a potential peer-learning model to replicate.

  • 2
    High Priority

    Address the SSC-to-HSC Performance Attrition (System-Wide)

    The 0.25-point average decline from SSC (4.26) to HSC (4.01), combined with 48.4% of students declining individually, points to a structural difficulty escalation at HSC level. Curriculum review, standardized bridging coursework, and early identification of students at risk of regression between SSC and HSC results should be institutionalized. Schools should track GPA Progression Delta as a primary performance KPI.

  • 3
    Medium Priority

    Investigate Quality-Over-Quantity in Learning Engagement

    Neither attendance (r = −0.014) nor study hours (r = −0.022) show positive correlations with HSC outcomes. Policymakers should move beyond blanket attendance enforcement and study-hour mandates, investing instead in research on pedagogical quality, active learning engagement, and student comprehension tracking. This requires richer classroom-level data collection than currently available in this dataset.

  • 4
    Medium Priority

    Expand and Improve Data Collection for Socio-Economic Analysis

    The near-zero correlations between all socio-economic variables and HSC outcomes in this dataset (income r = −0.008, internet access gap = 0.018 GPA) are notable but should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of socio-economic neutrality in Bangladesh's education system. A larger, nationally representative sample with more granular SES measures is needed to draw policy-relevant conclusions. Consider integrating household survey data with school records.

  • 5
    Monitoring

    Re-evaluate Public vs. Private School Assumptions

    Public school students marginally outperform private school students in HSC (4.018 vs 4.007). In several districts, public schools lead private schools (e.g., Barisal: 4.152 vs 4.069; Dhaka: 4.179 vs 3.945). This challenges the assumption that private schooling universally confers academic advantage at the higher secondary level. Resource allocation decisions should be evidence-based rather than institution-type based.
