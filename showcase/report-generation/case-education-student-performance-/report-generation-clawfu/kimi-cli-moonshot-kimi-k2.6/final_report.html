Executive Summary

This report analyzes 1,000 student records from eight districts in Bangladesh to identify the key drivers of academic success at the HSC level. The dataset spans demographic, socio-economic, behavioral, and institutional dimensions.

Key Finding: The national average HSC GPA is 4.013, with 26.8% of students achieving excellent performance (≥4.5). However, a significant geographic disparity exists: Barisal leads with an average HSC GPA of 4.11, while Rangpur trails at 3.91—a gap of 0.20 points. Notably, the overall GPA progression delta is slightly positive (+0.018), suggesting marginal system-wide improvement from previous GPA to HSC, but nearly half of all students (48.4%) experienced a decline.

Surprisingly, conventional predictors show weak effects in this dataset: family income correlates only −0.007 with HSC results, attendance correlates −0.014, and study hours correlate −0.022. The most meaningful differences emerge at the district and school-type levels, where institutional context appears to outweigh individual behavioral inputs.

3. Socio-Economic Factors and Their Impact on Performance

This section examines how family income, parent education, internet access, and private tuition relate to HSC outcomes. Effect sizes and mean differences are reported for all comparisons.

Parent Education

Parent education shows a non-linear relationship with student HSC performance. Students whose parents completed Secondary education achieve the highest mean HSC GPA (4.050), followed by Higher Secondary (4.035), Graduate (4.001), and Primary (3.964). The effect size between Primary and Secondary is small (Cohen's d = 0.146), and differences between Secondary, Higher Secondary, and Graduate are negligible. Statistical tests confirm no significant differences (all p > 0.10).

Policy Insight: The absence of a clear gradient suggests that parent education alone is not a strong lever for HSC improvement in this cohort. Interventions should not exclusively target students with less-educated parents, as the performance overlap across groups is substantial.

Internet Access & Private Tuition

Internet access shows a marginal positive association: students with access average 4.022 versus 4.004 without (difference = +0.017, Cohen's d = 0.029, p = 0.647). Private tuition shows an even smaller effect: 4.019 with tuition versus 4.007 without (difference = +0.012, Cohen's d = 0.020, p = 0.746). Neither difference is statistically significant.

Caution: While internet access and private tuition are often assumed to be strong predictors, this dataset shows no meaningful effect. This may reflect universal saturation (both are near 50% prevalence), measurement limitations, or the possibility that quality of use matters more than binary access.

Combined Socio-Economic Effects

Cross-tabulating internet access and private tuition reveals no interaction: the highest-performing subgroup is "Internet + Tuition" (4.023), but the lowest is "No Internet + No Tuition" (3.993)—a gap of only 0.030 points. Similarly, parent education combined with internet access shows no consistent pattern. The data do not support a simple "resource accumulation" model of academic success.

4. Study Behavior and Attendance Patterns

This section investigates the relationship between study hours, attendance, and HSC outcomes.

Study Hours per Week

The correlation between weekly study hours and HSC GPA is −0.022, indicating no meaningful linear relationship. When binned into Low (≤10h), Medium (11–20h), and High (>20h), mean HSC GPAs are 4.034, 3.986, and 4.025 respectively. The medium group underperforms slightly, but differences are negligible.

Interpretation: More study hours do not guarantee better outcomes. This may reflect differences in study quality, school-level teaching effectiveness, or reverse causation (weaker students studying more to compensate). Policy should focus on structured study support rather than simply increasing hours.

Attendance

Attendance correlates −0.014 with HSC results—a counter-intuitive weak negative association. Mean HSC GPA by attendance tier: Low (≤70%) = 4.019, Medium (71–85%) = 4.036, High (>85%) = 3.982. The highest-attendance group actually scores lowest on average.

One plausible explanation is that students with weaker academic foundations attend more to compensate, while higher-performing students may miss classes for private coaching or self-study. Alternatively, school quality may vary: students in less effective schools attend regularly but learn less per hour. Regardless, attendance alone is not a reliable predictor of HSC success in this dataset.

6. Key Findings and Policy Recommendations

The following recommendations are derived directly from the dataset and prioritized by effect magnitude and policy feasibility.

1

Prioritize District-Level Interventions Over Individual Resource Provision

Family income, internet access, and private tuition show negligible effects on HSC outcomes. In contrast, district-level mean HSC GPA varies by 0.20 points and progression delta varies by 0.135 points. Funding should shift from student-level stipends or device distribution toward district education office capacity building, teacher training, and curriculum alignment—especially in Rangpur, Khulna, and Mymensingh.

2

Investigate and Replicate Dhaka's Public School Model

Dhaka public schools achieve the highest mean HSC GPA of any district-school combination (4.179) and a strong positive progression delta (+0.176 for public). A structured qualitative and operational study of Dhaka public schools—focusing on pedagogy, assessment practices, and administrative support—should be conducted to identify replicable practices for other districts.

3

Address the "HSC Dip" Through Transition Support

Mean HSC GPA (4.01) is 0.24 points below mean SSC GPA (4.26), and 48.4% of students decline from Previous GPA to HSC. Targeted bridge programs during the SSC-to-HSC transition—focusing on subject-depth adjustment, study-skills training, and early academic monitoring—could mitigate this systemic drop.

4

Design Structured Study Programs, Not Hour Targets

Study hours and attendance are weakly or negatively correlated with HSC performance. Policy should move away from minimum-hour mandates and toward quality-assured study frameworks: guided problem sets, peer study groups, and formative feedback loops. The medium study-hour group (11–20h/week) actually underperforms, suggesting diminishing or negative returns to unstructured study time.

5

Launch an Urgent Intervention Package for Rangpur

Rangpur ranks last on mean HSC GPA (3.91), last on progression delta (−0.057), and has the highest below-3.5 rate (32.5%). A multi-pronged intervention—including targeted teacher coaching, additional academic support hours, and district-level performance monitoring—should be piloted and evaluated within 12 months.

6

Disaggregate School-Type Policy by District

Public schools outperform private schools in Dhaka, Sylhet, and Barisal, but underperform in Rajshahi and Khulna. A one-size-fits-all public-vs-private policy is inappropriate. Instead, regulatory oversight and quality assurance should be applied differentially based on local performance data, with underperforming institutions (regardless of type) receiving targeted support or intervention.

Data Limitations and Future Research

All findings are bound by the dataset's scope (n=1,000, no missing values). The absence of students with HSC GPA <3.0 suggests either selection bias or a floor effect. Causal inference is not possible with observational data; the correlations and mean differences reported here describe associations, not causal effects. Future research should incorporate school-level identifiers, teacher qualification data, and qualitative process measures to explain why district and institutional differences exist.
