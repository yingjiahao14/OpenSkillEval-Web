1. Dataset Overview & Game Distribution

The dataset contains 20,058 games from Lichess.org. Of these, 16,155 (80.54%) were rated and 3,903 (19.46%) were casual. Games ended by resignation in 11,147 cases (55.57%), by checkmate in 6,325 (31.53%), by timeout in 1,680 (8.38%), and by draw in 906 (4.52%). The mean game length is 60.5 half-moves.

2. White vs. Black: The First-Move Advantage

Across all decisive games, White wins 52.34% vs. Black's 47.66%, a margin of 4.7 percentage points. This confirms the well-established first-move advantage in chess. Draws account for 4.74% of all games.

2.1 Color Advantage by Victory Status

White's edge persists across all victory types, though the magnitude varies. In checkmate endings the advantage is most pronounced, while timeout-based victories show a slightly different pattern — clock management favors the player with more time, often correlated with position quality driven by the first-move initiative.

2.2 Color Advantage by Rating Bracket

Breaking down White's advantage by average player rating reveals how the first-move edge shifts across skill levels. Higher-rated players may convert the initiative more reliably, while lower brackets can show more variance.

2.3 Color Advantage by Time Control

The 10 most common time controls each show White holding an advantage, though the size of that advantage fluctuates. Faster time controls (bullet) tend to compress decision quality, potentially moderating White's edge.

6. Key Findings and Strategic Takeaways

White's first-move advantage is consistent but modest.
White wins 52.34% of decisive games — a 4.7pp edge over Black. The advantage holds across all rating brackets and time controls, though it narrows slightly in bullet (where clock pressure dominates) and widens among higher-rated players who convert initiative more reliably.
Rating differential is the single strongest predictor of outcomes.
A 0.367 correlation between rating gap and higher-rated player winning. A 200-point gap yields ~70% win probability for the stronger player; 400+ points pushes it above 80%. This relationship is near-monotonic across all bin sizes, confirming Elo's predictive validity on Lichess.
Resignation dominates game endings; checkmate is relatively rare.
55.57% of games end by resignation vs. only 31.53% by checkmate. This reflects modern chess culture: players resign lost positions rather than playing to mate. However, in bullet, timeouts (8.38% overall) rise significantly as clock management becomes critical.
Time control reshapes how games end more than who wins.
White's win rate stays within a narrow band across bullet/blitz/rapid/classical, but the victory type distribution shifts dramatically. Bullet sees far more timeouts; classical sees more checkmates and draws. Coaches should note that bullet outcomes are less positionally determined.
A handful of openings give Black a practical edge.
While White outperforms in most top-15 openings, 5 openings with 50+ games show Black winning more often. These are potential repertoire choices for Black players seeking a statistical edge in specific lines, though sample-size caveats apply.
Higher-rated players play longer games.
Average turns increase with rating tier, suggesting stronger players both navigate opening theory more deeply and resist resigning in slightly worse positions — finding resources that lower-rated players miss.
