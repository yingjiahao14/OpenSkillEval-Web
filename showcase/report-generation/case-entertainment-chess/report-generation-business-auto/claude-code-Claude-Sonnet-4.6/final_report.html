The dataset spans 20,058 Lichess games, of which 80.5% are rated (16,155 games) and the remaining 19.5% are casual/unrated. The overwhelming majority of games end in a decisive result — only 4.7% (950 games) end in draws, well below the draw rates seen in over-the-board and elite correspondence play.

The low draw rate reflects the online blitz/rapid context, where time pressure and playing-strength variance produce decisive results far more frequently than in classical tournament chess.

Color Advantage by Victory Status

White leads in both checkmate (52.9%) and resignation (52.4%) outcomes. The only category where black leads is outoftime: when games end on the clock, black wins 50.3% of those games — suggesting white may play faster or reach better positions before flagging.

The first-move advantage is a well-documented phenomenon in chess theory, and this dataset confirms it at the amateur online level. White's 52.3% decisive-game win rate is consistent with published statistics from Lichess and Chess.com studies (typically 52–56% across formats). The advantage is present but not deterministic — 47.7% of decisive games are won by black.

Notably, white's advantage shrinks as rating increases: in the 2000+ bracket, white wins only 50.6% of decisive games, versus 53.8% in the 1200–1399 range. This suggests that stronger players are better equipped to neutralize white's first-move initiative. The timeout anomaly (black winning 50.3% of timed-out games) is a small but consistent pattern and may reflect time management differences across color assignments.

Victory Status Distribution by Rating Tier

Checkmate rate drops dramatically with skill: 39.8% of sub-1200 games end in mate versus just 14.4% at 2000+. Higher-rated players recognize lost positions sooner and resign rather than fight to mate.

The relationship between rating differential and win probability is the most analytically compelling finding in this dataset. When two players are separated by fewer than 25 Elo points, the higher-rated player wins only 51.9% of decisive games — barely better than a coin flip. At a 50–99 point gap, that rises to 58.3%. At 200–299 points, the advantage climbs to 75.2%, and beyond 500 points, the higher-rated player wins 87.6% of decisive games.

Game length also scales meaningfully with rating. Sub-1200 players average 50.0 plies per game, while 2000+ players average 68.1 plies — a 36% difference. This reflects longer, more complex games at higher levels where both players are capable of sustaining complex positions.

The Scandinavian Defense: Mieses-Kotroc Variation is the starkest white-favoring opening in the top 15 — white wins 64.8% of decisive games. Conversely, the Van't Kruijs Opening (1.e3) is the most black-favoring, with black winning 64.2% of decisive games. This likely reflects a selection effect: players who open with 1.e3 tend to be less experienced or lower-rated, while black's response is more natural.

The Sicilian Defense (both core and Bowdler Attack variants) consistently favors black at the amateur level, contradicting the perception that the Sicilian is merely a drawing weapon. Opening ply depth also correlates with sophistication: deeper openings (Scotch Game at 6.0 ply, Italian Game at 6.0 ply) tend to show results closer to the expected white advantage, while shallow openings (Van't Kruijs at 1.0 ply, Scandinavian at 2.0 ply) show wider swings.

Rapid (10–29 minute) games dominate the dataset at 73.3%, with 10+0 being the single most common time control (7,721 games, 38.5%). This is consistent with Lichess's user base and the platform's promotion of 10-minute games.

Classical games (≥30 min base time) show a markedly higher draw rate of 7.8% versus 4.2–4.3% for blitz and rapid — as expected, longer thinking time produces more balanced outcomes. Classical games also have the highest resignation rate (61.8%), suggesting players at longer time controls are more willing to resign clearly lost positions rather than playing on for a flag.

Timeout rates decrease monotonically with longer time controls: 15.3% in bullet, 10.3% in blitz, 8.2% in rapid, and 3.5% in classical. This is mechanically expected — shorter games leave less buffer, but it's worth noting that even in rapid games, roughly 1 in 12 decisive results is a flag rather than a true chess outcome.

Key Findings and Strategic Takeaways

The following synthesis distills the most actionable patterns from all analyses. Each finding is derived directly from the dataset and has practical implications for players, coaches, and platform designers.

Finding 01
+4.7pp

White's First-Move Advantage Is Real But Modest

White wins 52.3% of decisive games — a consistent but surmountable edge. The advantage narrows to just 50.6% at 2000+ ratings, confirming that skill increasingly neutralizes color. For players below 1600, color assignment meaningfully affects expected win rate.

Finding 02
87.6%

Rating Is the Dominant Predictor of Game Outcome

At a 500+ Elo gap, the higher-rated player wins 87.6% of decisive games. Even a 200–299 Elo gap produces a 75.2% win rate for the stronger player. For matchmaking purposes, games within 25 Elo are effectively coin flips (51.9%), and rating brackets below 50 Elo are highly competitive.

Finding 03
64.2%

Van't Kruijs (1.e3) Is the Worst-Performing White Opening

The Van't Kruijs Opening gives black a decisive 64.2% win rate — the largest single-opening disadvantage for white in the top 15. The Sicilian Defense (56.6%) and Indian Game (59.2%) also consistently favor black. Opening choice at the amateur level has larger outcome effects than theory would suggest.

Finding 04
64.8%

Scandinavian Defense: Mieses-Kotroc Is White's Best Weapon

White wins 64.8% of decisive games against the Scandinavian Mieses-Kotroc Variation — the highest white win rate among the top 15 openings. The Philidor Defense #3 (65.5%) and Scotch Game (55.8%) also strongly favor white. Players seeking color-advantage maximization should study these lines.

Finding 05
55.6%

Most Games End in Resignation — Not Checkmate

Resignation accounts for 55.6% of all outcomes, with checkmate at 31.5%. At higher ratings (2000+), resignation rises to 67.8% while checkmate drops to 14.4%. Coaches working with lower-rated players should note that 39.8% of sub-1200 games reach checkmate — pattern training in basic mating sequences directly impacts win rate.

Finding 06
7.8%

Classical Time Controls Produce Markedly More Draws

Classical games (≥30 min) draw at 7.8%, nearly double the blitz/rapid rate of ~4.2%. Classical games also have the lowest timeout rate (3.5%), confirming that game termination mode is substantially driven by time pressure. For tournament organizers and platform designers, time control is a strong lever for controlling draw frequency.

Finding 07
+36%

Stronger Players Play Longer, More Complex Games

2000+ rated players average 68.1 plies per game versus 50.0 plies for sub-1200s — a 36% difference. This implies deeper opening knowledge, more complex middlegames, and prolonged endgame technique at higher levels. Game length is itself a useful proxy for positional complexity in this dataset.

Finding 08
ECO C

Open Games (ECO C) Are the Most White-Favorable ECO Family

ECO C openings (1.e4 ...e5) show a 54.0% white decisive win rate — the highest of any ECO family. ECO A (flank/irregular) openings are the most balanced at 50.1% white wins, likely reflecting the unpredictable nature of non-standard openings at the amateur level.

For platform analysts: The 4.7% draw rate signals a healthy, action-oriented player base. The dominance of rapid (73.3%) and resignation as termination mode (55.6%) are consistent with a medium-time-control casual player population. The strong correlation between rating differential and outcome confirms Lichess's rating system is well-calibrated — a 200+ Elo gap produces deterministic-leaning outcomes and may benefit from different matchmaking handling for casual queues.
