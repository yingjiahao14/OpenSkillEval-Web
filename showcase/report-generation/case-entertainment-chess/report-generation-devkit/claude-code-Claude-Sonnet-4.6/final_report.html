White vs. Black: The First-Move Advantage
Quantifying white's edge — overall, by rating bracket, and by time control.

White's first-move privilege is one of chess's oldest observations. In this dataset of 20,058 games, white claims 10,001 victories versus black's 9,107, with 950 draws. Among decisive games only, white's win rate is 52.3% against black's 47.7% — a 4.7 percentage-point edge that is consistent across nearly every subgroup analyzed.

Time Control Patterns and Game Characteristics
How increment codes shape game length, victory conditions, and color advantage.

This dataset is heavily concentrated in rapid and classical time controls — the Lichess team communities from which it was drawn tend toward longer formats. 10+0 is by far the most common time control (7,721 games, 38.5% of dataset), followed by 15+0 (6.5%) and 15+15 (4.2%). Blitz games (estimated total time <8 min) are effectively absent (n=4), so comparisons focus on rapid vs. classical.

Key Findings and Strategic Takeaways
Synthesized insights for players, coaches, and platform analysts.

The following findings represent the most actionable, non-obvious conclusions from the full analysis. Each is directly supported by the data and carries implications for how players, coaches, or platform designers should think about chess on Lichess.

1
White's first-move advantage is real but modest — and shrinks with skill.

White wins 52.3% of decisive games overall, a 4.7 pp edge over black. But at the 2000+ bracket, this narrows to just 50.6%, barely above a coin flip among decisive games. Coaches working with stronger players should emphasize that color matters less at high levels — preparation quality and middlegame execution dominate.

2
A 100-point Elo gap is the threshold where rating becomes reliably predictive.

Below 50-point gaps, the higher-rated player wins only ~49% — effectively random. Above 100 points, win probability climbs to 62.4% and above; above 200 points, to 75.3%. Rating systems work, but only when the differential is meaningful. Platform matchmaking that targets <50-point pairings creates genuinely competitive games where outcome is highly uncertain.

3
Van't Kruijs (1.e3) is the worst-performing first move in this dataset — by far.

White wins only 34.2% of games with Van't Kruijs — well below any other top-15 opening. Black wins 61.4%. This opening is disproportionately chosen by lower-rated players who may not realize how passive it is. A simple intervention: Lichess's opening explorer could flag openings with dramatically unfavorable empirical win rates at specific skill levels.

4
Resignation dominates — most games don't reach checkmate or timeout.

55.6% of games end in resignation, 31.5% in checkmate, just 8.4% in timeout. Resign rates are consistent across time controls, suggesting that players at this skill level (~1400–1800 average rating) are generally competent at recognizing losing positions. The checkmate rate of 31.5% is notably higher than at the grandmaster level, where resignation comes much earlier.

5
Clock increments dramatically reduce timeout losses — increments are not cosmetic.

The 8+0 time control produces a 17.9% timeout rate — nearly 3× the dataset average. Adding even small increments (15+15 drops this to 3.6%) fundamentally changes the game's outcome distribution. For platforms designing default time controls, increments serve a measurable quality-of-game function, not just a preference feature.

6
Higher-rated players play longer games — a diagnostic signal for skill.

Average game length increases monotonically from 50.0 turns at <1200 to 68.1 turns at 2000+. Higher-rated players resist early collapses, sustain complex middlegames, and convert endgames more efficiently rather than flagging quickly. This turn-count correlation (skill → game length) is a platform-observable signal that could supplement Elo in experience and skill assessment.

7
Black's timeout advantage is the one place white's edge disappears.

In out-of-time games, white wins only 49.7% — statistical parity. This counterintuitive result may reflect that white, as the attacker, often plays more complex/committal positions that are harder to manage under time pressure, or that black's more reactive play is naturally faster to execute. For time-pressured players: simplify and trade pieces when low on clock, regardless of color.
