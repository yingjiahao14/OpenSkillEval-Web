White vs. Black: The First-Move Advantage

White wins 52.3% of decisive games (10,001 of 19,108), versus Black at 47.7% (9,107 decisive wins). That is a +4.7 percentage-point White edge among decisive games. Including draws, White wins 49.9% of all games and Black wins 45.4%.

Statistical read: treating decisive White wins and decisive Black wins as two proportions gives z=9.15 and p=5.892e-20. The edge is therefore not just visible in the sample; it is large enough to be statistically meaningful in this 20,058-game dataset.

Rating Dynamics and Their Impact on Outcomes

Rating differential is the most direct predictor in the dataset. The signed correlation between white_rating - black_rating and a White decisive win is r=0.367. More interpretable: among games where one player is higher rated, the higher-rated player wins 62.2% overall, and the correlation between absolute rating gap and higher-rated-player win probability is r=0.225.

The binned curve shows the practical effect size: when Black is higher rated by 400+ points, White's all-game win rate falls to 15.5%; when White is higher rated by 400+ points, White's all-game win rate rises to 84.1%. This gradient is more informative than a single correlation because it shows where rating gaps begin to dominate color assignment.

Key Findings and Strategic Takeaways

  • White advantage is real but not overwhelming. White takes 52.3% of decisive games, a +4.7 pp edge over Black. Analysts should control for color when modeling outcomes, but rating gap remains a stronger practical lever.
  • Rating gaps quickly dominate baseline color effects. The higher-rated player wins 62.2% overall, and White's win probability moves from 15.5% when Black is +400 to 84.1% when White is +400.
  • Resignation drives game endings. Resignations account for 55.6% of all games, exceeding checkmates (31.5%) and timeouts (8.4%). Coaching analysis should therefore examine conversion and evaluation pressure before mate patterns alone.
  • Time controls reshape failure modes. The top time-control table shows timeout rates vary materially by increment code; 8+0 has the highest timeout share among the top 12 at 17.9%.
  • Opening conclusions need sample discipline. The top named opening, Van't Kruijs Opening, represents only 1.8% of games. Use top-opening comparisons for directional scouting, not universal opening-strength claims.
  • Actionable modeling path. A robust outcome model should include signed rating differential, color, time bucket/increment, opening family, and interactions between rating tier and time control; the binned charts show why linear-only summaries hide chess-relevant thresholds.
Data limitation: all percentages and correlations above are computed directly from data.csv. The data contains game metadata and move strings but not engine evaluations, clock-by-move data, or player intent, so causal claims about opening quality, blunders, or time pressure are intentionally avoided.
