1. Dataset Overview & Game Distribution

The dataset contains 20,058 completed chess games collected from Lichess.org. Of these, 16,155 games (80.5%) were rated, meaning player Elo ratings were affected by the outcome, while 3,903 games (19.5%) were casual (unrated). Every game includes full metadata: player identities and ratings, time controls, opening classification (ECO code and name), complete move sequences, and detailed outcome information.

Game Outcomes by Result

Victory Status Distribution

Games predominantly end via resignation (55.6%), reflecting the convention in online chess where players concede lost positions rather than playing to checkmate. Checkmate accounts for 31.5% of games, while timeout (8.4%) and draws (4.5%) round out the distribution. The low draw rate (4.5%) is typical of amateur online play, where decisive results are far more common than in elite over-the-board competition.

Key observation: White won 10,001 games (49.9%), Black won 9,107 (45.4%), with 950 draws (4.7%). Excluding draws, white wins 52.34% of decisive games — a 4.7 percentage-point edge. This quantifies the well-known "first-move advantage" in chess and serves as the baseline for deeper analysis in the following sections.

2. White vs. Black: The First-Move Advantage

Across all 20,058 games, white holds a clear but modest advantage. Of the 19,108 decisive games, white won 52.34% to black's 47.66%. This +4.68 percentage-point edge translates to a white-to-black win ratio of 1.098: for every 100 games black wins, white wins approximately 110.

3. Rating Dynamics and Their Impact on Outcomes

Player ratings on Lichess span a wide range: white ratings from 784 to 2,700 (mean: 1,596.6) and black ratings from 789 to 2,723 (mean: 1,588.8). The distribution is centered around the intermediate range (1,400–1,600), where the bulk of online play occurs.

5. Time Control Patterns and Game Characteristics

The dataset spans 175 unique time control configurations. The dominant format is 10+0 (blitz, no increment) at 7,721 games (38.5% of the dataset), followed by 15+0 rapid (1,311) and 15+15 (850). Time controls shape not only game length but also how games end — with faster controls producing more timeouts and fewer checkmates.

6. Key Findings and Strategic Takeaways

1
White's advantage is real but modest. Across all 20,058 games, white wins 52.34% of decisive games — a 4.68 pp edge. This confirms the first-move advantage, but it is far from decisive: in nearly half of all games, black overcomes it. The effect is strongest at high ratings (66.1% white wins at 2000+) and weakest at low ratings (30.7% below 1100, where random blunders dominate).
2
Rating differential is the dominant predictor. A 100–200 point rating advantage pushes win probability to ~67% for the higher-rated player; a 400+ point gap pushes it to 88%. The relationship is non-linear: small rating gaps (±50 points) produce near-even outcomes, but the win probability curve steepens rapidly beyond a 100-point differential.
3
Not all openings are created equal for white. The Philidor Defense (C41, white win 59.7%), Owen Defense (B00, 62.6%), and McConnell Defense (C40, 61.4%) are white's best-performing popular openings. Conversely, the Van't Kruijs Opening (A00, 41.1%) and Bowdler Attack (B20, 41.1%) see black winning more often than white. Opening depth correlates with white success: 6+ ply openings see 52.9% white wins vs. 45.7% for 0–2 ply openings.
4
Stronger players resign; weaker players get checkmated. At the 2000+ level, 66.7% of games end in resignation and only 17.7% in checkmate. Below 1100, the pattern inverts: 47.7% resignation, 40.5% checkmate. This shift is a clear marker of chess maturity — recognizing lost positions and conserving mental energy for the next game.
5
Time controls shape the game's character. Bullet games are chaotic: 14% end by timeout, and an equal share end by checkmate and resignation. Classical games are methodical: 69.6% resignation, only 2.8% timeout. White's advantage grows with slower controls (51.1% bullet → 53.6% classical), and draws also increase from 3.7% to 8.9%.
6
Game length increases with skill. Beginners average 48.8 turns per game; experts (1800–2000) average 65.8 turns — 35% longer. Drawn games are the longest at 83.8 turns, and resigned games the shortest at 53.9 turns. This suggests that stronger players' longer games reflect both deeper middlegame knowledge and more resilient defense, not simply slower play.
7
The first-move advantage compounds with rating. A player 50 points stronger with white wins 60.6% of the time; a player 50 points stronger with black wins only 56.1% (white's win rate flipped). The 4.5 percentage-point bonus from the first move persists across rating differentials, effectively meaning that color assignment is worth approximately 35–50 Elo points in practical terms.
