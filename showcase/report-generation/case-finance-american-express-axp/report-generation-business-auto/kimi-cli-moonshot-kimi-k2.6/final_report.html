Executive Overview

American Express has delivered one of the most remarkable long-term wealth-creation stories in U.S. financial services. From an adjusted price of $1.22 in June 1972 to $305.99 in March 2026, the stock generated a cumulative capital appreciation of 24,895% — equivalent to a compounded annual growth rate (CAGR) of 10.81% over 53.8 years.

However, this return came with significant volatility. The stock experienced a maximum drawdown of −83.9% during the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, and annualized volatility sits at 34.69% — well above the S&P 500 long-term average. Investors who held through the crises were rewarded, but the journey required substantial risk tolerance.

Key Takeaway: AXP has been a high-return, high-volatility equity. Its 10.81% CAGR over five decades outperforms broad market averages, but drawdowns exceeding 40% have occurred in every major economic crisis. Dividends contributed $34.27 per share cumulatively, with yield declining from ~6.8% in the 1980s to ~1.1% in the 2020s as price appreciation outpaced payout growth.

Trading Volume Analysis

Volume data becomes reliable after the early 1970s (zero-volume records in 181 early rows). Structural shifts in liquidity are visible: the 1980s–1990s averaged ~2.6M shares/day, while the 2000s–2010s saw a step-up to ~5.5M shares/day, reflecting electronic trading and ETF inclusion.

Volume Spikes & Correlation with Events

The single highest volume day was April 24, 2009 (90.3M shares), coinciding with a +20.6% price surge during the GFC recovery. Other major spikes clustered in 2008–2009 (Lehman aftermath, TARP uncertainty) and October 1984 (63.6M shares, likely split-related rebalancing).
