Executive Overview

From its split-adjusted opening close of $1.2242 on 1972-06-01 to a closing price of $305.99 on 2026-03-11, American Express has delivered a cumulative price return of 24,894.9% across 53.8 years — equivalent to a compounded annual growth rate (CAGR) of 10.81%. The current price of $305.99 sits -20.3% below the all-time high of $384.04 recorded on 2025-12-11. Over the full dataset, AXP distributed $34.27 in cumulative per-share dividends, paid across 197 discrete events.

Decade Narratives

1970s (-6.6% CAGR). A challenging entry decade for AXP shareholders. The split-adjusted price fell from $1.22 to $0.73 amid the 1973-74 oil shock, stagflation, and a 74.7% peak-to-trough drawdown.

1980s (+20.9% CAGR). Post-Volcker disinflation and financial-sector deregulation drove a +569% total return. The decade also encompassed Black Monday (Oct 19, 1987, -26.23% in one session).

1990s (+21.5% CAGR). The strongest decade in the dataset by total return: +597%. AXP benefited from the long economic expansion and the run-up in financial equities. The 1994 spin-off of Lehman Brothers reshaped the corporate structure.

2000s (-0.03% CAGR). The "lost decade" — essentially flat (-0.3%) net of two major bear markets and the steepest drawdown in AXP's history (-83.9% during the GFC). Annualized volatility peaked at 43.8%.

2010s (+13.5% CAGR). Recovery and re-rating: +253% total return at the lowest decadal volatility (22.7%). Dividend distributions accelerated to $10.70 per share.

2020s-to-date (+16.9% CAGR). A volatile but rewarding decade so far: COVID-19 triggered a 49.6% drawdown in just 32 days (Feb–Mar 2020), but premium-card monetization and consumer-spending strength have since lifted AXP to fresh all-time highs above $384. Dividend distributions of $14.43 already exceed those of any prior full decade.

Investor Implication. AXP's long-run compounding (~10.8% CAGR) is an aggregate of regime-dependent outcomes that range from materially negative (1970s, 2000s) to materially positive (1980s, 1990s). Position sizing and rebalancing should account for the realistic possibility of multi-year drawdown periods in excess of 50%.
