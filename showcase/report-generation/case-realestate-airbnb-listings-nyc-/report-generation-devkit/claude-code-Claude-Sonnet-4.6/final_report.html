Among USD-credible markets, Amsterdam commands the highest median nightly price at $222, roughly 65–70% above London, Barcelona, and Rome, which cluster tightly between $131–$135. This Amsterdam premium is consistent with a heavily supply-constrained market (only 1,770 listings, 92.5-day average availability) where regulatory caps have reduced inventory and bid up prices.

The mean-to-median spread is large across all cities — London's mean ($210) is 56% above its median ($135); Amsterdam's mean ($266) exceeds its median by 20%. This gap is driven by a thin stratum of ultra-premium listings pushing averages upward, meaning median is the more actionable pricing benchmark for most market participants.

Room-type pricing follows expected patterns: Entire home/apt commands a premium in all markets. Notably, London's Hotel room median ($245.50) exceeds its Entire home median ($176), likely reflecting brand-name properties. In Rome, Hotel rooms are priced below Entire homes ($122.50 vs $140), suggesting budget-category hotel stock competing in the Airbnb channel.

The high mean (22.9) combined with a low median (1) suggests a highly polarised market: most hosts are genuine individuals, but a small number of operators hold very large portfolios.

It should not be read as weak underlying tourism demand.

Bangkok's average 252.5 availability days per year (median: 295) is consistent with a market dominated by professional operators who maintain open calendars year-round. This is the highest in the dataset and supports the professional-host concentration findings in Section 3.

Amsterdam's low average availability (92.5 days; median 18) is the strongest regulatory fingerprint in the dataset. The city caps short-term rental at 30 nights per year for primary residences; the median of 18 days suggests most hosts are operating at well below the legal ceiling — or that listings are frequently near capacity.

Barcelona's minimum-nights distribution is the most alarming indicator of regulatory arbitrage: 38.5% of listings require more than 30 nights — effectively operating as medium-term rentals to sidestep short-term rental licensing requirements. Barcelona has been restricting new STR permits since 2014; hosts with non-tourist licenses are converting to monthly-stay listings rather than exiting. Paris shows a similar but less extreme pattern (12.6%, mean minimum nights: 43.9 days).

Key Takeaways & Strategic Implications

  • Amsterdam is the premium-price market with the most authentic regulatory model.
    At a median of $222/night among USD markets, Amsterdam commands a 65% price premium over Barcelona, London, and Rome. This premium is structurally supported: only 1,770 listings are available (smallest market), average availability is just 93 days/year, and professional hosts are nearly absent (6.8%). For investors, this means supply entry is legally constrained; for policymakers, Amsterdam demonstrates that tight night-cap enforcement produces measurable price and supply effects.
  • Barcelona and Bangkok are the most commercially concentrated markets — and carry the highest regulatory risk.
    Professional hosts (≥5 listings) control 58.3% of Barcelona and 60.4% of Bangkok supply. Barcelona already faces the most aggressive regulatory environment in this dataset (38.5% of listings have minimum nights >30 — clear arbitrage behaviour), and the city announced a full STR permit moratorium in 2024. Operators and investors in these cities face material regulatory disruption risk in the near term.
  • Rome offers the strongest guest demand signal in the dataset.
    Rome leads on every demand metric: 1.77 avg reviews/month, 14.35 LTM reviews, and 66.3% of listings reviewed within the last 12 months. Combined with a median price of $131 (competitive relative to Amsterdam), Rome represents a high-utilisation, accessible-price-point market — attractive for new host entrants seeking volume over per-night yield.
  • Paris and Sydney data gaps prevent pricing conclusions — obtain complete data before any investment analysis.
    Paris (13,927 listings) and Sydney (3,013 listings) have zero price data in this extract. Given that Paris is the second-largest market by listing count and both cities are top-tier tourism destinations, the absence of pricing is a critical data gap. Any capital allocation or yield modelling for these markets requires a separate data pull from Inside Airbnb or a commercial provider.
  • London is the deepest and most liquid market but shows moderate commercialisation.
    With 16,674 listings, London is 3× the size of the next largest non-Paris market. Professional hosts control 32% of supply — significant but not extreme. The median price of $135 is competitive, and availability at 146 days/year suggests a mixed market of casual and commercial operators. London's depth makes it more resilient to single-operator exits, making it a lower-volatility entry point than Barcelona or Bangkok.
  • Minimum-nights distributions are the leading indicator of regulatory stress.
    Barcelona (38.5%) and Sydney (16.6%) show elevated shares of listings with minimum stays exceeding 30 nights — a behavioural adaptation to STR licensing restrictions rather than genuine demand for long stays. Track this metric over time as a leading indicator of regulatory tightening in any market; rising minimum-night floors precede formal licensing enforcement.
  • Bangkok currency ambiguity must be resolved before cross-city price benchmarking.
    Bangkok's median price of 1,400 and minimum of 200 are inconsistent with USD denomination at any reasonable market interpretation. If these are Thai Baht, the implied USD median is approximately $39 — making Bangkok the most affordable market by a wide margin. Confirm currency normalisation with the data provider before including Bangkok in any USD price comparison or yield model.
