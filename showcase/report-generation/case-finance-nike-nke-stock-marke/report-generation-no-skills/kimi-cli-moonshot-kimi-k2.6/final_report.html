Executive Summary

Over the 26 years from January 2000 to January 2026, Nike (NKE) delivered a 1282% cumulative return, translating to a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 10.6%. An investor who purchased at the first available close ($4.47) and held through the end of the dataset would have seen each dollar grow to roughly $13.8 — a compelling long-term compounding story.

However, that return was not smooth. The stock experienced a maximum drawdown of -68.5% from its all-time high of $166.25 (November 05, 2021) to its subsequent trough of $52.31 (April 08, 2025), a decline that remains open as of the latest data point. Annualized volatility of 31.2% places NKE in the upper tier of large-cap risk profiles, and the stock recorded 18 positive years out of 27 calendar years.

Key milestone: The all-time low of $2.47 was set on February 25, 2000, while the all-time high of $166.25 was reached on November 05, 2021. The current price of $61.81 sits 62.8% below that peak.

Phase I: Dot-Com Bust and Early-2000s Chop (2000–2002)

Nike opened the millennium at $4.47 and quickly fell into a severe bear market alongside the broader tech collapse. From a January 2000 peak of $4.91, the stock plunged 49.7% to an all-time low of $2.47 by February 25, 2000. It took until December 26, 2000 — roughly 10 months — to reclaim the pre-drawdown high. The 2001–2002 period added two more major drawdowns of 39.0% and 38.4%, keeping the stock range-bound for nearly three years.

Phase II: The Great Compounding (2003–2007)

A powerful bull run began in early 2003. From roughly $4.36 at the start of 2003, Nike climbed to $12.60 by the end of 2007 — a four-year CAGR near 30%. This era was marked by consistent outperformance, minimal deep drawdowns, and a steady upward slope in the 200-day moving average.

Phase III: Global Financial Crisis (2008–2009)

The GFC delivered Nike’s third-largest peak-to-trough decline. From a June 2008 high of $13.84, the stock collapsed 44.2% to $7.73 by March 9, 2009. Recovery was slow but steady; the pre-crisis high was not fully reclaimed until March 1, 2010 — nearly 21 months after the trough.

Phase IV: The Golden Decade (2010–2019)

The 2010s were Nike’s most rewarding period for long-term holders. The stock rose from $13.27 at the start of 2010 to $93.45 by the end of 2019 — a CAGR of 21.6%. Key drivers included digital transformation, international expansion, and margin expansion. Even during this bull market, Nike was not immune to corrections: a 25.3% drawdown from late 2015 to late 2016 tested patience, requiring nearly 14 months to recover.

Phase V: Pandemic Shock and Parabolic Peak (2020–2021)

COVID-19 triggered a 39.8% crash in just two months (January–March 2020), but Nike recovered with unprecedented speed — reclaiming its pre-pandemic high by June 3, 2020, only 72 days after the March 23 trough. The subsequent stimulus-driven rally pushed the stock to its all-time high of $166.25 on November 05, 2021.

Phase VI: The Protracted Bear Market (2022–2026)

Since the November 2021 peak, Nike has entered its longest and deepest drawdown on record. The stock fell 68.5% to $52.31 by April 8, 2025, and as of January 30, 2026, remains 62.8% below the all-time high with no recovery in sight. This phase has erased the majority of gains accrued since 2019 and represents a fundamental repricing of the company’s growth outlook.

Key Takeaways and Investment Implications

1. Nike Has Been a Strong Long-Term Compounder — With Caveats

A 10.6% CAGR over 26 years is exceptional for a large-cap consumer stock. However, the payoff was heavily back-loaded: the 2010s contributed the bulk of total returns, while the 2020s have been deeply negative. Investors who entered near the 2021 peak are sitting on significant unrealized losses.

2. Drawdowns Are Deep, But Recoveries Have Historically Materialized

Excluding the ongoing 2021–2026 episode, every major drawdown exceeding 30% eventually recovered to new highs. The GFC took 21 months from trough to recovery; the dot-com bust took 10–18 months. The current drawdown is already longer and deeper than any prior episode, suggesting either a generational buying opportunity or a structural break in the growth narrative.

3. Volatility Is Elevated in Crises but Compresses in Bull Markets

Rolling 30-day volatility has swung from the low teens during calm periods to over 90% during panics. Investors with a multi-year horizon must be prepared for short-term turbulence that can exceed 50% peak-to-trough.

4. Volume Spikes Are Warning Signals

The five largest volume days in the dataset all coincided with daily moves of ±6% to ±20%. For risk managers, a sudden surge in turnover — especially on down days — warrants attention as it often marks a regime shift or capitulation event.

5. The Risk-Return Profile Has Deteriorated Recently

The Sharpe-like ratio (CAGR / volatility) of 0.34 over the full period is respectable, but the 2020s have dragged it down meaningfully. With the stock still 62.8% below its 2021 peak and volatility elevated, the near-term risk-adjusted outlook is more uncertain than at any point since 2009.

Bottom line for long-term investors: Nike’s historical record supports the case for patient, buy-and-hold capital — provided entry discipline is maintained. The stock rewards those who buy during deep drawdowns and penalizes those who chase parabolic peaks. The current environment, characterized by the largest drawdown on record, may represent a high-conviction entry point for investors with a 5+ year horizon, though only if the underlying business stabilizes and resumes earnings growth.
