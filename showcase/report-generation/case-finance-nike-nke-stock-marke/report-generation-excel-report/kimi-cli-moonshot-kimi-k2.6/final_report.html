Executive Summary

Over the 26-year period from January 2000 to January 2026, Nike Inc. (NKE) delivered a 1282% cumulative return, translating to a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 10.6%. An investor who purchased NKE at the first trading day of 2000 and held through early 2026 would have turned every $1 invested into approximately $13.8 — a compelling long-term compounding story.

However, this return was not earned smoothly. The stock experienced a maximum drawdown of 68.5%, with the all-time high of $166.25 reached in November 2021 and the all-time low of $2.47 in February 2000. Annualized volatility of 31.2% places NKE in the higher-volatility tier of large-cap consumer discretionary stocks, reflecting both its growth profile and sensitivity to macroeconomic cycles.

Key milestone: The 2010s were Nike's strongest decade, delivering a ~604% cumulative return (21.6% CAGR) with notably lower volatility (23.7%) than the 2000s. The 2020s have been challenging, with the stock down ~34% from the 2021 peak as of early 2026.

Long-Term Price Trend Analysis

The price chart reveals three distinct structural eras in Nike's stock history: (1) a volatile but ultimately rewarding 2000s, (2) a powerful secular bull market through the 2010s, and (3) a post-2021 correction phase that remains unresolved.

Structural Bull and Bear Phases

Using the 200-day moving average as a regime filter, we identify several sustained bull and bear phases. The most significant sustained bull markets include the 2003–2004 recovery (+46% over 303 days), the 2006–2008 run (+49% over 331 days), the 2012–2014 advance (+41% over 323 days), and the 2014–2016 surge (+65% over 431 days). The 2020–2021 pandemic recovery added another +48% over 267 days.

The deepest and most prolonged bear phases were the dot-com bust (2000–2002), the Global Financial Crisis (2008–2009), and the ongoing drawdown from the November 2021 peak. Notably, the 2022–2025 period marks the first time since 2000 that NKE has experienced two consecutive years of negative returns, suggesting a potential structural shift in investor sentiment or competitive dynamics.

The drawdown chart illustrates the investor experience of holding through peaks. Drawdowns exceeding 30% occurred during the dot-com crash, the GFC, and the current 2021–2026 period. The current drawdown of approximately 62.8% from the ATH is now among the largest on record, rivaling the 2000–2002 experience in magnitude if not yet in duration.

Key Takeaways and Investment Implications

1. Nike has been a strong long-term compounder

A 10.6% CAGR over 26 years places Nike in the top tier of large-cap performers. The power of compounding is evident: a $10,000 investment in January 2000 would have grown to approximately $138 by January 2026.

2. Drawdown tolerance is essential

With a maximum drawdown of 68.5% and multiple 30%+ declines, Nike is not a "sleep-well" stock for investors who cannot tolerate significant paper losses. The current drawdown from the 2021 peak is a stark reminder that even multi-decade winners experience prolonged corrections.

3. Volatility is regime-dependent

The 2010s demonstrated that Nike's volatility can compress dramatically during periods of steady earnings growth and benign macro conditions. Conversely, crisis periods (GFC, COVID, current cycle) produce volatility spikes that can exceed 40% annualized. Investors should calibrate position sizing to the volatility regime, not just the price level.

4. Volume confirms inflections

The highest volume days in Nike's history have clustered around earnings-driven gaps and macro shocks. Volume spikes above 4× the 20-day average have historically marked short-term turning points — both bottoms (2003, 2020) and tops (2021, 2024).

5. The 2020s present an open question

With four consecutive years of negative returns (2022–2025) and the stock trading ~63% below its all-time high, Nike is at a critical juncture. Long-term investors must assess whether the current weakness represents a cyclical correction within a secular uptrend — as occurred in 2000–2002 and 2008–2009 — or a more fundamental repricing of the company's growth trajectory and competitive moat.

Bottom line: Nike's 26-year track record supports its status as a premier long-term holding, but only for investors with the patience and risk tolerance to endure multi-year drawdowns. The current valuation, set against this historical risk-return profile, will determine whether the next decade resembles the 2010s or the more turbulent 2000s.
