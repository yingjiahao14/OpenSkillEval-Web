1. Dataset Overview & Coverage Summary

This analysis is based on 50,000 price records collected from 7,373 unique forecourt stations across England, Scotland, and Wales. The data spans from 07 February 2026 to 16 April 2026, covering 6 distinct fuel types.

The dataset is sourced from the UK Government's Fuel Finder API via fuelcosts.co.uk, under the mandated open pricing scheme. Each record captures a single fuel price observation at a specific station and timestamp.

3. Price Spread & Market Competitiveness

Price spread is the most policy-relevant metric for assessing whether consumers face meaningful price disparities. A narrow IQR suggests most consumers pay similar prices, while a wide full range indicates outlier stations charging significantly more or less.

Interpreting the spread: The IQR captures the price band within which the middle 50% of observations fall — a more robust measure of typical consumer experience than the full min–max range, which is sensitive to outliers.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Finding 1 — Price Levels: Average UK fuel prices in the observed period range from 145.9p/litre (E10 (Standard Unleaded)) to 186.2p/litre (B7 Premium Diesel). Standard grades (E10, B7 Standard) remain the most affordable options for consumers.
  2. Finding 2 — Consumer Price Disparities: The full price range for E5 (Super Unleaded) reaches 1889.0p. Even within the IQR, B10 Diesel shows the widest typical spread at 48.8p. This suggests consumers could save meaningfully by comparing prices before refuelling, particularly for premium products.
  3. Finding 3 — Reporting Timeliness: The median update lag of 0.0 hours indicates generally prompt reporting. However, 10.5% of records have a lag exceeding 24 hours, and 3.6% exceed 7 days. Stale data undermines the consumer benefit of the open pricing scheme and suggests enforcement of update frequency requirements may be warranted.
  4. Finding 4 — Premium Fuel Opacity: Premium and specialty fuels (B7 Premium, B10, E5) have significantly fewer reporting stations than standard grades. This reduced coverage means the open data scheme provides a less complete picture of the premium fuel market, potentially disadvantaging consumers seeking price comparisons for these products.
  5. Finding 5 — Market Structure: The tight IQR for standard fuels (E10, B7 Standard) suggests a broadly competitive market at the core. The wider spreads for premium products may reflect both genuine product differentiation and lower competitive pressure due to fewer alternatives.
  6. Finding 6 — Data Quality: The dataset of 50,000 records across 7,373 stations provides a robust evidence base. Negative lag values in 2.02% of records indicate minor timestamp synchronisation issues between systems, though these do not materially affect the analysis.
Policy Recommendation: The evidence supports strengthening update frequency requirements, particularly targeting stations with chronic lag exceeding 24 hours. Additionally, expanding coverage monitoring for premium fuel types would improve market transparency. The open data scheme is functioning well for standard fuels but has gaps in both timeliness and coverage for the premium segment.
