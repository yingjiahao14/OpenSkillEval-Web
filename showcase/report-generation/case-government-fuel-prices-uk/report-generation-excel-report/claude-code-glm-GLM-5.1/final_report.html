6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Significant price disparities exist across forecourts. The full price range for E5 spans 1889.0 ppl, and even the IQR for B10 reaches 48.8 ppl. For a typical 55-litre tank fill, this IQR translates to a difference of several pounds — a material amount for household budgets. Policy implication: consumers benefit from price comparison tools, and transparency mandates should be maintained and publicised.
  2. Premium fuels command a substantial price premium. HVO averages 188.9 ppl versus E10 at 145.9 ppl — a gap of 43.0 ppl. Consumers who do not require premium-grade fuel can save meaningfully by choosing standard grades. Policy implication: forecourt signage should clearly distinguish standard from premium pricing to prevent inadvertent mispurchase.
  3. Data freshness is generally adequate but a tail of stale records exists. The overall median update lag is 0.0 hours, indicating most prices are captured promptly. However, 3,687 records (7.4%) show lags exceeding 48 hours, with B7_STANDARD having the most stale entries (1,157). Policy implication: regulators should investigate whether retailers with persistently stale feeds are non-compliant with reporting obligations and consider enforcement mechanisms.
  4. Fuel type availability is uneven. B7_STANDARD is reported by 88.1% of stations, but B10 by only 0.5%. Consumers seeking less common fuels have fewer comparison points, reducing the effectiveness of market transparency. Policy implication: reporting requirements should ensure broad coverage of all mandated fuel types across all participating forecourts.
  5. Outlier prices merit regulatory attention. Maximum prices for several fuel types far exceed the 75th percentile, suggesting either very high-cost locations or potential pricing anomalies. Policy implication: outlier analysis should be integrated into routine market monitoring to identify potential anti-competitive behaviour or data errors.
