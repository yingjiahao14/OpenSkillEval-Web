1. Dataset Overview & Coverage Summary

This analysis is based on 50,000 price records collected from 7,373 unique forecourts (stations) across England, Scotland, and Wales. The dataset spans 07 February 2026 – 16 April 2026, with the majority of observations recorded in March 2026. Retailer price updates (source_updated_at) range from 17 December 2025 – 16 April 2026, indicating some stations carry forward older price data.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

The following findings synthesise the main analytical insights and their relevance to fuel pricing policy, consumer protection, and market transparency.

  1. Significant price disparities exist for diesel consumers. B7 Standard Diesel shows an IQR of 33.00 pence and a total range of 233.90 pence — substantially wider than E10's IQR of 16.00 pence. For a typical 50-litre fill-up, a diesel motorist could face a difference of up to £116.95 between the cheapest and most expensive forecourts. Policy implication: Enhanced price comparison tools and consumer awareness campaigns should prioritise diesel users.
  2. E10 (standard unleaded) operates as the most competitive and transparent segment. With the lowest standard deviation (10.54 pence) and the tightest IQR, the standard petrol market appears to function efficiently, with limited pricing power for individual retailers. Policy implication: The current open data framework is working well for E10; regulators should study what drives this convergence and whether similar dynamics can be encouraged for other fuels.
  3. Premium fuels command a consistent but variable premium. B7 Premium Diesel (median 187.90 ppl) and E5 (median 160.90 ppl) both trade at meaningful premiums to their standard counterparts, yet exhibit wider price spreads. Policy implication: The "premium" label may lack standardisation; clearer definitions could help consumers understand what they are paying for and whether the premium is justified.
  4. Data freshness is generally excellent but with a concerning long tail. While the median update lag is only 0.049 hours (~3 minutes), 10.5% of records are stale by over 24 hours, and 18.7% of stations have at least one severely stale record (>7 days). Policy implication: Regulators should consider introducing maximum freshness thresholds (e.g., 24 hours) with automated alerts for non-compliant retailers.
  5. Data quality issues require systematic attention. 120 records contain implausible prices (≤1 pence or >500 pence), and 1,011 records show negative update lags where the "update" timestamp post-dates the "recorded" timestamp. Policy implication: The government feed should implement real-time validation rules (e.g., price bounds, timestamp consistency checks) to prevent erroneous data from reaching consumer-facing applications.
  6. Alternative fuels remain niche. HVO and B10 together account for only 0.3% of records and are available at fewer than 100 stations each. Policy implication: If the government wishes to accelerate the transition to lower-carbon fuels, targeted incentives for retailer stocking and price reporting of alternatives may be warranted.
Methodological note: All statistics in this report are derived directly from the provided data.csv dataset. Price analyses exclude 120 records with prices outside the 50–500 pence range, which are treated as data entry errors. Lag analyses exclude 1,011 records with negative lags.
