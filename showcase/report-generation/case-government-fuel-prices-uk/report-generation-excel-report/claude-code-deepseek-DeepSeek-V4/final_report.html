3. Price Spread & Market Competitiveness

Price spread — the gap between the cheapest and most expensive stations for the same fuel — is a direct proxy for market competitiveness. Wide spreads indicate that consumers who do not shop around may pay significantly more than necessary. The Interquartile Range (IQR) captures the central 50% of prices, representing what most consumers actually encounter at the pump.

Policy-relevant findings:

  • The interquartile range for B10 is 47.8p/L, the widest IQR across all fuel types. Even within the middle 50% of stations, consumers face a £26.30 difference on a full tank.
  • E10 shows the tightest IQR at 16.0p/L, indicating strong price competition for the UK's most common fuel grade. Consumers face limited variability between the 25th and 75th percentile stations.
  • The spread-to-IQR ratio is highest for B10 (2.9x), suggesting outlier stations at the extremes of the price distribution that active shoppers can avoid.
Policy implication: The observed price spreads — particularly for diesel grades and premium fuels — demonstrate that active consumer price comparison yields meaningful savings. A driver filling up weekly could save £50–£100+ annually by consistently choosing lower-priced stations. This validates the Fuel Finder open data scheme and suggests potential benefit from mandating prominent on-forecourt price displays visible before motorists commit to entering a station.

  • B7_STANDARD benefits from the freshest reporting, with a median lag of 3 min. In contrast, HVO has the highest median lag at 1.0 hours.

Data quality concern: A non-trivial proportion of records show update lags exceeding one week, and a small subset exceed 30 days. This undermines the utility of the price feed for real-time consumer decision-making and raises questions about retailer compliance with the spirit of the open data mandate. We recommend that policy makers consider introducing a maximum update interval of 24–48 hours with enforcement mechanisms for persistent non-compliance.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

The following synthesised findings are derived directly from the data analysis above, assessed against the five KPIs mandated for this report (mean price per litre, price spread, IQR, median update lag, and unique station count). They are intended to inform evidence-based policy decisions around fuel market regulation and consumer protection.

1 Fuel prices follow a clear hierarchy by grade, with material differences in what consumers pay. The average price ranges from £1.45/L (E10) to £1.87/L (HVO), a premium of 42.4p/L. Standard unleaded (E10) at £1.45/L is the most affordable mainstream fuel, while standard diesel (B7_STANDARD) costs 22.0p/L more on average.
Policy implication: The premium fuel market shows less price sensitivity. Transparency tools should help consumers assess whether premium fuel price differentials are justified by measurable performance benefits.
2 Within-fuel price spreads are economically significant for consumers. For B10, the gap between the cheapest and most expensive station is 137.7p/L — equivalent to £75.73 on a 55-litre tank fill. Even within the IQR, B10 consumers face a £26.30 per-tank difference between the 25th and 75th percentile stations.
Policy implication: These spreads validate the utility of the Fuel Finder scheme. Policy makers should promote wider public awareness of price comparison tools and consider requiring roadside-visible price displays at forecourts to enable pre-entry price comparison.
3 Retailer reporting compliance is inconsistent, with a long tail of stale data. While the median update lag is 3 min, the P90 reaches 1.2 days (28h) and 3.6% of records are over a week old. B10 is the worst-performing fuel type on staleness (9.4% >1 week).
Policy implication: The voluntary nature of the open data scheme appears insufficient to ensure timely data. A mandated maximum update interval of 24–48 hours, with compliance monitoring and enforcement, would materially improve data quality for consumer use.
4 Station coverage varies substantially by fuel type, affecting competition dynamics. B7_STANDARD achieves 88.1% station coverage (6,494 stations), while B10 is available at only 34 stations (0.5%). Consumers of niche fuel grades have far fewer competing stations to choose from.
Policy implication: Per-fuel-type competition metrics should replace aggregate measures. For fuels with limited station coverage, the CMA and other regulators should consider whether market concentration warrants closer scrutiny.
5 The data infrastructure is functional at scale but has structural gaps that limit analytical depth. With 50,000 records from 7,373 stations and zero structural nulls, the data pipeline operates reliably. However, the absence of station metadata (brand/ownership group, geographic location, motorway vs. urban classification) prevents analysis of geographic price disparities, brand-level pricing strategies, and rural vs. urban competition — all critical dimensions for antitrust and consumer protection assessment.
Policy implication: Enriching the open data mandate to include postcode-sector location and brand affiliation would dramatically increase the scheme's value for competition monitoring without exposing commercially sensitive individual-station data. A phased enhancement of the reporting standard should be prioritised.
