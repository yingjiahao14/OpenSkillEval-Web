Key Findings & Policy Implications

1

E5 Super Unleaded Commands a Significant Price Premium

E5 has the highest mean price at 162.7p/litre (median 160.9p), compared to E10 at 145.9p and B7_STANDARD at 167.4p. The E5 premium over standard E10 is 16.8p/litre on average.

Policy implication: Since E10 became the standard petrol grade in 2021, the E5 premium effectively functions as a surcharge on older vehicles incompatible with E10. Government should monitor whether this premium is cost-justified or represents opportunistic pricing.
2

Wide Price Spreads Indicate Significant Consumer Price Disparities

The max−min price spread for E5 is 1889.0p/litre, equivalent to a £1133.40 difference per 60-litre fill. Even the IQR of 18.0p means the 75th-percentile consumer pays meaningfully more than the 25th-percentile consumer.

Policy implication: Price comparison tools and mandated public reporting are justified: consumers who shop around can achieve meaningful savings. Regulator should assess whether the tail of high prices represents geographic monopoly positions or premium branding.
3

Most Retailers Update Prices Promptly; a Minority Are Chronically Stale

The overall median reporting lag is 0.0 hours, indicating that the majority of retailers comply with near-real-time reporting expectations. However, 10.5% of records have a lag exceeding 24 hours, and 3.6% exceed 7 days — representing a significant minority of persistently non-compliant stations.

Policy implication: The government should introduce formal reporting thresholds (e.g., mandatory updates within 24 hours of any price change) and publish league tables of update frequency by retailer/brand to incentivise compliance.
4

E5 Reporting Has Fewer Stations and Higher Coverage Gaps

E5 is reported by only 4,447 stations (60.3% of all stations), compared to higher coverage for E10 and B7_STANDARD. This reflects genuine market structure (not all forecourts sell E5) but may also mask under-reporting by stations that do carry E5.

Policy implication: Regulators should cross-reference open data station lists against known E5-stocking forecourts to identify potential non-reporters, since E5 consumers face reduced price comparison ability.
5

Mean–Median Alignment Suggests Limited Extreme Outlier Distortion

For each fuel type, the mean and median prices are within 2.5p of each other, indicating approximately symmetric distributions without severe skew from extreme outliers. The dataset appears internally consistent with realistic UK fuel price ranges.

Policy implication: The data appears suitable for policy analysis at face value. Standard deviations of 114.7p (B10), 28.7p (B7_PREMIUM), 34.7p (B7_STANDARD), 31.4p (E10), 45.1p (E5), 34.8p (HVO) suggest moderate natural price variation rather than data quality problems.
6

Geographic Pricing Analysis Requires Supplementary Station Metadata

Station identifiers are anonymised hashes; without a separate station metadata table (brand, postcode, coordinates), geographic pricing disparities — potentially the most policy-relevant dimension — cannot be assessed. This represents a significant limitation of the current open data scheme.

Policy implication: The government should mandate that open data submissions include postcode-level location data (already partially available via the Fuel Finder API) to enable regional price transparency analysis. This is a critical gap for consumer protection enforcement.
