Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. 1.

    Median prices show a clear fuel hierarchy: HVO is highest at 189.9 PPL, while E10 is lowest at 144.9 PPL. Policy implication: grade-specific transparency matters because headline petrol/diesel averages mask materially different consumer price bands.

  2. 2.

    The typical consumer-facing spread is substantial for several fuels: B10 has the widest IQR at 48.8 PPL. Policy implication: even after avoiding extreme raw outliers, consumers can face meaningful differences depending on forecourt choice.

  3. 3.

    Raw max–min ranges are very large, led by E5 at 1889.0 PPL, reflecting extreme submitted values in the open feed. Policy implication: validation rules or anomaly flags would improve the usefulness of mandated price reporting.

  4. 4.

    Reporting freshness is strong at the median — 0.048 hours overall, about 2.9 minutes — but 5,266 records (10.5%) are more than 24 hours old and 1,802 (3.6%) exceed seven days. Policy implication: compliance monitoring should use both median freshness and stale-tail thresholds.

  5. 5.

    Coverage is broad for standard fuels: B7_STANDARD appears at 6,494 stations, while B10 appears at 34. Policy implication: niche fuels require cautious interpretation because low station coverage can distort comparisons.

  6. 6.

    The dataset contains anonymised station identifiers only, with no brand, address, or geospatial metadata. Policy implication: area-level disparity, brand behaviour, and local competition cannot be assessed from this file alone without a station metadata linkage.
