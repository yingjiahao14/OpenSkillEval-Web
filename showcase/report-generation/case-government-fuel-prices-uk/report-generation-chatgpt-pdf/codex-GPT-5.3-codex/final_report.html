3) Price Spread & Market Competitiveness

For policy relevance, both total spread (max−min) and IQR are used. Spread captures extreme disparity; IQR captures the typical band faced by most consumers.

  • Wider spreads indicate larger potential savings from cross-station shopping but may also indicate inconsistent pricing conditions.
  • IQR provides a robust competitiveness signal less affected by rare extremes.

6) Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. HVO is the highest-priced fuel type on average at 188.87 pence/litre, while E10 is lowest at 145.90 pence/litre — a mean gap of 42.96 pence.
  2. Across fuel types, the max–min spread and IQR show non-trivial within-type price dispersion, indicating that consumers can materially reduce costs by choosing lower-priced forecourts.
  3. Median reporting lag is 0.05 hours (IQR: 0.03–0.11), but 5,266 records exceed 24 hours and 3,687 exceed 48 hours, suggesting uneven freshness.
  4. Dataset coverage includes 7,373 unique stations and 6 fuel types, supporting broad market-level inference for the covered period.
  5. Station-level fuel availability is uneven: not all forecourts report all fuel types, which affects like-for-like consumer comparison and should be considered in transparency monitoring.
  • Consumer protection: Large within-fuel spreads support the value of transparent, timely price publishing for effective comparison.
  • Market monitoring: IQR and lag tail metrics can serve as operational indicators for ongoing competitiveness and reporting-compliance surveillance.
  • Data governance: Persistent stale-lag pockets may warrant targeted retailer outreach or compliance review.
