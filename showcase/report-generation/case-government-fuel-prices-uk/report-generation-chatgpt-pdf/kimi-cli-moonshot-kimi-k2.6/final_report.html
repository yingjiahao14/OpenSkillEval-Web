UK Retail Fuel Price Trend Analysis

Examining price distributions, market competitiveness, and reporting freshness across UK forecourts — February to April 2026

Prepared for government policy analysts and energy economists  |  Dataset: 49,878 records from 7,367 stations

Executive Summary

This report analyses retail fuel pricing patterns across UK forecourts using government-mandated open data. The dataset comprises 49,878 price records from 7,367 unique stations collected between 7 February and 16 April 2026. After removing 122 obvious data errors (prices below 50p or above £4.00 per litre), the analysis reveals significant price disparities across fuel types and meaningful variation in retailer reporting behaviour.

1. Dataset Overview & Coverage Summary

The dataset covers six fuel types recorded across UK forecourts. The majority of records relate to the four mainstream fuels: E10 (standard unleaded petrol), B7_STANDARD (standard diesel), E5 (super unleaded petrol), and B7_PREMIUM (premium diesel). Two niche fuels appear in smaller volumes: B10 (54 records) and HVO (90 records).

Data completeness is strong: zero missing values across all six columns in the raw dataset. However, 122 records (0.24%) contained obvious pricing errors and were excluded from analysis. A further 1,011 records (2.0%) show negative update lags, indicating minor clock synchronisation issues between retailer systems and the data collection platform.

2. Price Distribution by Fuel Type

The centrepiece of this analysis is the distribution of retail prices across fuel types. Using the latest recorded price per station-fuel combination (20,406 distinct price points across the four main fuels), we observe a clear hierarchy: premium fuels command a significant price premium, while standard grades cluster in a tighter band.

Figure 1. Box plot showing price distribution by fuel type. Diamonds indicate mean values. Outliers beyond whiskers are shown as individual points.

E10 (standard unleaded) shows the tightest distribution, with a mean of 150.4ppl and standard deviation of just 9.7ppl. B7_STANDARD (diesel) averages 178.4ppl with wider variation (σ = 15.6ppl). E5 (super unleaded) sits between the two standard grades at 165.7ppl, while B7_PREMIUM is the most expensive mainstream fuel at 194.6ppl on average.

3. Price Spread & Market Competitiveness

Price spread is the most policy-relevant dimension for assessing market competitiveness. A narrow interquartile range (IQR) suggests most consumers face similar prices; a wide IQR or large max-min range indicates meaningful disparities that may warrant regulatory attention.

Policy note: B7_STANDARD diesel shows the largest total range (233.9ppl), driven by a small number of extreme low-price and high-price outliers. While the IQR is more modest at 20.0ppl, consumers at the upper end of the distribution may be paying substantially more than the market median. The 90ppl minimum is particularly anomalous and may reflect data quality issues even after outlier removal.

E10 demonstrates the most competitive market structure: half of all stations price within a 12.2ppl band, and the IQR represents just 8.0% of the median price. This suggests strong price convergence among retailers for the UK's default petrol grade. By contrast, B7_PREMIUM's IQR of 23.0ppl (11.6% of median) indicates less price convergence, likely reflecting lower consumer price sensitivity for premium diesel and reduced competitive pressure.

Notable finding: E5 (super unleaded) exhibits the weakest reporting freshness, with only 75.0% of records updated within one hour and 4.8% stale beyond 7 days. 6. Price Trend Over Time

Weekly mean prices show a gradual upward trajectory across all fuel types through February and March 2026, with some divergence in early April. Diesel grades (both standard and premium) consistently trade at a premium to their petrol counterparts.

Figure 7. Weekly mean price trend by fuel type from February to April 2026. All series show an upward drift consistent with rising crude oil and wholesale refined product costs during this period.

The gap between B7_STANDARD and E10 widened slightly over the period, from approximately 25ppl in mid-February to nearly 30ppl by early April. This widening diesel premium has been a recurring feature of UK fuel markets and may reflect both supply-side factors (refining margins) and demand-side dynamics (commercial diesel demand recovery).

7. Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Diesel commands a persistent and widening premium over petrol. B7_STANDARD averages 28.0ppl more than E10, and the gap has grown over the observation period. Policy implication: Monitor diesel pricing closely for signs of market power or supply constraints, particularly given diesel's importance to commercial transport and logistics.
  2. E10 shows the most competitive market structure. With an IQR of just 12.2ppl (8.0% of median) and the smallest coefficient of variation, E10 prices are tightly clustered. This suggests strong consumer price sensitivity and effective competition for the UK's default petrol grade. Policy implication: The E10 market can be treated as a benchmark for competitive pricing behaviour.
  3. Premium fuels exhibit the widest price spreads and weakest convergence. B7_PREMIUM has the largest IQR (23.0ppl) and the highest mean price (194.6ppl). Consumers of premium grades face greater price dispersion and less transparent competition. Policy implication: Consider requiring clearer price differentiation disclosures at the pump, as premium fuel buyers may be less price-elastic and more vulnerable to excessive markups.
  4. Reporting freshness is generally excellent but with a concerning tail. While 80.6% of records are captured within one hour, 3.5% are stale beyond 7 days. On the latest record per station, 385 price points (1.9%) still exceed this threshold. Policy implication: Introduce minimum freshness standards for mandated price reporting; stations with consistently stale data should be flagged for compliance review.
  5. E5 reporting lags are systematically worse than other fuels. Only 75.0% of E5 records are captured within one hour, versus ~82% for other mainstream fuels. Policy implication: Investigate whether E5 data feeds use different technical infrastructure or lower reporting priority, and target remediation efforts accordingly.
  6. Station coverage gaps limit consumer choice for premium grades. B7_PREMIUM is available at only 44.0% of forecourts and E5 at 60.4%. Consumers in rural areas may have limited access to premium fuels. Policy implication: Coverage mapping should inform infrastructure planning, particularly for alternative fuels where availability is even more constrained (HVO: 0.7%, B10: 0.4%).
  7. Data quality issues persist despite overall completeness. 122 obvious outliers (prices <50p or >£4) and 1,011 negative lags indicate ongoing data integrity challenges. Policy implication: Strengthen validation rules at the point of data submission and introduce automated outlier flagging to improve the reliability of open price data.

Methodology & Data Quality Notes

All statistics are derived directly from data.csv (50,000 rows). 122 records with prices below 50ppl or above 400ppl were excluded as obvious data entry errors. For price distribution analysis, the latest recorded price per station-fuel combination was used to avoid overweighting stations with frequent updates. Update lag was calculated as the difference between recorded_at and source_updated_at; negative values (indicating clock skew) were floored at zero for summary statistics. Timestamps are in UTC.

Station identifiers (node_id) are anonymised hashes; no geographic or brand-level analysis is possible with this dataset alone. The analysis period spans 7 February to 16 April 2026, with the majority of source updates occurring within this window (a small number of earlier source dates reflect stations with very stale reporting).

Source: UK Government Fuel Finder scheme, compiled by fuelcosts.co.uk (CC BY 4.0). Analysis date: 29 April 2026. Report prepared for: Government policy analysts and energy economists.
