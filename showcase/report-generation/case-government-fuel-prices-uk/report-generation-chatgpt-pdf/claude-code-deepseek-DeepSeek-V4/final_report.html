6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

The following findings synthesise the analytical results and their relevance to fuel pricing policy, consumer protection, and market transparency.

  1. Clear fuel-type price hierarchy, but significant within-type dispersion. E10 is the cheapest fuel (mean 145.2 ppl), while HVO is the most expensive (mean 188.9 ppl) — a premium of 43.7 ppl. However, prices within each fuel type vary substantially: standard deviation ranges from 10.5 to 34.8 ppl, meaning two consumers buying the same fuel on the same day can pay meaningfully different prices. This underscores the value of price transparency tools for consumer decision-making.
  2. Price spreads warrant regulatory attention. The max−min range reaches 410.0 ppl for B7_STANDARD — meaning the most expensive station charges approximately £4.10 more per litre than the cheapest. Even the more conservative IQR (representing the middle 50% of stations) reaches 47.8 ppl for B10. For a typical 55-litre fill-up, this translates to a potential saving of £26.29 simply by choosing a median-priced station over a 75th-percentile one. Policy interventions should focus on ensuring consumers have ready access to real-time price comparison tools.
  3. Retailer reporting timeliness is inconsistent. While the median update lag of 0.0 hours indicates that most stations report reasonably promptly, 1.9% of all records show a lag exceeding 14 days — meaning the published price could be over two weeks out of date. This undermines the government's open data scheme. We recommend that the Fuel Finder mandate include a maximum reporting interval (e.g., retailers must update prices at least every 48 hours) and that non-compliant stations be flagged publicly.
  4. Fuel type coverage is uneven across stations. Only 0.0% of stations report all 6 fuel types, while 10.8% report only a single fuel type. This limits the comparability of price data across fuel grades and suggests that the mandatory reporting scheme does not yet require all stations to declare prices for all fuel types they sell. Extending the mandate to cover all dispensed fuel types at each forecourt would improve data completeness.
  5. Data quality issues exist but are manageable. 1,011 records (2.02%) exhibit negative update lags (source_updated_at after recorded_at), suggesting timestamp inconsistencies in the collection pipeline. While the proportion is small, regulators should investigate the root cause — clock synchronisation, batch processing artefacts, or timestamp field handling — to ensure the integrity of the published data feed.
Overall assessment: The UK's mandatory fuel price reporting scheme provides a valuable transparency tool, but its effectiveness is limited by inconsistent retailer reporting behaviour and uneven station coverage. Strengthening reporting obligations — particularly around update frequency and fuel-type completeness — would significantly enhance the scheme's value for consumers, regulators, and market analysts.
