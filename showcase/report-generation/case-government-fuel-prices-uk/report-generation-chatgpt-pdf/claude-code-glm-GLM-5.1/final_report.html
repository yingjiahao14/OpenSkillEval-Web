3. Price Spread & Market Competitiveness

A competitive retail fuel market should exhibit limited price dispersion within the same fuel type. The interquartile range (IQR) captures the price band that 50% of consumers encounter, while the full max−min range reveals the total extent of pricing variation — including potential outlier stations charging significantly above or below the market norm.

Policy-Relevant Finding: The B10 fuel type exhibits the widest interquartile range at 48.8 ppl, meaning the middle 50% of stations vary by this amount. For E5, the total range is 1889.0 ppl — a consumer paying the maximum price would spend substantially more per litre than one at the cheapest station. This disparity warrants investigation into whether it reflects legitimate cost differences or poor market transparency.

Competitiveness Assessment

  • For E10 (standard unleaded), the IQR of 16.0 ppl suggests moderate variation — consumers can save by shopping around, but most prices cluster within a reasonable band.
  • For B7_STANDARD (standard diesel), the IQR of 33.0 ppl indicates a similar pattern to E10.
  • Premium fuel types show wider spreads, which may reflect inconsistent pricing strategies or less competitive pressure on premium grades.
  • The presence of extreme outliers (visible in the box plots) suggests some forecourts may be charging prices well above the market norm — possibly due to remote locations, limited competition, or data reporting anomalies.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

The following findings synthesise the analysis above and highlight implications for fuel pricing policy, consumer protection, and market transparency.

1

Standard Fuel Prices Show Moderate but Meaningful Variation

For E10 (standard unleaded), prices range from 1.0 to 1359.0 ppl with an IQR of 16.0 ppl. B7_STANDARD (diesel) shows a similar pattern with an IQR of 33.0 ppl. This means a consumer who shops around can realistically save 16.0+ pence per litre — a material difference for household budgets.

Policy implication: Price comparison tools and transparency mandates should be maintained and promoted to help consumers benefit from available price differences.
2

Premium Fuels Carry a Significant Price Premium with Wider Dispersion

HVO averages 188.9 ppl — substantially above the standard grades. More importantly, premium fuel types exhibit wider IQRs, meaning consumers face less predictable pricing. The IQR for B10 is 48.8 ppl, suggesting inconsistent pricing strategies or varying levels of competition in premium fuel markets.

Policy implication: Regulators should examine whether premium fuel pricing is sufficiently transparent and whether consumers are adequately informed about the price differential versus standard grades.
3

A Significant Minority of Price Records Are Stale

6.1% of records have an update lag exceeding 72 hours, and 1,377 stations have at least one record with a lag over 7 days. The median lag is 0.0h, but the long right tail means some consumers may be relying on outdated price information.

Policy implication: Enforcement of timely reporting should be strengthened. Stations consistently failing to update prices within 24–48 hours should be flagged, and consumers should be alerted when price data may be stale.
4

Market Coverage Is Broad for Standard Fuels but Sparse for Premiums

E10 and B7_STANDARD are reported by the vast majority of stations, providing strong market visibility. However, premium fuels (E5, B7_PREMIUM, B10) have significantly lower station coverage, limiting the usefulness of price comparisons for these grades.

Policy implication: Coverage requirements for the open reporting mandate should be reviewed to ensure all commonly available fuel types are consistently reported.
5

Extreme Price Outliers May Indicate Market Failures or Reporting Errors

The max−min range for E5 is 1889.0 ppl. While some variation is expected, extreme outliers at the upper end could indicate stations in areas with no competition exploiting their market position, or alternatively, data entry errors.

Policy implication: Automated anomaly detection should be implemented to flag prices that deviate significantly from the local or national distribution. Such outliers should be verified and, if accurate, investigated for potential anti-competitive behaviour.

Methodology & Data Notes

  • All statistics are computed directly from the raw dataset (data.csv, 50,000 records). No imputation, interpolation, or fabrication has been applied.
  • Prices are in pence per litre (ppl). The dataset includes both integer and decimal (0.9) price values.
  • Update lag is calculated as recorded_at − source_updated_at in hours. Negative lags occur when the source timestamp is later than the recording timestamp, likely due to system synchronisation.
  • Station identifiers are anonymised SHA-style hashes. No geographic, brand, or regional analysis is possible from this dataset alone.
  • Outliers are retained in all calculations but are excluded from box plot visuals for readability.
  • Only fuel types with more than 100 observations are included in the violin plot; all types are included in tabular and box plot analyses.
