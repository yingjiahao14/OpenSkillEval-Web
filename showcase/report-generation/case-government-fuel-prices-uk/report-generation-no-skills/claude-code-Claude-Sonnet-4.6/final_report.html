However, the mean lag of 18.8 hours — far higher than the median — signals a long-tailed distribution driven by a subset of stale records.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

Evidence-based insights for fuel pricing policy, consumer protection, and market transparency

  1. E10 petrol remains the most price-competitive fuel grade at a median of 144.9p/L

    With the tightest IQR of 16.0p/L across all six fuel types, E10 shows the greatest price convergence across UK forecourts. This likely reflects high competitive intensity at the standard grade level and possibly the effect of well-publicised comparison tools. The 22p/L median premium of B7_STANDARD diesel (167.9p/L) over E10 deserves ongoing monitoring given diesel's disproportionate importance for freight, commercial transport, and rural households.

  2. 10.5% of records show update lags exceeding 24 hours, indicating a material compliance gap

    The UK Government Fuel Finder scheme requires retailers to update prices promptly. While 78.4% of all records were propagated within 1 hour of the retailer's last update, 5,266 records (10.5%) show a lag of more than 24 hours, and 1,802 records (3.6%) show lags exceeding 7 days. This suggests a non-trivial minority of forecourt operators are not meeting the expected update cadence, potentially misleading price comparison services and consumers. Regulators should consider automated alerting for stations with lags above a defined threshold.

  3. Premium fuels (B7_PREMIUM, HVO) command a ~43p/L premium over E10, with wide inter-station variation

    B7_PREMIUM diesel has a median price of 187.9p/L and an IQR of 30.0p/L, indicating that the premium-grade market is less price-uniform than the standard petrol market. HVO (Hydrotreated Vegetable Oil) sits at 189.9p/L median with only 54 stations reporting — its negligible network coverage means HVO is currently inaccessible to most UK motorists. Policy supporting HVO expansion could benefit fleet decarbonisation programmes.

  4. B10 and HVO show disproportionately stale reporting rates, undermining price transparency for these fuels

    B10 stations have a 28.1% rate of records with lag >24h (vs. the overall average of 10.5%), and HVO shows 19.8%. While small sample sizes (64 and 91 records respectively) limit firm conclusions, these elevated stale rates suggest niche fuel retailers may face less competitive pressure to update prices frequently. Given policy interest in promoting alternative fuels, ensuring timely price reporting should be a priority for these categories.

  5. 2,745 stations (37.2%) report only 1 or 2 fuel types — a potential data completeness concern

    Stations reporting fewer than three fuel types may not be fully compliant with open data obligations, or may reflect forecourts that genuinely stock a limited range. Without station metadata (brand, address, or operational status), it is not possible to distinguish between incomplete data submission and legitimate single-fuel operators (e.g., specialist diesel depots). A match with station registry data would allow targeted enforcement.

  6. A small number of implausible price records (0.24%) suggest data quality issues in the retailer reporting pipeline

    120 records contain prices below 100p/L or above 300p/L — including values as low as 0p and as high as 1,879p. These almost certainly represent data entry errors, test records, or unit conversion issues (e.g., prices expressed in £ rather than pence). The government data aggregator (fuelcosts.co.uk) and upstream API operators should implement automated validation rules to flag or reject price values falling outside plausible bounds before publication.

  7. The typical price band most consumers encounter (IQR) spans 16–33p/L depending on fuel type, with real financial consequences

    The difference between a 25th-percentile and a 75th-percentile station is 16p/L for E10 and 33p/L for B7_STANDARD. On a 50-litre fill, this equates to £8–£16.50 per visit — a significant sum for cost-conscious households, particularly in the context of sustained cost-of-living pressure. These findings strengthen the case for consumer-facing real-time price transparency tools and continued open data mandates.

  8. Negative lag records (2.0%) warrant investigation but are unlikely to indicate systemic data corruption

    The 1,011 records where source_updated_at is later than recorded_at are all within 1 hour of zero lag, suggesting minor clock skew between the retailer's system and the collection agent rather than fundamental timestamp errors. Nonetheless, standardising clock synchronisation requirements in the UK Fuel Finder API specification would improve data integrity for downstream analytics.
