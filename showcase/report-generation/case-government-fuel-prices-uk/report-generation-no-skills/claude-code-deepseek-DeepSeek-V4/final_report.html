Data Quality

Each record was assessed for price validity using an IQR-based outlier detection method applied per fuel type (values outside [Q1 − 1.5×IQR, Q3 + 1.5×IQR] were flagged).

  • 311 records flagged as outliers (0.62% of total) — extreme values such as £0.00, £0.01, or implausibly high prices exceeding £10.00.
  • 49,689 records retained for the main price distribution analysis.
  • The timestamp fields (recorded_at, source_updated_at) contained no missing or unparseable values.
  • Price values are stored as floating-point pence per litre (e.g., 139.9 = £1.399/litre), reflecting the fractional pricing used at UK forecourts.
Note on geographic coverage: Station metadata (brand, address, coordinates) is not included in this dataset. All station-level analysis uses anonymised node_id hashes. Geographic or brand-level comparisons are therefore not possible with this file alone.

Price dispersion — the range between the lowest and highest prices for the same fuel type — is a key indicator of market competitiveness. Wide spreads suggest consumers face materially different prices depending on where they fill up, which may indicate weak competitive pressure or information asymmetries.

4. Retailer Reporting Freshness Analysis

The update lag — the time difference between when a retailer last updated their price (source_updated_at) and when it was captured by the data collection system (recorded_at) — is a measure of reporting freshness. Shorter lags indicate more timely reporting and better data quality for consumers and policymakers relying on the open data feed.

Overall Update Freshness

  • Median update lag across all records: 0.0 hours (approximately 0.0 days).
  • Mean update lag: 18.8 hours (0.8 days). The positive skew (mean > median) reflects a long tail of stations with very stale data.
  • P95 lag: 106.4 hours (4.4 days) — 5% of records have update lags exceeding this threshold.
  • Maximum lag: 1263.6 hours (52.6 days) — the single stalest record in the dataset.

Policy Considerations

Update lag has direct implications for data quality under the UK's open data scheme:

  • Records with lags exceeding 7 days may not reflect current forecourt prices, undermining the utility of the feed for consumer price comparison tools.
  • Variation in lag across fuel types may indicate that certain fuel grades receive less frequent attention from retailers when updating their feeds.
  • A mandated maximum update interval (e.g., requiring retailers to refresh prices at least weekly) could improve data freshness, particularly for the tail of stale records.
Note: Some records show negative lag values (source_updated_at after recorded_at), which may occur when collection happens before the source timestamp is finalised, or due to minor clock skew between systems. The proportion of negative lags is small and they are included in the analysis as-is.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

The following findings synthesise the analysis and are presented for an executive policy audience. Each finding is tied to a specific policy implication relevant to market transparency and consumer protection.

1. The diesel premium persists. B7_STANDARD diesel (mean £1.67/litre) costs approximately £0.22 more per litre than E10 petrol (mean £1.45/litre). This premium, while well-established, continues to impose a disproportionate burden on diesel-dependent consumers and businesses. Policy implication: Consider whether the diesel-petrol price gap warrants review under consumer fairness frameworks.
2. Meaningful price dispersion exists within all fuel types. The interquartile range (IQR) — representing the middle 50% of prices consumers encounter — ranges from £0.16 to £0.48 across fuel types. For standard petrol (E10), consumers in the 75th percentile pay approximately £0.16 more per litre than those in the 25th percentile. Policy implication: This spread justifies continued investment in consumer-facing price comparison tools to help drivers identify cheaper fuel locally.
3. Update freshness varies considerably. The median update lag of 0.0 hours suggests that, on average, reported prices are reasonably current. However, the long tail of stale records (P95 = 106.4 hours, max = 1263.6 hours) means a meaningful minority of reported prices may not reflect actual forecourt prices at the time consumers consult the data. Policy implication: Consider introducing a maximum update interval (e.g., 48–72 hours) as a condition of scheme participation to improve data reliability.
4. Station coverage is comprehensive for mainstream fuels but incomplete for niche fuels. Over 7,373 stations report prices, providing broad geographic coverage. However, B10 and HVO are each reported by fewer than 100 stations, limiting the dataset's utility for assessing competition in emerging fuel markets. Policy implication: As the fuel mix diversifies (HVO, B10, electric charging), the reporting mandate should be reviewed to ensure emerging fuel types achieve comparable coverage.
5. Data quality is generally high but outlier records persist. 0.62% of records (311) were flagged as price outliers — values that are implausibly low (e.g., £0.00/litre) or high (e.g., >£10.00/litre). While a small proportion, these represent data entry or transmission errors that may distort naive aggregations. Policy implication: The data collection pipeline should implement automated validation rules (plausible price ranges per fuel type) to reject or flag erroneous submissions at the point of ingestion.
