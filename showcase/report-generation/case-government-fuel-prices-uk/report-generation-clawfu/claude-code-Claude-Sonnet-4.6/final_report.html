Section 01

Dataset Overview & Coverage Summary

This dataset comprises 50,000 price observations drawn from the UK Government's Fuel Finder scheme, spanning 7 February to 16 April 2026 — a period of approximately 10 weeks. Source price update timestamps extend back to 17 December 2025, reflecting older price reports that were still active during the collection window. The data represents 7,373 unique forecourts across England, Scotland, and Wales. No null values were detected across any column.

Section 02

Price Distribution by Fuel Type

Price distributions reveal substantial variation both between and within fuel types. Standard unleaded petrol (E10) commands the lowest average price at 145.9p/litre, while premium diesel (B7_PREMIUM) and HVO are the most expensive at approximately 186–189p/litre. Standard diesel (B7_STANDARD) sits midway at 167.4p/litre. The spread of prices within each fuel type — examined in detail in Section 3 — reveals meaningfully different competitive dynamics.

Section 03

Price Spread & Market Competitiveness

The interquartile range (IQR) — the price band in which the central 50% of stations are clustered — is the most policy-relevant measure of consumer price disparity. A narrow IQR indicates competitive pricing pressure; a wide IQR suggests consumers in higher-priced areas face material disadvantage. Separately, the max−min spread captures the full market range but is sensitive to data anomalies.

Policy Alert — Diesel Disparity: Standard diesel (B7_STANDARD) shows an IQR of 33p/litre — more than double the E10 IQR (16p). This means a typical diesel driver at a high-price forecourt pays approximately £4.95 more per 15-litre fill than at a low-price forecourt. Given that diesel is disproportionately used by commercial and van operators — businesses with lower price sensitivity and less flexibility to shop around — this disparity has direct implications for freight costs and small business operating expenses.

This is consistent with E10 being the default pump grade, driving high volume and consumer price awareness. The Petrol Retailers Association's pump comparison tools appear to be most effective for this fuel category.
Section 04

Retailer Reporting Freshness Analysis

The lag between source_updated_at (when a retailer last reported a price to the government feed) and recorded_at (when the data collection system captured the record) measures data freshness and indirectly reflects retailer compliance with the open-data mandate. A short lag indicates real-time or near-real-time updating; a long lag suggests a price may be stale at the time of publication.

Negative Lag Anomaly: 1,011 records (2.0%) show source_updated_at timestamped after recorded_at, which is logically impossible. The maximum negative lag observed is approximately 1 hour. This likely reflects clock skew between retailer systems and the government ingestion platform. These records should be treated as unreliable for freshness analysis and may indicate a systemic timestamp synchronisation issue in the feed infrastructure.
Stale Data Concern: 3.6% of records (1,802) carry a source update lag exceeding 7 days. For consumers using the Fuel Finder app, these records may display prices that are materially incorrect. B10 fuel shows the worst stale-report rate at 28.1% of records exceeding 24 hours, though this fuel type has limited station coverage (34 stations) and low sample size.

The majority of stations (62.5%) report 3 or 4 fuel types, reflecting the standard UK forecourt offering of E10, B7_STANDARD, E5, and B7_PREMIUM. A substantial minority (37.2%) report only 1–2 fuel types, often smaller independents or stations focused on diesel-only fleets. Coverage Gap: HVO (Hydrotreated Vegetable Oil — a renewable diesel alternative) is available at only 54 stations in this dataset. As HVO becomes increasingly relevant to decarbonisation policy, its absence from the Fuel Finder scheme at most stations limits consumers' ability to make low-carbon fuel choices based on price information.
Section 06

Key Findings & Policy Implications

The following findings are synthesised from the quantitative analysis above. Each is framed for relevance to consumer protection policy, market transparency regulation, and the ongoing review of the UK Fuel Finder open data mandate.

1

Significant upward price trend across all fuel types (Feb–Apr 2026)

E10 rose from approximately 135p/litre in early February to 159p/litre by mid-April 2026 — an increase of ~18%. B7_STANDARD showed a steeper rise from ~141p to ~192p (+36%). These movements, occurring over just 10 weeks, represent a substantial real-terms cost increase for consumers and businesses dependent on road fuel.

Policy: Monitor wholesale-to-retail price pass-through rates; publish CMA market update
2

Diesel price disparity is the most acute consumer harm risk

B7_STANDARD (standard diesel) has an IQR of 33p/litre — more than double E10's IQR of 16p/litre. A consumer at the 75th-percentile diesel station pays 33p/litre more than one at the 25th percentile. For a commercial van with a 70-litre tank, this represents a £23.10 per-fill differential. This disproportionately affects small businesses, hauliers, and rural consumers with limited forecourt choice.

Policy: Strengthen price comparison tools; consider regional pricing transparency requirements
3

The majority of retailers update prices promptly — but a significant tail does not

78.4% of records show a source-to-collection lag under 1 hour, indicating most retailers comply in near-real-time. However, 10.5% of records (5,266) exceed a 24-hour lag, and 3.6% (1,802 records) reflect prices more than 7 days stale. At the station level, 18.7% of forecourts have at least one price record with a lag exceeding 7 days.

Policy: Mandate maximum 24-hour update frequency; introduce automated compliance monitoring
4

Negative timestamps indicate a systemic feed infrastructure issue

2.0% of records (1,011) carry source_updated_at timestamps that post-date recorded_at — a logical impossibility. This is not a data entry anomaly but a systemic pattern suggesting clock desynchronisation between retailer reporting systems and the government ingestion platform. These records cannot be used for freshness analysis and should be treated as unreliable.

Policy: Require NTP synchronisation audit of government feed infrastructure; add timestamp validation to API
5

Extreme outliers suggest incomplete data validation at the point of entry

123 records carry prices outside the plausible range of 50–300p/litre, including values of 0p, 1p, and up to 1,889p. These are almost certainly data entry errors or test records submitted through the government API without adequate validation. Their presence, while numerically small (0.25% of records), undermines public trust in the feed's accuracy.

Policy: Implement server-side price range validation (e.g. 70–250p) with rejection/quarantine of outliers
6

HVO and B10 coverage is negligible — a gap in green fuel transparency

HVO (renewable diesel) is reported by only 54 stations and B10 by only 34 stations in this dataset. As HVO increasingly features in fleet decarbonisation strategies and government Net Zero targets, the near-absence of these fuels from the Fuel Finder scheme means consumers cannot make price-informed low-carbon choices. The government should consider mandating HVO price reporting for stations that stock it.

Policy: Extend Fuel Finder mandate to HVO and B10; consider incentives for adoption reporting
7

E10 shows the most competitive pricing dynamics of any mainstream fuel

With an IQR of just 16p/litre (11% of median), E10 exhibits the tightest price clustering, suggesting effective market competition and strong consumer price-switching behaviour. The Fuel Finder scheme appears to be functioning as intended for this fuel type. Policymakers should examine what structural features drive E10 competitiveness and assess whether they can be replicated for diesel.

Policy: Use E10 as benchmark for assessing competitive effectiveness of open data mandates
