1. Dataset Overview & Coverage Summary

This analysis is based on government-mandated open fuel price data collected from UK forecourts. The dataset comprises 50,000 individual price observations across 7,373 unique stations (anonymised via hashed identifiers).

The data covers the period 07 February 2026 – 16 April 2026, spanning approximately 68 days. 6 distinct fuel types are represented.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

The following findings synthesise the main insights from this analysis, framed for government policy analysts and energy economists assessing market competitiveness, pricing transparency, and retailer compliance.

Finding 1
HVO is the most expensive fuel type; E10 the cheapest
The average price of HVO is 188.9 ppl, compared to E10 at 145.9 ppl — a difference of 43.0 ppl. Premium diesel commands a substantial premium over standard fuels, which is expected but the magnitude warrants continued monitoring.
Policy Implication: Policy focus should be on ensuring premium fuel pricing is transparent and that consumers are not inadvertently purchasing premium grades at inflated margins.
Finding 2
Significant price disparities exist within fuel types, especially E5
The raw price spread (max - min) for E5 is 1889.0 ppl, though this is inflated by erroneous outlier prices (e.g., prices below 100p or above 250p). More meaningfully, the IQR indicates consumers in the middle 50% of stations face a price band of 16.0 ppl (E10) to 33.0 ppl (B7_STANDARD). A typical motorist filling 50 litres could pay between £8.00 and £16.50 more depending on which station they visit.
Policy Implication: These disparities suggest either local market power or information asymmetry. Strengthening price transparency tools and promoting real-time comparison could improve consumer outcomes.
Finding 3
Most price updates are timely, but a notable tail of stale data exists
The median update lag is 0.0 hours. 80.4 pct of records have a lag under 1 hour, and 89.5 pct under 24 hours. However, 3.6 pct of records have lags exceeding one week, and 1.8 pct of stations (133 of 7,373) have a median lag over 168 hours. Negative lags (recorded before source update) affect 2.02 pct of records, suggesting minor clock synchronisation issues. This indicates a subset of retailers are not updating their feeds promptly.
Policy Implication: The government should consider enforcement mechanisms for timely price reporting, as stale data undermines the purpose of the open data scheme and disadvantages consumers relying on price comparison tools.
Finding 4
Fuel type coverage varies significantly across stations
B7_STANDARD is reported by 6,494 stations (88.1 pct), while B10 is only reported by 34 stations (0.5 pct). 796 stations (10.8 pct) report only a single fuel type, limiting comparison for consumers with specific fuel needs.
Policy Implication: Mandating broader fuel type reporting could improve market transparency, especially for consumers using alternative or premium fuels.
Finding 5
Diesel (B7) carries a premium over unleaded (E10)
B7_STANDARD averages 167.4 ppl vs E10 at 145.9 ppl — a difference of 21.5 ppl. This premium is consistent with wholesale market dynamics but the retail margin spread warrants continued monitoring.
Policy Implication: Tracking the diesel-unleaded price differential over time can reveal whether retailers pass through wholesale cost changes symmetrically or exercise pricing power.
Finding 6
Data quality issues: erroneous outlier prices detected
The dataset contains 80 records with prices below 100p and 50 records with prices above 250p — values almost certainly attributable to data entry errors, unit confusion, or reporting anomalies. Examples include prices of 0.0p, 1.0p, and values exceeding 1,000p per litre. These outliers inflate the max-min spread significantly and should be cleaned in any operational use of this data.
Policy Implication: The Fuel Finder scheme should implement validation rules at the point of data entry to reject prices outside plausible ranges, improving data quality for consumers and analysts alike.
