1. Dataset Overview & Coverage Summary

This report analyses 50,000 price records collected from 7,373 unique forecourts across England, Scotland, and Wales. The data spans the period from 07 February 2026 to 16 April 2026 (recorded timestamps), with source updates ranging from 17 December 2025 to 16 April 2026.

The majority of outliers (89) appear in early February, suggesting possible ingestion teething issues at dataset start.

The median update lag across all fuel types is approximately 0.05 hours (under 3 minutes), indicating that the data pipeline typically captures price changes almost immediately after retailers report them. However, the mean lag is far higher (19.15 hours) due to a long tail of stale records.

Data quality concern: 1,802 records (3.6%) have an update lag exceeding 7 days, and 1,011 records (2.0%) show negative lags where source_updated_at is later than recorded_at. Negative lags likely reflect clock synchronisation issues between retailer systems and the central data collector, but they undermine confidence in timestamp accuracy. The long-tail stale records suggest some stations go for extended periods without confirming prices, potentially leaving consumers reliant on outdated information.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Price hierarchy is clear and stable. E10 remains the cheapest mainstream fuel (~145 ppl mean), while premium diesel (B7_PREMIUM) and HVO both exceed 185 ppl on average. Policymakers should note that the E5 premium over E10 (~16 ppl) is modest, suggesting limited consumer harm from octane-grade pricing.
  2. Market competitiveness varies sharply by fuel type. E10 shows the tightest IQR (16.0 ppl), indicating a highly competitive standard-petrol market. Diesel markets (B7_STANDARD and B7_PREMIUM) exhibit wider spreads, giving retailers more pricing power. Regulators may wish to monitor diesel margins more closely than petrol.
  3. Extreme outliers signal data-quality risks. 126 records (0.25%) contain implausible prices, including zero values and entries above 1,000 ppl. While these are a small fraction, they risk eroding public trust in the open-data scheme if displayed to consumers. A real-time validation filter (e.g., rejecting prices outside 50–300 ppl) would be a low-cost improvement.
  4. Reporting freshness is generally excellent but with a concerning long tail. The median lag of 0.05 hours shows the pipeline works well for live data, yet 1,802 records (3.6%) are stale by over a week. Consider introducing a “data freshness” indicator on consumer-facing apps and prompting retailers with stale feeds to update.
  5. Alternative-fuel availability remains marginal. HVO and B10 are stocked by fewer than 1% of stations. If decarbonisation policy aims to shift demand toward these fuels, infrastructure incentives or supply-chain support may be needed before consumer behaviour can change materially.
  6. Negative timestamp lags require technical investigation. 1,011 records (2.0%) show source_updated_at later than recorded_at. This points to clock-drift or timezone-handling issues between retailer POS systems and the central aggregator. Resolving this would improve auditability and compliance monitoring.
