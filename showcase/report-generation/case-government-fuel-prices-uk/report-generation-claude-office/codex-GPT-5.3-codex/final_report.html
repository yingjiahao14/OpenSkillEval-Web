6) Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Fuel-type price hierarchy is clear: HVO is the highest-priced fuel on average (188.87 p/L), while E10 is lowest (145.90 p/L). This indicates a persistent grade premium structure relevant to consumer substitution behaviour.
  2. Consumers face non-trivial intra-fuel price dispersion: the widest max−min spread reaches 1889.00 p/L for E5, indicating substantial savings potential through comparison-shopping.
  3. IQR-based competitiveness differs by fuel: typical price bands (IQR) vary across fuels, with the tightest at 16.00 p/L (E10). Narrower IQR suggests stronger local price convergence.
  4. Reporting freshness is mixed: median lag is 0.05 hours, but 5,266 records exceed 24 hours. Tail lag behaviour implies uneven compliance/reporting cadence and can reduce consumer confidence in “live” prices.
  5. Coverage heterogeneity can bias apparent competition: fuel types with narrower station coverage are less comparable for consumers and may need separate policy treatment in transparency assessments.
  6. Regulatory implication: introducing or tightening lag-based service-level expectations (e.g., soft thresholds around 24h/72h) could improve data reliability without imposing direct price controls.
