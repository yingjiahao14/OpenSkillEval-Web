1. Dataset Overview & Coverage Summary

The dataset comprises 49,870 retail fuel price records collected from 7,365 unique UK forecourts (node_ids) between 2026-02-07 and 2026-04-16. Records were collected across six distinct fuel types, covering petrol, diesel, and alternative fuel categories under the UK Government's open Fuel Finder scheme.

Following removal of 130 records (0.26%) with prices outside the physiologically implausible range of 100–250 pence per litre (likely sensor or reporting errors, including entries priced at 1.0 ppl), the clean dataset stands at 49,870 records used for all downstream analysis.

⚠ 5,168 records (10.4%) show a reporting lag exceeding 24 hours. 9,690 records (19.4%) exceed 1 hour lag. Additionally, 1,011 records (2.03%) show a negative lag (recorded_at before source_updated_at), indicating clock-skew or data pipeline timing inconsistencies in the collection system.

The high standard deviations across all fuel types (60–111 hours) confirm a bimodal distribution: the majority of retailers update promptly (median under 3 minutes), while a subset update infrequently, sometimes going days without refreshing their reported price. This has direct implications for the reliability of price comparison tools relying on this feed.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

1
Petrol E10 is consistently cheapest; diesel consumers face higher and more variable prices. E10 averages 145.2 ppl — 22 ppl cheaper than B7_STANDARD (167.0 ppl) and 42 ppl cheaper than premium diesel variants. The diesel-petrol price gap is of material interest to consumers and warrants monitoring under fuel poverty assessments.
2
E5 super unleaded has the widest price spread (93 ppl), indicating significant retailer pricing heterogeneity. While E10 shows a tight IQR of 16 ppl (most stations cluster tightly), E5's 93 ppl range suggests some retailers are pricing E5 as a premium product at a wide variety of markups. This could reflect genuine octane-related cost differences — or room for retailer margin optimisation at the consumer's expense.
3
Approximately 10.4% of price records are stale (>24 hours old), undermining real-time price transparency. The median update lag of 2.9 minutes is excellent and reflects many retailers' near-real-time feed compliance. However, the 5,168 stale records represent a meaningful fraction where consumers using price comparison tools may see outdated information — directly reducing the scheme's intended consumer benefit.
4
Negative update lags detected in 1,011 records (2.03%) suggest systematic clock-skew in the data collection pipeline. These anomalies — where recorded_at precedes source_updated_at — are not physically possible and indicate data pipeline timing inconsistencies. This should be investigated and corrected; such anomalies reduce trust in data quality and may mask genuine staleness.
5
HVO and B10 are effectively niche fuels — available at fewer than 1% of stations. While these alternative fuels are important for fleet decarbonisation, their near-absence from the mainstream forecourt network limits consumer choice and the scheme's ability to support policy objectives around low-emission transport. Reporting coverage mandates for renewable fuels may need review.
6
Station-level fuel type diversity is low: 79.7% of stations report 3 or fewer fuel types. This is not necessarily a concern — E10 and B7_STANDARD coverage is broad (~85% each) — but it means price competition analysis is structurally limited to these two fuel types for the majority of stations. Any policy conclusions about market-wide price competition should be qualified accordingly.
