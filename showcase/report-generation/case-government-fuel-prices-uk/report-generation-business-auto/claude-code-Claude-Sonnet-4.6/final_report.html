This dataset is a 50,000-record sample drawn from the UK Government's mandatory Fuel Finder open data scheme, compiled by fuelcosts.co.uk. Each record represents a single fuel price observation at an anonymised forecourt. The source_updated_at timestamp in the raw data extends back to December 2025, indicating some stations had not refreshed their reported prices since late 2025 at the time of collection.

ℹ

Additionally, 1,011 records (2.0%) exhibit a negative update lag (source timestamp after recorded timestamp), which may indicate clock-skew or feed submission anomalies — these are excluded from freshness analysis.

Section 03

Price Spread & Market Competitiveness

Price spread — both the interquartile range (IQR, covering the middle 50% of stations) and the full max-minus-min range — is the most direct indicator of market competitiveness available in this dataset. A narrow IQR suggests convergent pricing; a wide one signals that consumers face meaningfully different costs depending on which forecourt they visit.

Section 04

Retailer Reporting Freshness Analysis

Update lag measures the time between when a retailer last updated their price in the government feed (source_updated_at) and when it was captured by the data collection system (recorded_at). A lag of near-zero indicates a live, up-to-date price; large lags indicate stale data that may misrepresent what a consumer would actually pay on arrival.

This analysis covers 48,989 records with non-negative lags. The 1,011 records (2.0%) where source_updated_at falls after recorded_at are anomalous (possible clock-skew) and are excluded.

Section 06

Key Findings & Policy Implications

The following findings are derived exclusively from the 50,000-record sample analysed. Each finding is linked to a concrete policy implication for the fuel market regulation and consumer protection agenda.

  • 1
    Diesel price dispersion is significantly higher than petrol — and materially expensive for consumers
    Standard diesel (B7_STANDARD) shows an IQR of 33.0p/l (19.7% of median), more than double that of E10 petrol (IQR: 16.0p/l, 11.0%). A driver using a 50-litre tank who fills at a P75 vs. P25 forecourt pays £16.50 more per fill — over £850/year for weekly fillers. Premium diesel (B7_PREMIUM) shows a similarly wide IQR of 30.0p/l.
    Policy implication: The diesel market shows weaker price convergence than petrol. Regulators should consider whether the open data scheme is generating sufficient competitive pressure in diesel pricing, and whether targeted consumer communications (e.g. best-price alerts) are warranted for B7_STANDARD users.
  • 2
    The government price feed is predominantly real-time, but a stale tail merits attention
    80% of records carry a lag of under 1 hour between retailer update and data capture, with a median lag of just 3 minutes. However, 3.7% of records (≈1,802) are over 7 days stale, and the worst case is 52+ days old. These stale records may be actively displayed to consumers via the Fuel Finder service, undermining the scheme's transparency goal.
    Policy implication: Introduce a mandatory staleness threshold (e.g. 48 hours) after which records are flagged or suppressed in consumer-facing interfaces. Stations with persistently stale data should be identified for compliance review.
  • 3
    HVO (renewable diesel) has the poorest reporting discipline of mainstream fuel types
    HVO records show a median lag of 1.04 hours (vs. ~3 minutes for mainstream fuels) and only 48.3% are fresh within 1 hour. While the HVO sample is small (91 records, 54 stations), it represents an emerging fuel category relevant to net-zero targets. Poor price visibility may deter consumer uptake.
    Policy implication: As the government promotes alternative fuels, HVO stations should receive specific guidance on feed update obligations. Incentives or audits targeting the green fuel segment would improve data reliability in this growth area.
  • 4
    Premium fuel premiums are substantial and warrant scrutiny
    B7_PREMIUM diesel commands a median premium of 20.0p/l over B7_STANDARD (187.9p vs. 167.9p). E5 super unleaded costs a median of 16.0p/l more than E10 (160.9p vs. 144.9p). These spreads are significant in absolute consumer cost terms; the B7_PREMIUM premium alone adds ~£10 per 50-litre tank fill.
    Policy implication: Regulators should monitor whether premium fuel mark-ups reflect genuine product differentiation costs or reflect reduced competitive pressure at the premium end of the market. The wide IQR of B7_PREMIUM (30p/l) suggests limited price discovery for premium diesel buyers.
  • 5
    10.8% of stations report only a single fuel type — potential compliance gap
    796 forecourts (10.8%) report just one fuel type in this dataset. While some may be genuine specialty operators (e.g. HVO-only or fleet diesel), others may be partially non-compliant with the UK mandatory open data reporting requirement, which mandates reporting of all fuel types sold.
    Policy implication: A targeted audit of single-fuel-type reporting stations could identify whether this represents operational specialisation or selective compliance. Cross-referencing with operator licensing data (outside the scope of this dataset) would quantify the non-compliance risk.
  • 6
    Data integrity anomalies (negative lags, extreme outliers) suggest feed quality controls are insufficient
    2.0% of records (1,011) carry a source timestamp after the recorded timestamp — physically impossible in a correct feed. A further 174 records (0.35%) carry prices outside any plausible range (0p to 1,889p/l). These anomalies are distributed across fuel types and are unlikely to be isolated incidents.
    Policy implication: The scheme administrator should implement automated validation rules at feed ingestion: reject prices outside a plausible range (e.g. 80–300p), flag timestamps where source > recorded, and provide quarterly data quality reports to participating retailers. A cleaner feed directly improves consumer trust in the Fuel Finder service.
