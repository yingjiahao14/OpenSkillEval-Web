6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Significant Price Dispersion Exists, Especially in Premium Segments
    While E10 standard unleaded shows a tight IQR of 16.00p, premium diesel (B7 Premium) and B10 exhibit IQRs of 30.00p and 48.60p respectively. Consumers of premium and specialty fuels face substantially greater price variation, suggesting weaker price competition in these segments. Policy implication: Targeted consumer-information campaigns for premium-fuel buyers could yield meaningful savings.
  2. All Fuel Types Experienced Sharp Price Rises Through Q1 2026
    Between February and April 2026, average prices rose 21.21p for E10, 46.61p for B7 Standard Diesel, and 45.06p for B7 Premium Diesel. Diesel grades rose faster than petrol, widening the diesel-premium gap. Policy implication: Monitor diesel price dynamics closely; the accelerating spread may signal supply-chain pressure or margin expansion in the diesel market.
  3. Data Freshness is Generally Good, but a Substantial Minority of Stations Lag
    The median update lag across all records is just 0.048 hours, indicating the open-data pipeline is largely effective. However, 5,266 records (10.5%) and 342 stations (4.6%) exhibit median lags exceeding 24 hours. Policy implication: Consider introducing a maximum reporting-lag threshold (e.g., 24 hours) with automated alerts for non-compliant retailers to ensure consumers see current prices.
  4. 92 Clear Price Errors Flagged — Data Validation Should Be Strengthened
    Approximately 0.18% of records contain implausible prices (≤1p or >300p), clustered in early-February data. While the error rate is low, the magnitude of some errors (up to £18.89/litre) would severely mislead consumers if displayed uncritically. Policy implication: Mandate real-time range-validation checks at the point of data ingestion (e.g., reject prices outside 50p–300p) to prevent erroneous data reaching consumer-facing apps.
  5. Premium Fuel Availability is Uneven — Consumer Choice Varies by Location
    Only 44% of stations offer premium diesel and 60% offer super unleaded (E5). HVO and B10 are available at fewer than 1% of forecourts. Policy implication: If government seeks to accelerate adoption of lower-carbon fuels (HVO, B10), infrastructure incentives or minimum-availability requirements may be needed to ensure geographic equity of access.
  6. The Market for Core Fuels is Broadly Competitive and Transparent
    With 6,244 stations (84.7%) offering E10 and 6,494 (88.1%) offering B7 Standard Diesel, and with narrow IQRs for both, the core fuel market appears to function competitively. 5,545 stations (75.2%) stock both core fuels, giving most consumers meaningful choice. Policy implication: Current open-data policy for standard grades is working; maintain and extend the scheme to ensure real-time accuracy.
