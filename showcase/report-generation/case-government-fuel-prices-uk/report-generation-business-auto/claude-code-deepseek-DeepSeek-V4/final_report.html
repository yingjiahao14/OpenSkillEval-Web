UK Retail Fuel Price Trend Analysis

A comprehensive analysis of retail fuel pricing patterns across UK forecourts, examining price distributions by fuel type and temporal update behaviour based on government-mandated open data.
Prepared for: Government Policy Analysts & Energy Economists • February 2026 • Source: UK Government Fuel Finder Scheme via fuelcosts.co.uk • Licence: CC BY 4.0

1. Dataset Overview & Coverage Summary

The dataset comprises 50,000 price observations collected from 7,373 unique forecourts across England, Scotland, and Wales. Records span from 7 February 2026 to 16 April 2026, covering a 68-day window of the UK Fuel Finder open data scheme. Six fuel types are represented, with E10 standard unleaded and B7_STANDARD diesel accounting for the majority of records.

Data Quality Note: 127 records (0.25% of total) contained implausible price values — including zero, negative, or extreme outliers exceeding reasonable UK forecourt price ranges. These records were excluded from the statistical analyses below. The main body of data is of good quality with no missing values across any column.

3. Price Spread & Market Competitiveness

Price spreads — the difference between the cheapest and most expensive forecourt for the same fuel — are a direct measure of market competitiveness. The interquartile range (IQR) indicates the band within which the middle 50% of prices fall, providing a better gauge of the typical consumer experience than the full min–max range.

Key competitiveness observations:

  • E10 (Standard Unleaded) shows the narrowest IQR at 16.0 ppl, indicating a relatively tight market. However, the full spread of 96.9 ppl means some consumers are paying substantially more than the cheapest available option.
  • E5 (Super Unleaded) has an IQR of 18.0 ppl and a full spread of 93.0 ppl — a meaningful price gap for consumers who require higher-octane fuel.
  • B7_STANDARD (Standard Diesel) shows the widest IQR among mainstream fuels at 33.0 ppl, with a max–min spread of 140.4 ppl. This suggests diesel pricing is notably less consistent across the UK than petrol.
  • B7_PREMIUM (Premium Diesel) has an IQR of 30.0 ppl and the largest max–min spread among premium fuels at 165.9 ppl.
  • B10 and HVO niche fuels show wider proportional spreads but are based on much smaller sample sizes (54 and 89 records respectively).
Policy Relevance: The 16.0–33.0 ppl IQR range across mainstream fuels translates to a potential saving of £8–£17 on a typical 50-litre fill-up simply by choosing a lower-priced forecourt. For diesel drivers, the disparity is particularly acute: a 33.0 ppl IQR represents roughly a 20% variation around the median price.

4. Retailer Reporting Freshness Analysis

The lag between source_updated_at (when the retailer last confirmed their price) and recorded_at (when the data was collected) measures how fresh the reported prices are. A smaller lag indicates more frequent and timely retailer reporting.

Update Lag by Fuel Type

Reporting behaviour patterns:

  • The median update lag is just 3 minutes — indicating that the majority of retailers have automated, near-real-time price feeds. This is a strong signal of a functional reporting infrastructure.
  • E5 (Super Unleaded) shows the worst freshness profile: a mean lag of 25.8 hours and a P90 of 36.1 hours, with the P99 reaching 520 hours (~22 days). Super unleaded prices are updated less frequently than standard grades.
  • B7_STANDARD (Standard Diesel) and B7_PREMIUM have the best reporting compliance, with P90 lags of only 8.5 and 26.9 hours respectively.
  • 1,011 records (2.0%) show a negative lag where source_updated_at is after recorded_at, suggesting clock synchronisation issues or timestamp recording order errors in the data pipeline.
  • Approximately 3.2% of records have a lag exceeding 7 days, indicating some stations only update sporadically — a concern for consumers relying on the published data for real-time price comparison.
Compliance Concern: While the median reporting lag is excellent, the long tail of stale data (P99 > 460 hours for all fuel types) means a small subset of forecourts are reporting prices that may be weeks out of date, undermining the scheme's value for real-time consumer decision-making.

5. Station Coverage & Fuel Type Availability

Of the 7,373 unique forecourts in the dataset, not all report every fuel type. Understanding which fuels have the broadest station coverage is important for assessing the scheme's comprehensiveness and identifying gaps in consumer choice data.

Fuel Types Reported Per Station

Coverage insights:

  • B7_STANDARD (6,494 stations, 88.1%) and E10 (6,244 stations, 84.7%) have near-universal coverage — as expected for standard diesel and unleaded, the two most commonly sold fuel types in the UK.
  • E5 Super Unleaded is reported at 4,447 stations (60.3%), reflecting its status as a premium option available at most but not all forecourts.
  • B7_PREMIUM (3,241 stations, 44.0%) coverage is notably lower — nearly half of forecourts do not report a premium diesel price.
  • HVO (54 stations) and B10 (34 stations) are niche fuels with very limited coverage, as expected for emerging/specialist fuel types.
  • The average station reports 2.8 fuel types (median 3), with the most common configuration being 3 fuel types (2,717 stations). Only 2 stations report all 6 fuel types.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Price Transparency Reveals Meaningful Consumer Savings Opportunities

    The IQR of 16.0–33.0 ppl across mainstream fuels means consumers can save £8–£17 on a 50-litre fill-up by choosing a competitively priced forecourt. For diesel drivers, the 33.0 ppl IQR (roughly 20% of the median price) is a material household budget consideration. Policy recommendation: strengthen consumer-facing price comparison tools and consider mandatory forecourt price display requirements at point-of-entry.

  2. Diesel Market Shows Lower Price Consistency Than Petrol

    B7_STANDARD diesel has the widest IQR (33.0 ppl) among mainstream fuels, compared to 16.0 ppl for E10. Combined with the largest station-to-station spread (140.4 ppl max−min), this suggests diesel pricing is less competitive and more geographically fragmented. This warrants further investigation into whether rural or motorway diesel pricing is disproportionately high.

  3. Real-Time Reporting Is Strong Overall, but a Long Tail of Staleness Persists

    The median update lag of ~3 minutes demonstrates that the majority of retailers have functioning automated reporting. However, approximately 3.2% of records are over 7 days stale, and P99 lags exceed 460 hours for all fuel types. These stale records undermine the scheme's credibility for time-sensitive consumer decision-making. A minimum reporting frequency standard, with compliance monitoring, would improve data utility.

  4. Super Unleaded (E5) Reporting Quality Lags Behind Standard Grades

    E5 has the poorest update freshness (mean 25.8h lag, P90 36.1h), the highest proportion of stale records, and the second-highest outlier rate. This may reflect lower prioritisation of premium fuel reporting by retailers. Given that E5 is often the only choice for older vehicles not compatible with E10, this data gap has equity implications.

  5. Data Quality Controls Are Needed for the Fuel Finder Feed

    127 records (0.25%) contained implausible price values — including 0, 1, and values exceeding 1,800 ppl — along with 2.0% of records exhibiting negative update lags. While the overall data quality is good, a lightweight validation layer (range checks, timestamp ordering) before publication would prevent misleading data from reaching consumer-facing tools.

  6. Station Coverage Is Comprehensive for Mainstream Fuels, but Premium and Niche Fuels Have Significant Gaps

    With 88% and 85% of stations reporting B7_STANDARD and E10 respectively, the scheme provides excellent coverage of standard fuels. However, the 40% gap between E10 and E5 coverage means super unleaded pricing data is missing for roughly 2,900 stations — a blind spot for consumers and analysts assessing premium fuel market dynamics.
