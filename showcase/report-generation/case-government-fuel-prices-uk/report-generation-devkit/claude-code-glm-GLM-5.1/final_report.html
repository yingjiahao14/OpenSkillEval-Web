6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Standard diesel (B7_STANDARD) and E10 unleaded dominate the dataset, accounting for the vast majority of price records. These two fuel types have the broadest station coverage and the most data points, making them the most reliable indicators of market conditions.
  2. Consumers face significant price disparities. The E5 fuel type shows a price spread of 1889.0p per litre across stations. Even for the most common fuels, the IQR of 33.0p (B7_STANDARD) and 16.0p (E10) means consumers can save several pence per litre by shopping around — equating to roughly £3–4 on a typical 55-litre fill.
  3. Data freshness is generally good but with concerning outliers. The median update lag is 0.0h, indicating most prices are captured within minutes of being reported. However, 7.4% of records (3,687) have lags exceeding 48 hours, and 3.6% (1,802) exceed one week — suggesting some retailers are not updating their feeds promptly. This undermines the utility of the transparency scheme for consumers relying on current prices.
  4. Premium fuels show wider price variation than standard grades. B7_PREMIUM has a spread of 1398.0p and IQR of 30.0p, compared to B7_STANDARD's IQR of 33.0p. This suggests less price competition in the premium segment, where consumers may be less price-sensitive.
  5. Station fuel type coverage varies significantly. While many stations report prices for 2–3 fuel types, coverage for specialty fuels like B7_PREMIUM and B10 is limited to a small fraction of stations. Policy makers should consider whether mandated reporting should be extended or more strictly enforced to ensure comprehensive consumer access to price information.
