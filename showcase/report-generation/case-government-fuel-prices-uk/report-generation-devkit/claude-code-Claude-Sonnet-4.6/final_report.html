Dataset Overview & Coverage Summary

This dataset is sourced from the UK Government's mandatory Fuel Finder open-data scheme, compiled by fuelcosts.co.uk. It contains 50,000 price records spanning 7,373 unique forecourts across England, Scotland, and Wales. Each record captures a single fuel-price observation at a specific station and timestamp, covering the period from 7 February to 16 April 2026.

Retailer Reporting Freshness Analysis

The update lag is defined as recorded_at − source_updated_at. A lag of zero means the data collection system captured the price immediately after the retailer reported it. Large positive lags indicate stale prices — the retailer has not updated their reported price even though the collection system has re-polled them. Negative lags (1,011 records, 2.0%) indicate timestamps where source_updated_at is later than recorded_at, likely reflecting minor clock skew or feed processing delays; these are excluded from freshness calculations.

§ 6

Key Findings & Policy Implications

The following findings synthesise the most policy-relevant insights from the data. Each finding is accompanied by its primary implication for consumer protection, market regulation, or fuel pricing policy.

1
Market Structure

E10 is the most price-competitive fuel; diesel markets show greater consumer disparity

E10 (standard petrol) has the tightest IQR at 16 ppl, indicating broad pricing competition among stations. By contrast, B7_STANDARD diesel has an IQR of 33 ppl — more than double — meaning a diesel driver has a much wider price range to navigate. At 50 litres per fill, this IQR gap represents a potential £8.50 per-tank difference in cost, purely from choosing between middle-of-the-market stations. Premium diesel (B7_PREMIUM) has a similarly wide IQR of 30 ppl. Policy implication: Consumer-facing price comparison tools should especially highlight diesel price variability at the local level to help motorists make cost-informed decisions.

2
Pricing Trend

All fuel prices have risen substantially over the analysis period (Feb–Apr 2026)

Median prices rose across all four core fuel types between February and April 2026: E10 +18.9% (132.0 → 156.9 ppl), E5 +16.7% (149.9 → 174.9 ppl), B7_STANDARD +33.7% (142.0 → 189.9 ppl), and B7_PREMIUM +28.1% (163.9 → 209.9 ppl). The scale of this increase — particularly for diesel — in under 10 weeks is striking. Policy implication: The government should cross-reference this trend with wholesale crude and refinery margins to determine whether retail prices are rising proportionately or evidence of margin widening is present.

3
Retailer Compliance

The majority of retailers update prices promptly; a significant minority have stale data

83% of records have a lag under one hour (median ~3 minutes), indicating strong compliance across most retailers. However, 1,377 unique stations (18.7% of all forecourts) have at least one price record lagging more than 7 days, and maximum lags exceed 52 days. These stations are failing to maintain current reported prices. Policy implication: Regulators should consider a mandatory maximum-lag standard (e.g. 24-hour update requirement) and publish a non-compliance list to drive improvement through market and reputational pressure.

4
Data Quality

A small number of anomalous price records suggest data validation gaps in the feed

Across all fuel types, fewer than 0.4% of records contain prices outside the plausible 100–250 ppl range — including values as low as 0 ppl and as high as 1,879 ppl. Additionally, 2.0% of records have negative lags (source timestamp after recorded timestamp), suggesting clock synchronisation or feed processing issues. While individually minor, these anomalies indicate that server-side validation of the Fuel Finder data submission API should be strengthened. Policy implication: Input validation rules on the government feed (minimum/maximum price bounds, timestamp consistency) would improve data integrity at source.

5
Future-Proofing

Low-carbon fuels (HVO, B10) have negligible market coverage; early-stage monitoring is critical

HVO and B10 together represent fewer than 0.3% of records and are available at fewer than 1% of forecourts. HVO also shows elevated reporting lag (median 1.04 hrs vs. 0.05 hrs for conventional fuels). As government policy promotes decarbonisation of road transport, ensuring these fuel types are accurately and promptly reported will be increasingly important for consumer transparency and policy evaluation. Policy implication: Establish tracking KPIs for low-carbon fuel station coverage growth alongside conventional fuel reporting quality metrics.

6
Consumer Access

796 stations report only a single fuel type — potential under-reporting gap

10.8% of forecourts in the dataset report prices for only one fuel type. While some specialist retailers may legitimately offer only one fuel, many UK forecourts sell both petrol and diesel. This gap may represent partial non-compliance with the open data mandate. Policy implication: The regulator should cross-reference the Fuel Finder submission records against DVLA/HMRC fuel retail licence data to identify forecourts that sell multiple fuels but report only one — a potential compliance enforcement priority.
