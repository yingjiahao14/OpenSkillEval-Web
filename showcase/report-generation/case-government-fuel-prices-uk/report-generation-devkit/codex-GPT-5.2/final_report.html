Price Spread & Market Competitiveness

This section focuses on dispersion measures that are most policy-relevant for competition and consumer outcomes: the interquartile range (IQR) as the “typical” spread most consumers face, and the full max–min range which surfaces extreme outliers.

Key dispersion indicators (by fuel type)

Largest IQR: B10 at 48.80 PPL.
Smallest IQR: E10 at 16.00 PPL.
Largest max–min range: E5 at 1,889.00 PPL.

Interpretation note: very large max–min ranges can be driven by data errors or exceptional observations (e.g., implausibly low values). IQR is more robust for assessing typical competitive dispersion.

Competitiveness framing

  1. Higher IQR implies wider “normal” price bands across stations, consistent with weaker price convergence and potentially lower transparency or competitive intensity.
  2. Lower IQR implies tighter clustering of prices, consistent with stronger convergence (though not necessarily lower absolute prices).
  3. Outlier-heavy ranges should be investigated for data integrity and/or exceptional station behaviour before being used for enforcement decisions.

Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Price dispersion is material for several major fuels. The largest IQR is for B10 (48.80 PPL), indicating a wide “typical” price band across stations. Policy implication: consider whether transparency measures (e.g., consumer-facing tools) are sufficient to drive convergence where typical dispersion is high.
  2. Extreme price ranges are very large and appear outlier-driven. The largest max–min range occurs for E5 (1,889.00 PPL), alongside 76 observations under 50 PPL. Policy implication: treat max–min as an alerting metric; pair it with robust statistics (IQR) and data validation when assessing competitiveness or enforcement triggers.
  3. Reporting is usually near-real-time, but a non-trivial tail is stale. Overall median update lag is 0.05 hours, yet 10.53% of observations lag >24h and 3.60% lag >7 days. Policy implication: compliance monitoring should focus on the long tail of stale updates, not the median performer.
  4. Some timestamp inconsistencies exist. 1,011 observations (2.02%) have negative lag (up to ~60 minutes). Policy implication: strengthen data-quality checks (clock sync and ingestion ordering) before using freshness metrics for penalties.
  5. Niche fuels have limited coverage. HVO appears at 54 stations and B10 at 34 stations, so dispersion/freshness metrics for these should be interpreted cautiously. Policy implication: avoid over-weighting niche categories in market-wide conclusions.

Method notes: Prices are analysed in pence per litre as provided. Update lag is computed as recorded_at − source_updated_at in hours (UTC timestamps). Visuals are embedded inline (SVG) and the report is self-contained.
