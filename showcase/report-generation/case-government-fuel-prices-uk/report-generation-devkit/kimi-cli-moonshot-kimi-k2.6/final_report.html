3. Price Spread & Competitiveness Analysis

Price spread—the gap between the cheapest and most expensive station for a given fuel—serves as a proxy for market competitiveness. A narrow spread suggests tight competition and transparent pricing; a wide spread may indicate local market power, supply constraints, or data-quality issues.

Figure 2. Total price range (max–min) versus inter-quartile range by fuel type. A large gap between total range and IQR signals the presence of outliers even within the cleaned dataset.

The mean price spread across all fuel types is 43.4p per litre (from E10 at 132.95p to HVO at 176.35p). Among mainstream fuels, the E5–E10 differential is 22.96p, which has direct implications for consumer choice and biofuel policy incentives.

Notably, E10 records the smallest IQR (5.10p), confirming it as the most uniformly priced fuel. B7_STANDARD follows closely (IQR 5.90p). The premium diesel segment (B7_PREMIUM) shows a moderate IQR of 10.00p, reflecting brand differentiation and additive premiums.

Competitiveness assessment: The petrol market (E10, E5) displays a bimodal competitiveness profile—E10 is tightly clustered, while E5 shows wider dispersion (IQR 12.10p), suggesting heterogeneous station strategies for premium unleaded. Diesel markets (B7_STANDARD vs. B7_PREMIUM) are well-segmented, with a 19.78p mean premium for the upgraded variant.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. E10 is the most competitively priced and least volatile mainstream fuel. With a mean of 132.95p and an IQR of only 5.10p, E10 offers consumers the most predictable pricing. For policymakers, this supports the continued E10 blending mandate as a cost-stabilisation mechanism. Biofuel Policy
  2. A significant mean-price spread (43.4p) exists between the cheapest and most expensive fuel grades. HVO and B7_PREMIUM command premiums of 43.41p and 29.10p respectively over E10. This gradient has equity implications for consumers dependent on specific fuel types (e.g., older vehicles restricted to E5). Consumer Protection
  3. Diesel markets show clear premium segmentation. B7_PREMIUM averages 162.05p versus 142.27p for B7_STANDARD—a 19.78p differential. The tight IQR of B7_STANDARD (5.90p) suggests effective competition in the mass-market diesel segment. Market Regulation
  4. Update freshness is bimodal: near-real-time for some stations, severely stale for others. With a median lag of 22.7 hours but 20.8% of records exceeding one week, the data pipeline exhibits a "two-speed" pattern. Regulators should consider mandating maximum update-frequency thresholds for price-comparison platforms. Data Governance
  5. Data-quality issues remain material. 85 anomalous records in February alone—including zero-priced sales and spikes above 1,000p—indicate upstream validation gaps. A pre-publication sanity-check filter (e.g., 50p–500p bounds) would improve dataset reliability without distorting legitimate market signals. Data Integrity
  6. Alternative fuels (B10, HVO) lack market depth. With only 16 and 16 observations respectively in February, statistical confidence is low. Policy incentives for these fuels should be paired with enhanced monitoring infrastructure to ensure robust price transparency. Net-Zero Transition
  7. All fuel categories exhibit a rising price trajectory from February to April 2026. E10 rose 24.44p, B7_STANDARD rose 47.62p, and B7_PREMIUM rose 46.04p. This broad-based escalation warrants monitoring against wholesale cost pass-through benchmarks to detect potential margin expansion. Price Monitoring

7. Data Quality & Limitations

  • Outlier treatment: 85 records (1.02% of February data) were excluded for falling outside the 50p–500p per litre band. This includes 1 zero-priced entries and 29 extreme spikes. These values are treated as data errors rather than market signals.
  • No geographic or brand metadata: The dataset lacks station location, retailer brand, or regional identifiers. Consequently, spatial price analysis and brand-competitiveness assessments are not possible.
  • Sample-size disparity: B10 and HVO are represented by fewer than 20 stations each. Statistics for these fuels should be interpreted with caution and not used for high-stakes policy design without supplementary data.
  • Temporal scope: February 2026 data is concentrated in the period 7–28 February. Early-month coverage may be incomplete, and the dataset contains a material March/April extension used here only for trend context.
