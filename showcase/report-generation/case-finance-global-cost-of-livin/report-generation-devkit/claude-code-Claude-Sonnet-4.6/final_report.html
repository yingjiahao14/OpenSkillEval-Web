Section 03

Affordability and Rent Burden Analysis

Housing cost as a share of income is the single most actionable affordability metric for policymakers. This section examines the rent-to-salary ratio across all 80 cities, identifying where housing costs represent a structural crisis regardless of nominal cost levels.

Global Mean Rent-to-Salary Ratio: 61.5%

On average, a city worker globally spends more than 61 cents of every dollar earned on city-center rent alone — a ratio widely considered unsustainable at scale. 27 of 80 cities (33.8%) exceed 65% rent burden.

High-Income Cities ≠ Affordable Cities

Seattle's rent-to-salary ratio of 71.2% is the highest globally, exceeding even low-income cities. The top 5 highest RTS% cities are all high-income (US/Swiss) — demonstrating that wage growth has not kept pace with rent inflation.

Section 05

Inflation and Oil Shock Exposure

External economic shocks — sustained inflation and oil price volatility — amplify cost-of-living pressures beyond structural income-cost mismatches. This section assesses vulnerability to these shocks at city and exposure-category level.

Extreme Inflation Outliers

Buenos Aires (150%), Beirut (50%), and Istanbul (47%) represent existential affordability threats. At these inflation levels, salaries lose purchasing power faster than wages can adjust, trapping residents in deepening poverty.

High Oil Exposure Correlation

Cities with High oil shock exposure average 20.4% inflation — nearly 6× the rate of Low-exposure cities (3.4%). This correlation signals that oil-import dependency is a structural multiplier of inflationary risk.

Section 06

Key Findings and Policy Implications

Synthesis of analysis across all dimensions into actionable findings. Cities and regions requiring urgent attention are identified, with directional policy focus areas derived directly from the data.

Key Findings

1

Rent Burden Is a Universal Crisis — Not Confined to Low-Income Cities

The global mean rent-to-salary ratio of 61.5% signals a cross-income-level housing affordability crisis. Remarkably, the highest rent burdens are found in wealthy cities: Seattle (71.2%), Zurich (70.8%), Geneva (70.2%), Lausanne (70.1%), and Boston (69.9%). 27 of 80 cities exceed 65% — meaning a third of all analyzed cities have structurally unsustainable housing markets. Policy interventions focused solely on "poor" cities will miss the majority of the affected population.

2

Tier 5 Cities Face a Compound Crisis: Low Wages + High Inflation + Oil Dependency

The 14 Tier 5 cities average $455/month in salaries against a 28.4% average inflation rate. Crucially, 11 of these 14 cities have High oil shock exposure, indicating that external commodity price shocks amplify domestic inflation in structurally vulnerable economies. Buenos Aires (150%), Beirut (50%), Cairo (28%), and Lagos (34%) require immediate attention as hyperinflationary conditions erode real wages faster than any housing subsidy can compensate.

3

South Asia Hosts the Largest Concentration of Severe Affordability Crises

South Asia has 8 cities in the dataset, with 5 classified Tier 5 (Severe) — the highest concentration of any region. Average salary in the region is $553/month with 53.6% average rent burden and 8.4% average inflation. Mumbai, Delhi, Dhaka, Karachi, and Lahore collectively represent over 100 million urban residents facing severe affordability conditions. India alone (Mumbai, Delhi, Bangalore) spans Tiers 4–5 despite rapid GDP growth, indicating urban wage gains are not translating into affordability improvements.

4

The Purchasing Power Gap of 148.7 Index Points Reflects Extreme Global Inequality

Basel's PPP index of 183.7 versus Beirut's 35.0 spans nearly the entire range of economic capacity. Tier 1 cities average PPP of 157.1 versus Tier 5 average of 48.0 — a 3.3× differential. This means residents in the most affluent cities have more than 3× the effective purchasing power of those in crisis cities, even before accounting for differences in social safety nets or currency volatility.

5

Oil Shock Exposure Is the Strongest Predictor of Inflation Vulnerability

Cities with High oil exposure average 20.4% inflation versus 3.4% for Low-exposure cities — a 6× differential. This is the largest structural predictor in the dataset. High-exposure cities are concentrated in South Asia, West Africa, and the Middle East conflict zones. Energy transition policies and fuel-price subsidy frameworks are likely more impactful than direct cost-of-living interventions in these markets.

6

Western Europe and North America Drive High RTS% Despite High Wages

Western Europe averages 66.9% RTS and North America 67.0% — the two highest regional rent burdens globally — despite having the highest average salaries ($3,200–$6,800/month range). This indicates rent inflation has consistently outpaced wage growth in advanced economies. The dominant crisis label in North America is "High Cost – High Wages," suggesting structural supply constraints in housing rather than income inadequacy as the primary driver.

Areas for Further Research

  • Informal economy adjustments: Many Tier 4–5 cities have significant informal sectors; official salary figures likely understate true consumption capacity and overstate relative affordability.
  • Temporal tracking: Single-year snapshots obscure trends. Longitudinal data would identify which cities are converging toward or diverging from crisis conditions fastest.
  • Subsidy/social transfer effects: The dataset reflects market prices without adjusting for housing vouchers, rent controls, or social benefits that materially alter effective affordability in some cities.
  • Within-city inequality: City-level averages mask extreme distributional effects; median and percentile data would better capture the lived experience of lower-income urban residents.
