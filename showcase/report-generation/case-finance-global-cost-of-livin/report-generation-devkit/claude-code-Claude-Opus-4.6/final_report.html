1. Executive Overview

This report examines cost-of-living pressures across 80 major cities worldwide in 2026, spanning 16 geographic regions. The analysis reveals a deeply fragmented global economic landscape: while affluent cities in Western Europe and North America face high absolute costs offset by strong purchasing power, cities in South Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa, and conflict-affected regions confront severe affordability crises driven by low incomes, hyperinflation, and geopolitical instability.

All 80 cities in the dataset have rent-to-salary ratios exceeding 50%, indicating that housing affordability is a universal pressure point — but the drivers and severity vary enormously.

3. Affordability and Rent Burden Analysis

Housing cost as a share of income is the single most actionable metric for policymakers. A rent-to-salary ratio above 30% is conventionally considered burdensome; above 50% is severe. Every city in this dataset exceeds 50%, indicating a global housing affordability crisis — but the nature of the crisis differs dramatically between high-income and low-income cities.

Rent Burden Distribution

Critical finding: All 80 cities have rent-to-salary ratios above 50%. The global mean is 61.5%. The highest burden is in Seattle (71.2%) and the lowest is in Lahore (51.0%). The range is narrow (20.2 percentage points), but the underlying economics are fundamentally different: high-income cities have high rents offset by strong salaries, while low-income cities have low rents that nonetheless consume a large share of meager incomes.

Salary vs. Rent Burden: A Structural Divide

The scatter plot below reveals two distinct clusters: (1) high-income cities ($3,000+/month) with rent burdens of 62–72%, where high absolute rents drive the ratio; and (2) low-income cities (<$1,500/month) with rent burdens of 51–60%, where even modest rents are unaffordable relative to income.

Notably, the highest rent burdens are concentrated in the wealthiest cities — Swiss cities (Zurich 70.8%, Geneva 70.2%), U.S. tech hubs (Seattle 71.2%, San Francisco 69.2%), and Nordic capitals. This suggests that even strong salaries cannot fully offset extreme housing costs in these markets. However, for lower-income cities, the policy concern is more acute: a 53% rent burden on $280/month (Kathmandu) or $165/month (Cairo) leaves virtually no margin for other essentials.

6. Key Findings and Policy Implications

Core Findings

1. Housing affordability is a universal crisis, but structurally bifurcated. All 80 cities show rent-to-salary ratios above 50%. In high-income cities, this is driven by extreme housing costs despite strong wages. In low-income cities, it reflects the inability of even modest rents to be absorbed by meager incomes. Policy interventions must be tailored: supply-side reforms for the former, income and subsidy support for the latter.

2. Fourteen cities face acute, multidimensional crises. The 14 Tier 5 cities — including Delhi, Dhaka, Karachi, Cairo, Lagos, Buenos Aires, Beirut, and Kyiv — combine low purchasing power (avg 48.0), extremely low salaries (avg $455), and high inflation (avg 28.4%). These cities require the most urgent policy attention, as residents face compounding pressures with minimal economic buffers.

3. Inflation is the primary crisis accelerant. The average inflation rate among Tier 5 cities is 28.4%, compared to 1.4% in Tier 1 and 3.0% in Tier 2. Hyperinflation in Buenos Aires (150%), Beirut (50%), and Istanbul (47%) is eroding purchasing power faster than any other structural factor. Macroeconomic stabilization is a prerequisite for affordability improvements in these contexts.

4. Oil shock exposure is a reliable predictor of economic fragility. High oil-exposure cities average 20.4% inflation and 67.8 PPP, versus 3.4% inflation and 128.6 PPP for low-exposure cities. However, oil-producing nations are exceptions — Doha, Riyadh, and Dubai have high exposure but low inflation and strong purchasing power. The vulnerability is concentrated in oil-importing economies.

5. The purchasing power gap is extreme and widening. The 148.7-point gap between Basel (183.7) and Buenos Aires (35.0) means residents in the most empowered city have over 5x the real purchasing power of the least empowered. This gap correlates strongly with crisis tier classification and inflation rates.

Cities Requiring Urgent Attention

Priority watchlist (Tier 5, inflation > 10%): Buenos Aires (150.0%), Beirut (50.0%), Lagos (34.0%), Karachi (28.5%), Lahore (28.5%), Cairo (28.0%), Accra (18.0%), Kyiv (14.5%), Colombo (14.0%), Dhaka (10.5%). These 10 cities face the most severe confluence of low income, high inflation, and structural fragility.

Areas for Further Research

Income inequality within cities: Averages mask intra-city disparities. A detailed analysis of income distribution within high-crisis cities would better target interventions.

Temporal trends: This snapshot captures 2026 conditions. Comparing against prior years would reveal whether crises are worsening, stabilizing, or improving in specific cities.

Non-housing costs: While rent burden is the most visible pressure, healthcare, transportation, and food costs compound the affordability crisis — particularly in cities with low groceries index scores but high inflation.

Policy effectiveness: Cross-referencing crisis tiers with policy interventions already in place (rent controls, subsidies, inflation-targeting regimes) would help identify which approaches are working.
