Executive Overview

The global cost-of-living landscape in 2026 reveals a stark divergence between affluent, high-wage cities and those gripped by acute affordability crises. Among the 80 major cities analyzed, 14 cities (18%) fall into the most severe crisis tier (Tier 5), where residents face extreme rent burdens, collapsing purchasing power, or hyperinflationary conditions. At the opposite end, 7 cities occupy Tier 1 — affluent locations where high costs are offset by strong purchasing power.

Headline: The crisis is not uniformly about high prices. It is about the disconnect between prices and incomes. Zurich is the most expensive city (CoL index 118.5) and has a very high rent-to-salary ratio (70.8%), but its purchasing power index of 164.4 keeps living standards high. Cairo, by contrast, has the lowest cost-of-living index (26.0) yet still extracts 53.1% of a meager USD 165 monthly income for rent — a crisis of income inadequacy, not price levels.

Key extremes:

  • Most expensive: Zurich (118.5), Geneva (116.5), Basel (112.4)
  • Least expensive: Cairo (26.0), Lahore (26.0), Kathmandu (24.0)
  • Highest rent burden: Seattle (71.2%), Zurich (70.8%), Geneva (70.2%)
  • Lowest rent burden: Kathmandu (51.8%), Dhaka (51.4%), Lahore (51.0%)
  • Highest inflation: Buenos Aires (150.0%), Beirut (50.0%), Istanbul (47.0%)
  • Lowest inflation: Bern (1.2%), Shanghai (0.9%), Beijing (0.9%)

Affordability and Rent Burden Analysis

Housing cost as a share of income is the single most actionable metric for policymakers. Across all 80 cities, the average rent-to-salary ratio is 61.5%, meaning the typical city-center 1-bedroom apartment consumes more than half of the average net salary. In 27 cities, this ratio exceeds 65%, and in 4 cities it exceeds 70%.

Critical Threshold: A rent-to-salary ratio above 50% is widely considered severe. In this dataset, all 80 cities (100%) exceed this threshold, indicating a global affordability emergency. Even the "best" city, Lahore, requires 51.0% of average income for rent.

What Separates High-Crisis from Affluent Cities?

Affluent Cities (Tier 1):

  • Cost-of-living index averages 97.4 (above New York)
  • Purchasing power index averages 157.1 — the highest of any tier
  • Inflation is low (1.4% average)
  • Rent burden is high in absolute terms (67.4%) but offset by very high salaries
  • Examples: Zurich, Geneva, Basel, Lausanne, Bern, Doha, Riyadh

Severe Crisis Cities (Tier 5):

  • Cost-of-living index averages only 32.2 (well below New York)
  • Purchasing power index collapses to 48.0
  • Inflation averages 28.4% — 20× the affluent tier
  • Rent burden is lower in percentage terms (53.6%) but catastrophic in real terms given near-subsistence incomes
  • Examples: Beirut, Manila, Kyiv, Accra, Lagos, Colombo, Buenos Aires, Mumbai, Delhi, Dhaka, Karachi, Cairo, Lahore, Kathmandu
Key Insight: The defining feature of Tier 5 is not high prices — it is eroded purchasing power combined with high or hyperinflation. A policy focused only on rent controls in these cities would miss the larger macroeconomic instability driving the crisis.

Key Findings and Policy Implications

Critical Findings

  1. The crisis is income-driven, not price-driven, in the Global South. Cities like Cairo, Lagos, and Dhaka have cost-of-living indices below 30, yet residents spend 50–55% of tiny incomes on rent. Interventions must prioritize income support and macroeconomic stabilization over price controls.
  2. Western Europe and North America face an absolute housing shortage. Rent burdens above 65% in 27 cities — concentrated in Swiss and US cities — signal insufficient supply relative to demand. Zoning reform, social housing investment, and tenancy protections are the most effective levers.
  3. Hyperinflation is concentrated but catastrophic. 7 cities have inflation above 25% (Buenos Aires 150.0%, Beirut 50.0%, Istanbul 47.0%, Lagos 34.0%, Karachi 28.5%, Lahore 28.5%, Cairo 28.0%), but they house tens of millions of people. Currency stabilization and central bank independence are prerequisites for any sustainable affordability policy.
  4. Oil exposure amplifies risk where fiscal capacity is weak. High oil exposure combined with high inflation defines the most vulnerable cities. Targeted fuel subsidies or public transport investment can buffer the poorest households.
  5. Purchasing power is the best predictor of lived experience. The 148.7-point gap between Basel (183.7) and Beirut (35.0) in purchasing power index explains more about quality of life than the 86.4-point gap in cost-of-living index.

Areas for Further Research

  • Intra-city inequality: City-level averages mask neighborhood-level extremes. Sub-city data on rent burdens by income decile would sharpen targeting.
  • Policy intervention tracking: A longitudinal panel measuring which policies (rent control, housing vouchers, zoning reform) most effectively reduce rent-to-salary ratios.
  • Remittance dependence: Several Tier 5 cities (e.g., Manila, Dhaka, Karachi) are heavily remittance-dependent. The impact of exchange-rate shifts on real household income deserves deeper analysis.
  • Climate vulnerability intersection: Cities with high oil exposure and low fiscal capacity are often also climate-vulnerable. Integrated stress-testing of combined shocks would inform adaptive social protection.
