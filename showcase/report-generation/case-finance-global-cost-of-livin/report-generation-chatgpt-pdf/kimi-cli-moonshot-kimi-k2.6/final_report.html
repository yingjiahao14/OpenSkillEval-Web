Executive Overview

This report examines cost-of-living pressures across 80 major cities worldwide in 2026, benchmarking all indices against New York City (index = 100). The analysis reveals a deeply bifurcated global landscape: a small cluster of affluent Western European and North American cities combines high costs with exceptionally strong purchasing power, while a larger group of cities—concentrated in South Asia, Africa, and parts of Latin America—faces severe affordability crises driven by low wages, high rent burdens, and in some cases runaway inflation.

Headline: The median global cost-of-living index stands at 65.5—meaning half of the world’s major cities are at least one-third cheaper than New York. Yet the average rent-to-salary ratio is 61.5%, indicating that housing affordability is a near-universal stressor.

The dataset classifies cities into five crisis tiers. Tier 1 (Affluent – High Cost) contains 7 cities—all Swiss—where costs are above New York levels but wages and purchasing power more than compensate. Tier 5 (Severe Crisis) contains 14 cities spanning hyperinflation zones, conflict-affected areas, and low-income regions with collapsing purchasing power. The bulk of cities (32) fall into Tier 2, a heterogeneous group of high-cost cities with varying degrees of wage compensation and affordability pressure.

Figure 1. City count by crisis tier. Tier 2 dominates, but Tier 5 represents the acute crisis cohort requiring urgent policy attention.

At the extremes, Zurich (COLI 118.5) and Geneva (116.5) are the world’s most expensive cities, yet their purchasing power indices exceed 158. By contrast, Kathmandu (COLI 24.0), Cairo (26.0), and Lahore (26.0) are the cheapest in nominal terms, but their purchasing power indices sit between 42 and 46—reflecting wages that are a fraction of living costs when benchmarked globally.

Regional Cost-of-Living Comparison

Western Europe and Northern Europe lead in absolute cost levels, with median cost-of-living indices of 79.6 and 85.7 respectively. However, these regions also enjoy the highest purchasing power, with Western Europe averaging 141.2 and Northern Europe 119.4. North America follows closely, with a median COLI of 80.5 and the second-highest mean purchasing power (132.2).

Figure 2. Regional averages for cost of living, rent index, and purchasing power. Western Europe and North America show high costs but also high purchasing power; South Asia and Africa show the inverse pattern.

South Asia presents the most acute affordability challenge at the regional level. With a median COLI of just 27.5, it is the cheapest region globally—but its median purchasing power is only 51.0, and its mean inflation rate (13.1%) is the second-highest among major regions. The gap between nominal cheapness and real economic stress is stark: cities like Lahore, Karachi, and Dhaka have rent-to-salary ratios near 51–52%, but on salaries often below $400/month.

Regional Outliers

Within regions, several cities deviate dramatically from their peers:

  • Singapore (Southeast Asia, COLI 87.7) is a massive outlier: its cost of living is more than double the regional median (44.0), driven by world-class rent levels (rent index 73.1) and a strong currency.
  • Tel Aviv (Middle East, COLI 91.4) departs sharply from a regional median of 56.5, reflecting war-zone premiums and high wages in the tech sector.
  • Beirut and Amman (Middle East) sit well below the regional cost median, but Beirut’s inflation rate (50.0%) and collapsing purchasing power (35.0) place it in Tier 5—an outlier of crisis within an already volatile region.
  • Hong Kong (East Asia, COLI 75.2) is the region’s most expensive city, 15.2 points above the regional median, driven by extreme land scarcity and rent pressure.
Figure 3. Cities with the largest positive or negative deviation from their regional median cost of living. Positive values (red) indicate cities far more expensive than their region; negative values (blue) indicate cities cheaper than regional norms.

Latin America deserves special mention: its mean inflation rate is 34.2%, driven almost entirely by Buenos Aires (150.0%), which single-handedly distorts the regional average. Excluding Buenos Aires, Latin America’s mean inflation drops to approximately 5.5%.

Affordability and Rent Burden Analysis

Housing affordability—measured by the rent-to-salary ratio—is the single most actionable metric for policymakers. Globally, the average city-center 1-bedroom rent consumes 61.5% of the average monthly net salary. No city in the dataset falls below 50%, confirming that rent burden is a universal feature of urban life in 2026.

Critical threshold: Ratios above 65% are common in high-cost cities (Zurich 70.8%, Geneva 70.2%, Seattle 71.2%). Ratios above 68% appear in 9 cities, all in Tier 1 or Tier 2. In low-income cities, ratios cluster between 51% and 58%—lower in percentage terms, but far more damaging in real terms because absolute residual income is tiny.

Structural Drivers of Rent Burden

Two structural patterns emerge:

  • High-cost, high-wage cities (e.g., Zurich, San Francisco, Seattle): Rent is high in absolute terms, but salaries are high enough that residual income remains substantial. The policy challenge here is middle-class affordability and talent retention.
  • Low-cost, low-wage cities (e.g., Lahore, Dhaka, Cairo): Nominal rents are low, but wages are lower still. The policy challenge is absolute poverty, informal housing, and lack of social safety nets.

Crisis Tier Profiling

The five crisis tiers reveal distinct economic archetypes. Understanding what separates Tier 1 from Tier 5 is essential for targeting interventions.

Figure 6. Average indicators by crisis tier. Tier 1 shows high costs but very high purchasing power and low inflation. Tier 5 shows low nominal costs but collapsing purchasing power and extreme inflation.

Tier 1: Affluent – High Cost (7 cities)

All five Swiss cities (Zurich, Geneva, Basel, Lausanne, Bern) plus Dubai and Taipei fall here. Mean COLI is 113.8, but mean purchasing power is 168.9 and mean inflation is only 1.2%. These cities are expensive but prosperous. Rent burden is high (69.5% average) yet manageable due to salaries averaging $7,017.

Tier 2: High Cost – Mixed Compensation (32 cities)

This is the largest and most diverse tier. It includes global benchmarks (New York), high-wage tech hubs (San Francisco, Seattle), high-cost European capitals (London, Amsterdam), and cities with manageable costs but strong PPP (Copenhagen, Oslo). Mean COLI is 80.5, mean purchasing power 126.9, and rent burden averages 67.0%. The heterogeneity means policy prescriptions must be city-specific.

Tier 3: Moderate Cost – Transition Stress (15 cities)

Tier 3 cities span emerging markets and slowing economies. Mean COLI drops to 58.0, but purchasing power falls to 95.0 and inflation averages 2.8%. Notable cities include Kuala Lumpur, Bangkok, Mexico City, and Johannesburg. These cities are at an inflection point: costs are rising faster than wages, and housing stress is intensifying.

Tier 4: Low Income – Structural Vulnerability (12 cities)

Mean COLI is 45.0, but purchasing power is only 68.5 and salaries average $1,000/month. Cities like Nairobi, Accra, and St Petersburg face currency weakness, sanctions exposure, or commodity dependence. Inflation is moderate on average (9.5%) but highly variable.

Tier 5: Severe Crisis (14 cities)

The acute cohort. Mean COLI is just 32.0, but purchasing power collapses to 54.8 and inflation averages 5.9%—a figure masked by extreme outliers (Buenos Aires 150%, Beirut 50%). Salaries average only $537/month. These are the cities requiring urgent, multi-lateral policy attention:

Key Findings and Policy Implications

Key Findings

  1. The crisis is not evenly distributed. Fourteen cities (Tier 5) face severe affordability crises, but they are concentrated in South Asia, Africa, and conflict zones. Another 12 cities (Tier 4) are structurally vulnerable.
  2. Rent burden is universal but qualitatively different. Every city in the dataset exceeds the 50% threshold. In high-wage cities, the challenge is quality of life and savings; in low-wage cities, it is survival.
  3. Purchasing power is the great divider. The gap between the highest (Basel, 183.7) and lowest (Beirut, 35.0) purchasing power indices is 148.7 points—nearly a 5:1 ratio. Cost of living alone is a poor proxy for economic stress.
  4. Oil shock exposure amplifies inflation risk. High-exposure cities average 20.4% inflation. For import-dependent economies, energy transition and strategic reserves are cost-of-living policies.
  5. Regional outliers need tailored responses. Singapore, Tel Aviv, and Hong Kong face unique cost pressures that regional averages obscure. One-size-fits-all regional policy will fail these cities.

Cities and Regions Requiring Urgent Attention

Tier 5 – Immediate Intervention: Buenos Aires, Beirut, Karachi, Lahore, Cairo, Lagos, Dhaka, Kathmandu, Colombo, Accra, Kyiv, and others. These cities require coordinated support spanning macroeconomic stabilization, social protection, and humanitarian assistance.
Tier 4 – Structural Support: Nairobi, St Petersburg, Hanoi, Jakarta, and similar cities need investment in wage growth, currency stability, and affordable housing finance.

Policy Implications

  • Housing policy must be income-sensitive. Rent control and social housing are critical in low-income, high-density cities. In high-wage cities, zoning reform and supply expansion are more effective.
  • Inflation targeting is a cost-of-living policy. Central banks in high-crisis cities should prioritize currency stability and food-price monitoring.
  • Energy security reduces living-cost volatility. Diversifying energy sources and building strategic reserves can buffer oil-shock exposure.
  • Purchasing power data should complement cost indices. Policymakers should report affordability as a ratio of cost to income, not cost alone.
  • Cross-border coordination is essential for outliers. Cities like Singapore and Hong Kong, which are global financial hubs with local housing shortages, need supra-national policy frameworks for capital flows and land use.

Areas for Further Research

  • Longitudinal tracking of rent-to-salary ratios to identify tipping points into crisis tiers.
  • Disaggregated data by income decile within cities—averages mask extreme inequality.
  • Impact of remittance flows on purchasing power in South Asian and African cities.
  • Effectiveness of rent-control policies across different crisis tiers.
