1Executive Overview

This report examines cost-of-living pressures across 80 major cities worldwide in 2026, using New York City as the baseline index (100). The analysis reveals a sharply bifurcated global landscape: a cluster of affluent, high-cost cities in Western and Northern Europe alongside North American hubs, and a long tail of cities in South Asia, Africa, and parts of Latin America facing severe affordability crises driven by low wages, high rent burdens, and— in some cases—extreme inflation.

The median Cost of Living Index across all cities is 65.5, but the spread is enormous: from Zurich at 118.5 to Kathmandu at 24.0. More critically, the average rent-to-salary ratio stands at 61.5%, indicating that housing alone consumes well over half of typical net income in many cities. Fourteen cities fall into the most severe crisis tier (Tier 5), where structural affordability breakdowns, hyperinflation, or conflict dynamics are present.

Headline: Housing affordability—not just absolute cost—is the defining fault line of the 2026 cost-of-living crisis.

3Affordability and Rent Burden Analysis

Housing affordability is the single most actionable metric for policymakers. The dataset shows that rent burden is only weakly correlated with absolute cost: some low-COL cities suffer extreme rent-to-salary ratios because salaries are even lower. A threshold of 50% is widely used as a severe-burden marker; 35 cities (44%) exceed this level.

The ten cities with the highest rent burdens are not exclusively high-cost hubs. While Zurich (70.8%) and Geneva (70.2%) appear at the top due to sky-high rents, they are accompanied by lower-COL cities such as Delhi (55.6%) and Dhaka (51.4%), where meager salaries make even modest rents unaffordable. This duality—high-cost/high-wage stress versus low-cost/low-wage trap—must shape differentiated policy responses.

Tier 1 (Affluent / Low Cost – High Wages): Characterized by very high purchasing power (mean 158.4) and elevated but manageable rent burdens (~69%). These cities are expensive in absolute terms but not in crisis.

Tier 2 (High Cost – Manageable / High Wages): The largest group. Costs are above median, yet strong wages and institutions buffer residents. Average inflation is moderate (~2.9%).

Tier 3 (Moderate Cost – Mixed Stress): A transitional band where rent burdens remain high (~61%) but purchasing power begins to weaken. Cities here are at risk of sliding into higher tiers if wages stagnate.

Tier 4 (Low Income – Cost Rising / Sanctions Impact): Purchasing power collapses to ~70.6 and inflation spikes to ~11.4%. These cities face external shocks (sanctions, commodity prices) or rapid unplanned growth.

Tier 5 (Severe / Hyperinflation / War-Driven Crisis): The acute crisis group. Average inflation is 39.1%, purchasing power is just 51.9, and rent burdens are compounded by currency collapse or conflict. These 14 cities should be the top priority for emergency economic assistance and targeted housing programs.

5Inflation and Oil Shock Exposure

Annual inflation and oil-price vulnerability are key transmission channels for external shocks into local cost-of-living stress. The global mean inflation rate is 8.3%, but the distribution is heavily skewed: a small set of cities experiences hyperinflation while most advanced economies remain below 5%.

Buenos Aires (150%), Istanbul (47%), Beirut (50%), and Lagos (34%) headline a group of cities where inflation alone is eroding real wages faster than policy can respond. In these contexts, nominal rent increases— even modest ones—trigger severe affordability shocks.

Oil Shock Exposure

Oil shock exposure captures vulnerability to fuel-price spikes. Cities rated "High" exposure show slightly lower average rent burdens but significantly higher inflation volatility, often because they are net importers or have subsidized regimes that are fiscally unsustainable.

6Key Findings and Policy Implications

Key Findings

  • Affordability, not cost, is the crisis. The correlation between absolute Cost of Living Index and rent burden is weak (r ≈ 0.25). Policymakers should target rent-to-salary ratios rather than headline price levels.
  • 14 cities are in Tier 5 acute crisis. They span South Asia (Delhi, Mumbai, Dhaka, Karachi, Lahore, Kathmandu), Africa (Lagos, Accra, Cairo), the Middle East (Beirut), Eastern Europe (Kyiv), and Latin America (Buenos Aires). These cities need urgent, coordinated support.
  • Inflation is a force multiplier. Cities with annual inflation above 10% see accelerating rent burdens even when nominal rents are stable. Indexation of wages and rents, where politically feasible, should be prioritized.
  • Oil exposure amplifies volatility. High-exposure cities average double the inflation of low-exposure peers. Diversifying energy sources and strengthening public-transport networks can buffer future shocks.
  • Regional outliers matter. Hong Kong, Vancouver, and Sydney punch above their regional averages on rent burden. Within-region targeting can be more efficient than broad continental programs.

Policy Implications

  • Housing supply & rent stabilization: Expand social housing and tenancy protections in cities where rent-to-salary ratios exceed 50%. In high-wage cities, focus on zoning reform; in low-wage cities, focus on direct rental subsidies.
  • Income support & wage policy: In Tier 5 cities, minimum-wage adjustments and cash-transfer programs are essential to prevent homelessness and food insecurity.
  • Macro-stabilization: For hyperinflation cities (Buenos Aires, Istanbul, Beirut, Lagos, Cairo, Karachi, Lahore), monetary and fiscal stabilization is a prerequisite for any sustainable affordability policy.
  • Energy resilience: Cities with High oil-shock exposure should accelerate investment in public transit electrification and renewable baseload to reduce import dependence.
  • Data & monitoring: Establish quarterly rent-burden dashboards at the city level, disaggregated by income decile, to detect emerging stress before it reaches crisis tiers.
Next steps: Prioritize the 14 Tier 5 cities for rapid diagnostic missions. Within Tier 2–4, focus on cities with rising inflation and High oil exposure as the next wave of potential crises.
