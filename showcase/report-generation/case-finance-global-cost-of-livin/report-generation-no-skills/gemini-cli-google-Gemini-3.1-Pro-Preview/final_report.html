Executive Overview

In 2026, the global urban economic landscape is characterized by severe affordability pressures, exacerbated by inflation and high rent burdens. Across the 80 major cities analyzed, the median cost of living stands at 65.5 (relative to New York = 100). The typical city resident now spends 61.5% of their average net salary on rent, underscoring a pervasive housing affordability crisis.

While cities like Zurich top the indices for nominal costs, the true measure of economic stress lies in the gap between local purchasing power and these essential costs. A stark divide exists between affluent, high-cost havens and regions battling acute economic crisis, highlighted by a purchasing power gap of 148.7 points globally. 14 cities are currently classified within the most severe crisis tier, demanding urgent policy intervention.

Key Findings and Policy Implications

  • The Housing Affordability Crisis is Systemic: With an average global rent burden of 61.5%, housing costs have structurally decoupled from local wages in many urban centers. Policy intervention must prioritize affordable housing supply, rent stabilization, and wage-growth initiatives.
  • Severe Stress is Concentrated: The 14 cities in the highest crisis tier require immediate economic relief. These areas suffer from a toxic combination of high inflation, high structural costs, and low purchasing power.
  • Purchasing Power Inequality: Nominal cost of living is less relevant than the local purchasing power gap. High-cost cities (like those in Switzerland) remain livable due to corresponding high incomes, while mid-cost cities in developing regions are entering crisis due to stagnant wages.
  • Vulnerability to External Shocks: Cities with high oil shock exposure face compounding inflationary pressures. Long-term urban planning should accelerate transitions to sustainable, less volatile energy and transit infrastructure to protect consumer purchasing power.
