1. Executive Overview

The global cost-of-living landscape in 2026: headline statistics, crisis distribution, and the most and least affordable cities.

The 2026 Global Cost of Living Crisis Index covers 80 major cities across 16 regions. The data reveals a deeply bifurcated global landscape: Swiss cities dominate the top of the cost index (Zurich at 118.5, Geneva at 116.5), while South Asian cities anchor the bottom (Kathmandu at 24.0, Lahore and Cairo at 26.0). The median cost-of-living index of 65.5 — roughly two-thirds of New York's benchmark — masks extreme divergence at both ends.


The crisis tier distribution tells a more urgent story. Of 80 cities, 14 are classified at Tier 5 (the most severe crisis level), spanning hyperinflation economies (Buenos Aires, Lagos, Cairo), conflict zones (Beirut, Kyiv), and severe affordability crises across South Asia (Delhi, Dhaka, Karachi, Lahore, Mumbai). Another 12 cities sit at Tier 4, characterized by low incomes and rising costs. Together, these 26 cities — nearly one-third of the dataset — face acute economic stress requiring policy attention.


The global average rent-to-salary ratio of 61.5% signals systemic housing unaffordability. Critically, this burden is not confined to low-income cities: Seattle (71.2%), Zurich (70.8%), and Geneva (70.2%) top the rent burden rankings, showing that even high-wage cities impose severe housing costs relative to incomes. The purchasing power gap of 148.7 points (Basel at 183.7 vs. Beirut at 35.0) underscores the vast inequality in what a salary can actually buy across the globe.

Regional Outliers

Middle East: Extreme internal divergence. Doha (PPP index 145.0, salary $4,500) operates as a low-cost, high-wage city, while Beirut (PPP 35.0, salary $600) is in hyperinflationary collapse. The region's average masks a 110-point PPP gap between its best and worst performers.
North America: Rent indices far exceed other regions. With an average rent index of 65.3, North America's rental market is 60% more expensive than Western Europe's (49.6) despite comparable cost-of-living indices. New York (rent index 100.0) and San Francisco (80.5) drive this disparity.
Southeast Asia: Singapore is a regional outlier. At a CoL index of 87.7 and salary of $5,060, Singapore is far removed from the regional averages (51.3 and $1,692). Excluding Singapore, the region's average CoL drops to 42.3 and average salary to $850.
Eastern Europe: Kyiv's war-driven crisis. Most Eastern European cities cluster in Tiers 3–4 with moderate costs and improving wages. Kyiv (Tier 5, inflation 14.5%) is a dramatic exception, classified as a war-driven crisis with salaries of just $700/month.

3. Affordability and Rent Burden Analysis

Housing cost as a share of income — the single most actionable metric for policymakers.

Critical finding: The global average rent-to-salary ratio is 61.5%, far above the 30% threshold generally considered affordable. All 80 cities in the dataset exceed this threshold, indicating a systemic global housing affordability crisis. Even the lowest ratio (Lahore, 51.0%) is well above the affordability benchmark.

Key Affordability Insights

High-wage cities are not immune. Seattle (71.2%), Zurich (70.8%), and Geneva (70.2%) lead rent burden rankings despite average salaries above $6,200/month. In these cities, rents scale with — or outpace — wages, keeping the burden structurally high.
Low-income cities face a different crisis. While cities like Cairo (53.1%) and Lahore (51.0%) show lower rent-to-salary ratios, their absolute salary levels ($165 and $320/month respectively) mean that even "moderate" rent burdens leave almost nothing for food, healthcare, and transportation.
Urban-suburban rent gap. The average gap between city-center and outside-center rents is $400/month across the dataset. In New York ($1,300 gap) and San Francisco ($868 gap), moving outside the center offers significant relief. In many South Asian cities, the gap is under $100, offering minimal savings.

Tier Profiles

Tier 1 — Affluent, High Cost (7 cities): Five Swiss cities plus Doha and Riyadh. Characterized by the highest salaries ($6,055 avg), strongest purchasing power (157.1), and the lowest inflation (1.4%). These cities are expensive but economically resilient. The inclusion of Gulf oil-rich cities alongside Swiss financial centers highlights how resource wealth and financial sector dominance produce similar cost profiles.
Tier 2 — High Cost, Manageable (32 cities): The largest tier, spanning major Western cities from New York to Sydney. Salaries average $4,418 with moderate inflation (3.0%). Most cities here have functional labor markets that partially offset high costs, though housing remains a significant burden (66.1% rent ratio).
Tier 3 — Moderate Cost, Growing Pressure (15 cities): A transitional tier including Miami, Tokyo, Seoul, and several Eastern European cities. Salaries drop sharply to $1,943 and purchasing power falls below New York's baseline. These cities face emerging affordability challenges as costs rise faster than wages.
Tier 4 — Low Income, Cost Rising (12 cities): Bangkok, Jakarta, Johannesburg, and others where average salaries of $829/month intersect with rising costs. Inflation averages 8.9%, eroding already thin margins. Istanbul (47% inflation) is the standout within this tier.
Tier 5 — Severe Crisis (14 cities): The most urgent tier. Average inflation of 28.4% — driven by Buenos Aires (150%), Beirut (50%), and Lagos (34%) — combines with salaries averaging just $455/month. These cities face compounding crises: conflict (Kyiv, Beirut), hyperinflation (Buenos Aires, Cairo, Lagos), or structural poverty (Dhaka, Karachi, Kathmandu). Policy intervention is most urgently needed here.

Analysis

Oil exposure and inflation are strongly linked. Cities with high oil shock exposure average 20.4% inflation, compared to 3.4% for low and medium exposure cities. All 10 highest-inflation cities are classified as having high oil shock exposure — a clear signal that energy dependency amplifies price instability.
Inverse relationship between inflation and PPP. High-inflation cities average a purchasing power index of just 48.0 (Tier 5), compared to 157.1 for low-inflation Tier 1 cities. Rapid price increases erode real wages, creating a compounding affordability crisis — as prices rise, purchasing power falls, pushing more households into economic distress.
Oil-rich ≠ oil-exposed. Gulf cities (Doha, Riyadh, Dubai) are classified as high oil shock exposure but benefit from being oil exporters with low inflation (1.8–3.1%). The "exposure" measure captures structural dependency, but the direction of that exposure — exporter vs. importer — produces opposite outcomes. Importing cities like Istanbul, Lagos, and Cairo bear the brunt.

6. Key Findings and Policy Implications

Synthesis of the analysis into actionable findings for policy prioritization.

Key Findings

  1. Housing affordability is a universal crisis. Every city in the dataset has a rent-to-salary ratio above 50%. The global average of 61.5% means that, on average, well over half of a worker's income goes to rent. This transcends income levels and regions.
  2. Tier 5 cities require immediate intervention. The 14 Tier 5 cities combine extremely low incomes ($455/month avg), hyperinflation (28.4% avg), and in several cases active conflict. These cities are not experiencing a cost-of-living challenge — they are in economic crisis.
  3. Inflation is the great accelerant. Cities with inflation above 10% universally show deteriorating purchasing power and worsening crisis tiers. The top three inflation cities (Buenos Aires 150%, Beirut 50%, Istanbul 47%) represent three distinct crisis archetypes: monetary policy failure, conflict-driven collapse, and currency crisis.
  4. High-wage cities face structural rent pressure. Affluent cities (Tiers 1–2) are not insulated from the crisis. Their rent-to-salary ratios (66–67%) are actually higher than Tier 5 cities (53.6%), driven by property markets that absorb wage gains. Policy here should focus on housing supply and rent regulation.
  5. Regional averages mask dangerous intra-regional divergence. The Middle East spans from Doha (PPP 145, Tier 1) to Beirut (PPP 35, Tier 5). Southeast Asia spans from Singapore ($5,060 salary) to Manila ($500). Regional policy must account for this heterogeneity.

Areas for Further Research

  • Wage growth trajectories: This dataset captures a single year. Longitudinal analysis of wage growth vs. cost growth would reveal whether affordability gaps are widening or narrowing in each tier.
  • Housing supply elasticity: Understanding why rent-to-salary ratios remain above 50% even in low-cost cities requires analysis of housing supply constraints, land-use regulations, and construction costs.
  • Oil shock transmission: The strong correlation between oil exposure and inflation warrants deeper causal analysis — particularly examining how energy import dependency translates into broader consumer price inflation.
  • Sub-national variation: This dataset covers cities; extending the analysis to secondary cities and rural areas within the same countries would reveal whether national-level policies are effectively moderating local pressures.
