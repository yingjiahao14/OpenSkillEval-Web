Critical Finding: The global average rent-to-salary ratio stands at 62.0% — far above the internationally recommended affordability threshold of 30%. Even in high-wage cities, rent burdens remain crushing: Seattle residents spend 71.2% of net income on a city-center apartment. In cities like Beirut, Manila, and Mumbai, low absolute rents mask severe stress when measured against near-subsistence wages.

Key Findings and Policy Implications

1. Universal Rent Burden Crisis

With a global average rent-to-salary ratio of 62.0% — more than double the 30% affordability benchmark — housing unaffordability is not confined to expensive cities. Even cities like Manila (55.1%) and Dhaka (51.4%) exhibit severe rent burden despite low absolute costs, because local wages are proportionally lower. Policy priority: universal rent subsidy frameworks and social housing investment in both high- and low-income city contexts.

2. Swiss Anomaly: High Cost, High Wages

All five Swiss cities (Zurich, Geneva, Basel, Lausanne, Bern) rank #1–5 globally on the COL index, yet maintain purchasing power indices of 163–184, far exceeding New York's benchmark of 100. This reflects a structural high-wage economy where productivity compensates for cost. Policy implication: COL indices alone are insufficient — purchasing power parity must accompany any comparative affordability assessment.

3. Hyperinflation Zones Require Emergency Intervention

Buenos Aires (150%), Beirut (50%), Istanbul (47%), and Lagos (34%) represent cities where monetary instability has fundamentally broken the cost-of-living framework. In these contexts, nominal salary figures become meaningless within months. Policy priority: IMF stabilization programs, currency reform, and targeted social transfers for the most vulnerable residents.

4. South Asia: Low Cost Does Not Mean Affordable

Cities in South Asia (average COL of 28.9) have the lowest nominal costs globally, yet their average PPP of 52.5 — combined with monthly salaries under $700 in most cities — indicates severe structural unaffordability. Delhi, Dhaka, Kathmandu, and Karachi all rank Tier 5. Policy priority: wage floor legislation, expanded access to credit, and targeted food security programs.

5. Oil Shock Exposure Amplifies Existing Vulnerabilities

24 cities (~30% of the dataset) face high oil shock exposure. In resource-rich Gulf states, cheap fuel offsets living costs; in import-dependent economies, oil price volatility directly transmits to food, transport, and utility costs. The 14 Tier-5 crisis cities have an average oil shock exposure of "High" or "Medium," suggesting that commodity price policy is inseparable from urban affordability management.

6. The Purchasing Power Gap Demands Policy Attention

The 148.7-point gap between Basel's PPP (183.7) and Beirut's (35.0) represents the widest documented divergence in effective purchasing power across the surveyed cities. This gap is not simply a function of wealth — it reflects policy failures in monetary management, labor market regulation, and housing market governance. International development frameworks should target closing this gap through coordinated macroeconomic and urban policy interventions.
