Global Cost of Living Crisis Index 2026

A comparative analysis of economic stress across 80 major cities and regions

Executive Overview

In 2026, the global cost-of-living landscape is sharply bifurcated. The median Cost of Living Index across 80 major cities stands at 65.5 (relative to New York = 100), but the dispersion is extreme: Swiss cities cluster above 110, while South Asian and African cities fall below 30. Housing affordability is the most universal pressure point—the average rent-to-salary ratio is 61.5%, with several cities exceeding 70%.

Fourteen cities sit in the most severe crisis tier (Tier 5), spanning conflict zones, hyperinflation economies, and severe affordability crises. The Purchasing Power Gap of 148.7 index points underscores the vast inequality in real living standards: Basel records the highest purchasing power (183.7), while Beirut bottoms out at 35.0.

Headline for policymakers: Crisis is not confined to high-cost cities. Low nominal costs in emerging markets often mask severe affordability stress because salaries are even lower. Rent burden and inflation are the most actionable levers for intervention.

Affordability and Rent Burden Analysis

Housing affordability is the single most actionable metric for policymakers. The dataset shows a global mean rent-to-salary ratio of 61.5%, but the distribution is wide: from 51.0% in Lahore to 71.2% in Seattle. Critically, a high ratio can arise from two very different dynamics—high rents (Seattle, Zurich) or very low salaries (Lahore, Dhaka)—and the policy response must differ accordingly.

Key Findings and Policy Implications

Findings

  1. Housing is the universal pinch point. Every city in the dataset exceeds a 50% rent-to-salary ratio. The policy lever is not whether to act, but how: supply expansion in high-cost cities (Seattle, Zurich, Boston) versus income support and rent control in low-wage cities (Dhaka, Karachi, Cairo).
  2. Crisis severity does not follow cost alone. Tier 5 cities have lower average cost indices (32.2) than Tier 1 (97.4), but their purchasing power is less than one-third (48.0 vs 157.1). Affordability is a ratio, not an absolute.
  3. Inflation and oil exposure are tightly coupled. High oil shock exposure cities average 20.4% inflation versus 3.4% for Low exposure. Energy policy is inflation policy in these contexts.
  4. Regional averages hide local emergencies. Latin America’s mean inflation (34.2%) is driven by Buenos Aires; the Middle East’s mean (10.7%) by Beirut and Istanbul. Targeted city-level programs may outperform broad regional schemes.
  5. Fourteen cities are in acute crisis (Tier 5). They span South Asia, Africa, Eastern Europe, and Latin America. Common threads: inflation >10% (except Manila and Mumbai), salaries <$700, and purchasing power <60.

Policy Implications

  • Urgent attention: Tier 5 cities—especially Beirut, Buenos Aires, Lagos, Cairo, Karachi, and Lahore—require coordinated macro-stabilization, social protection, and humanitarian support. Inflation targeting and currency stabilization are prerequisites for any durable affordability improvement.
  • Housing supply: In Tier 1 and Tier 2 cities with rent burdens >68% (Seattle, Boston, San Francisco, Zurich), zoning reform, social housing investment, and tenancy protections should be prioritized.
  • Energy resilience: For High oil shock exposure cities, diversifying energy sources and building strategic reserves can dampen inflation volatility.
  • Data gaps: Further research should track informal-sector incomes (underrepresented in official salary data), subsidy pass-through rates, and the distributional impact of rent burdens within cities.
Bottom line: The cost-of-living crisis is not a single phenomenon. It is at least three distinct crises—high-cost/high-wage compression, low-wage/high-burden poverty traps, and hyperinflationary collapse—each requiring a tailored policy architecture. This index provides the map; policymakers must now choose the routes.
