1. Executive Overview

The 2026 Global Cost of Living Crisis Index reveals a starkly divided world. Across 80 major cities, cost-of-living indices range from 118.5 (Zurich) to 24.0 (Kathmandu) — a nearly five-fold gap. The median index of 65.5 sits well below the New York baseline, indicating that most cities are cheaper in absolute terms — but cheaper does not mean affordable.

The dataset classifies cities into five crisis tiers. 14 cities (18%) fall into Tier 5 — the most severe category — characterized by hyperinflation, conflict, or extreme affordability gaps. Meanwhile, 7 cities in Tier 1 represent affluent, high-cost centers with strong purchasing power.

The average rent-to-salary ratio across all cities is 61.5%, and 80 of 80 cities exceed the 50% severe-burden threshold. The purchasing power gap of 148.7 points — from Basel to Beirut — underscores the vast inequality in economic capacity globally.

3. Affordability and Rent Burden Analysis

Rent burden — the share of income consumed by city-center housing — is the single most actionable affordability metric. Across the dataset, every city exceeds 50% rent-to-salary ratio, indicating a global housing affordability crisis. The highest burden is in Seattle at 71.2%, while even the lowest (Lahore) stands at 51.0%.

Critical Finding: All 80 cities in this dataset exceed the 50% rent-to-salary threshold, meaning city-center one-bedroom rent consumes more than half of the average net salary everywhere. The traditional 30% affordability benchmark is unmet in every city analyzed.

6. Key Findings and Policy Implications

Key Findings

  1. Universal rent burden: All 80 cities exceed 50% rent-to-salary ratios. Housing affordability is a global crisis, not a regional one.
  2. Income does not resolve affordability: Swiss cities earn $6,500–$8,000/month yet face ~70% rent ratios. High wages attract high rents.
  3. Tier 5 cities face compounding crises: 14 cities combine low income (avg $455/month), high inflation (28.4%), and external shocks.
  4. Inflation inequality is extreme: A 149-point spread (0.9% to 150.0%) between the most and least stable economies.
  5. Oil shock exposure amplifies vulnerability: High-exposure cities average 20.4% inflation vs 3.4% for Low-exposure cities.
  6. Regional outliers demand attention: Cities like New York (rent index 100 vs North America avg) and Buenos Aires (150% inflation in Latin America) diverge sharply from regional norms.

Policy Implications

Urgent Intervention Needed
  • Beirut, Kyiv, Lagos, Cairo, Buenos Aires: Tier 5 cities with hyperinflation or conflict — require humanitarian-level economic support.
  • Karachi, Lahore, Dhaka: South Asian megacities with severe inflation (>10%) and minimal purchasing power — structural reform needed.
Areas for Further Research
  • Why do all cities exceed 50% rent burden? Investigate whether this reflects data methodology or a genuine global pattern.
  • Longitudinal tracking: How have these indices shifted since 2024?
  • Sub-city analysis: Intra-urban inequality (city center vs periphery rent gaps).
