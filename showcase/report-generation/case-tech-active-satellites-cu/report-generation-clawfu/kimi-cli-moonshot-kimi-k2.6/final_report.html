Catalog Overview & Key Figures

The CelesTrak / NORAD catalog contains 14,875 active satellites as of early 2026. Low Earth Orbit (LEO) dominates the population, accounting for 86.5% of all spacecraft. The median orbital inclination is 53.2°, reflecting the large population of Starlink and OneWeb shells clustered in the 50°–55° band.

Nearly all satellites (90.5%) were launched in 2020 or later, a direct consequence of the Starlink build-out and competing broadband constellations. Only a handful of legacy assets (e.g., early calibration spheres, some Molniya-class comsats, and long-lived geostationary platforms) remain active from pre-2000 launches.

Orbital Regime Classification

Satellites were classified using MEAN_MOTION thresholds applied to the catalog data:

  • LEO — Mean motion > 14 rev/day (~160–2,000 km altitude)
  • GEO — Mean motion between 0.9 and 1.1 rev/day (~35,786 km)
  • MEO — Mean motion between 1.1 and 14 rev/day (navigation constellations, O3b, etc.)
  • HEO — Eccentricity > 0.5 (highly elliptical orbits such as Molniya)

Launch Year Trends

The growth curve of currently active satellites is the headline story of this catalog. After decades of gradual accumulation, the active fleet exploded from ~1,400 in 2019 to 14,875 in early 2026 — a 10× increase in six years.

2025 alone added 4,329 active satellites, and 2026 (partial year) has already contributed 962. The inflection point is unmistakable: 2020 marks the start of the mega-constellation era, with annual additions rising from ~500 to >4,000 within five years.

Orbital Parameter Deep Dive

Beyond population counts, the catalog’s orbital parameters reveal the engineering choices behind today’s dominant missions.

Inclination

The median inclination of 53.2° sits squarely in the Starlink / OneWeb shell band (≈53° and ≈87° respectively). The bimodal distribution below shows a massive 50°–70° peak (prograde LEO shells) and a secondary 90°–110° peak (polar / sun-synchronous remote-sensing orbits). Only a handful of satellites exceed 110° (retrograde or highly elliptical).

Eccentricity

99.0% of active satellites have eccentricity ≤ 0.01, indicating near-circular orbits optimized for stable coverage and minimal station-keeping. The 32 outliers with eccentricity > 0.5 are classic HEO assets (Molniya-style communication relays and some scientific missions).

Mean Motion

Mean motion confirms the LEO concentration: 12,860 satellites (86.5%) orbit faster than 14 rev/day. The 596 GEO assets cluster tightly around 1.0 rev/day, while MEO navigation satellites span 1–5 rev/day.

Key Takeaways & Outlook

  • LEO is now the default orbital regime. With 86.5% of active satellites in LEO, spectrum coordination and debris-mitigation rules must prioritize altitudes below 2,000 km.
  • Starlink defines the statistical center of gravity. At 10,083 spacecraft, SpaceX operates more than two-thirds of all active satellites. Any policy framework that treats all operators symmetrically will under-weight Starlink’s impact on conjunction risk, RF interference, and re-entry traffic.
  • Launch cadence has entered a new regime. The jump from ~150 satellites/year (2019) to >4,000/year (2025) is not a one-time spike; it reflects reusable-rocket economics and regulatory approval of large constellations. Expect the 2026 total to exceed 20,000 if current licensing trajectories hold.
  • Orbital congestion is a LEO-specific problem. GEO and MEO populations are stable or slowly growing; the congestion risk is therefore concentrated in a narrow band of mean motion (>14 rev/day) and inclination (50°–55° and 85°–98°).
  • Data-driven monitoring is essential. Because the catalog changes weekly, analysts should automate ingestion of CelesTrak updates to track constellation fill rates, re-orbiting compliance, and emerging operators (e.g., Amazon Kuiper, which already shows 216 active satellites in this snapshot).
