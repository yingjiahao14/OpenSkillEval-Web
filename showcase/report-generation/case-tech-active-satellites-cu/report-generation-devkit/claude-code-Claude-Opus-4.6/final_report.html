2. Launch Year Trends

Launch year is extracted from the first four characters of each satellite's OBJECT_ID international designator. This represents when the still-active satellite was launched — not the total historical launch count, since deorbited and failed satellites are excluded from the catalog.

The growth trajectory is the defining story of the modern orbital environment. The active fleet has undergone exponential expansion since 2020, driven almost entirely by mega-constellation deployment. Over 90% of all currently active satellites were launched in 2020 or later.

The Starlink Effect

A single operator — SpaceX — accounts for 67.8% of the entire active satellite population. This level of concentration has no precedent in the history of spaceflight. Starlink's dominance skews virtually every aggregate statistic in the catalog:

  • The median inclination of the full catalog (53.2°) aligns with Starlink's primary shell.
  • The LEO share (94.6%) drops to 83.2% when Starlink is excluded.
  • The median mean motion shifts from 15.27 to 14.98 rev/day without Starlink.
  • The post-2020 launch share drops from 90.5% to 70.5% excluding Starlink.

6. Key Takeaways & Outlook

Finding 1: Unprecedented Concentration

A single operator (SpaceX/Starlink) controls 67.8% of all active satellites. This level of orbital dominance raises questions for spectrum regulators, debris-mitigation policymakers, and competition authorities. Any operational anomaly in the Starlink fleet — whether a firmware bug causing uncontrolled deorbits or a collision cascade — would affect two-thirds of all active spacecraft.

Finding 2: The Post-2020 Explosion

90.5% of all active satellites were launched in the past six years. The fleet grew from ~1,400 objects at end of 2019 to nearly 15,000 by Q1 2026 — a >10× increase in just over six years. 2025 was the single largest launch year on record (4,329 active satellites).

Finding 3: LEO Is the New Frontier

Low Earth Orbit now hosts 94.6% of the active fleet, up from an estimated ~65% a decade ago. The 550-km shell alone (mean motion ~15.28 rev/day) contains thousands of Starlink satellites. This concentration creates congestion hotspots that demand active traffic management and conjunction assessment at unprecedented scale.

Finding 4: Inclination Clustering

The inclination distribution is heavily bimodal: a dominant peak at ~53° (Starlink primary shell) and a secondary peak at ~97° (sun-synchronous orbit). Between them, the 60°–80° range is relatively sparse, representing an opportunity for future constellations seeking to minimize conjunction risk.

Finding 5: Policy Implications

  • Spectrum allocation: With 10,000+ Starlink satellites and 651 OneWeb satellites sharing LEO broadband spectrum, coordination and interference management are critical.
  • Debris mitigation: The sheer number of objects in the 500–600 km band requires robust deorbit compliance monitoring. Even a 1% failure-to-deorbit rate would leave ~100 uncontrolled objects.
  • Constellation licensing: Future mega-constellation applicants (Amazon Kuiper, Telesat Lightspeed, various Chinese proposals) will face an environment already shaped by a dominant incumbent.
  • Space situational awareness: Tracking 14,875 active objects — plus tens of thousands of debris fragments — requires continuous investment in ground-based and space-based surveillance.
