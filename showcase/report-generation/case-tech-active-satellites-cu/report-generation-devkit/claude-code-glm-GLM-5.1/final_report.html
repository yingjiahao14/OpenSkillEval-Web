2. Orbital Regime Classification

Satellites are classified using MEAN_MOTION thresholds, with eccentricity as a secondary discriminant for HEO:

  • LEO: MEAN_MOTION > 14 rev/day (altitude ~160–2,000 km)
  • MEO: 2.0 ≤ MEAN_MOTION ≤ 14 rev/day (altitude ~2,000–35,786 km)
  • GEO: 0.9 ≤ MEAN_MOTION < 2.0 & ECCENTRICITY < 0.25 (~35,786 km altitude)
  • HEO: ECCENTRICITY ≥ 0.25 (highly elliptical, e.g., Molniya)

6. Key Takeaways & Outlook

Orbital Congestion Is a LEO Problem

With 12,860 of 14,875 active satellites in low Earth orbit — and 10,083 of those belonging to a single operator — LEO is no longer a shared resource in any equitable sense. Conjunction risk, spectrum interference, and debris generation are overwhelmingly concentrated in this band. Policy frameworks for orbital slot management and de-orbit compliance must be LEO-first.

Single-Operator Concentration Creates Systemic Risk

SpaceX's Starlink constellation represents 67.8% of all active satellites. Any operational anomaly, cyber event, or regulatory action affecting this fleet would reshape the orbital environment overnight. The statistical picture of "what is in orbit" is effectively a Starlink picture — median inclination, LEO share, and recent launch trends are all artifacts of one program.

Fleet Turnover Is Historically Unprecedented

90.5% of active satellites were launched since 2020. This implies that the pre-2020 orbital population has been almost entirely displaced by new launches — partly through retirement/replacement, and partly through sheer scale of new deployments. The sustainability question is not just about numbers but about replacement cadence and end-of-life compliance at this scale.

MEO and GEO Remain Stable Niches

MEO (1,336 satellites, 9.0%) is dominated by navigation constellations (GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou) that operate in stable, well-characterized shells. GEO (661 satellites, 4.4%) hosts high-value communications and weather platforms. Neither regime shows the explosive growth seen in LEO, and both benefit from long-established coordination frameworks.

Implications for Stakeholders

  • Spectrum regulators: LEO-to-ground spectrum is effectively allocated to a handful of mega-constellation operators. New entrants face a crowded interference environment.
  • Debris policy: With ~3,500+ new LEO satellites per year, even a 1% non-compliance rate on de-orbit creates significant cumulative risk.
  • Constellation licensing: The gap between Starlink (10,083) and the next-largest constellation (OneWeb, 651) suggests that license approvals are the primary bottleneck on orbital growth, not launch capacity.
  • Space sustainability: The median satellite in orbit is now a recently launched LEO communications platform with a ~5-year design life. Rapid replacement cycles demand robust de-orbit assurance.
