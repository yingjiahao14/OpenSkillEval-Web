1. Catalog Overview & Key Figures

High-level summary of the active satellite population as captured by the CelesTrak catalog.

The catalog contains 14,875 active satellites with orbital element sets (TLEs) generated between 26 February and 30 March 2026. The population is overwhelmingly concentrated in Low Earth Orbit (94.6%), with a median orbital inclination of 53.16° — a value strongly influenced by Starlink's 53° shell, which alone accounts for 67.8% of all active spacecraft.

A striking 90.5% of the catalog (13,461 satellites) was launched in 2020 or later, underscoring the exponential growth in orbital activity over the past six years. Nearly all orbits are near-circular: 98.96% of satellites have eccentricities below 0.01.

Data Notes: All figures derive from the CelesTrak TLE dataset (14,875 records). The EPOCH field indicates element-set generation time, not launch date. Launch year is extracted from the first four digits of OBJECT_ID (international designator). ECCENTRICITY and BSTAR required string-to-float conversion.

2. Orbital Regime Classification

Satellites classified into LEO, MEO, GEO, and HEO regimes using mean-motion and eccentricity thresholds.

Classification Method (MEAN_MOTION thresholds):
LEO — Mean motion > 11.25 rev/day (orbital period < 128 min, altitude roughly < 2,000 km)
GEO — Mean motion between 0.9 and 1.1 rev/day (near-synchronous, ~35,786 km altitude)
MEO — Mean motion between 1.1 and 11.25 rev/day (intermediate altitudes)
HEO — Eccentricity > 0.25 (highly elliptical orbits, e.g., Molniya), regardless of mean motion

6. Key Takeaways & Outlook

Synthesis of findings and implications for spectrum allocation, orbital debris policy, and constellation licensing.

1. Unprecedented Growth Trajectory

The active satellite population has grown from ~1,400 pre-2020 to 14,875 today — a 10× increase in six years. With 2025 alone adding 4,329 spacecraft and 2026 on pace to match, the annual launch rate shows no signs of plateauing. Regulatory frameworks, conjunction screening capacity, and spectrum coordination processes designed for hundreds of satellites must adapt to an environment of tens of thousands.

2. Single-Operator Concentration Risk

Starlink's 10,083 satellites (67.8% of the catalog) create an unprecedented concentration of orbital assets under one operator. This has systemic implications: a single deorbit policy change, operational anomaly, or business disruption could affect two-thirds of all active spacecraft. Policy advisors should consider whether current licensing frameworks adequately address concentration-of-risk scenarios at this scale.

3. LEO Congestion Demands New Norms

With 94.6% of all satellites in LEO and strong clustering at specific inclinations (53°, 43°, 97°), the most popular orbital shells are becoming operationally dense. The 50–55° inclination band alone contains 5,324 satellites. Conjunction assessment, collision avoidance maneuver coordination, and post-mission disposal enforcement are now operational necessities rather than theoretical concerns.

4. GEO Remains Strategically Vital

Despite representing only 4.0% of satellites by count (595), the GEO belt retains outsized importance for telecommunications, meteorology, and early-warning missions. Its near-zero inclination median (0.06°) reflects tight stationkeeping. As LEO constellations grow, spectrum coordination between LEO downlinks and GEO operations will intensify — a key dimension for ITU and national regulators.

Bottom line: The orbital environment of 2026 bears little resemblance to that of 2019. The combination of exponential growth, single-operator dominance, and LEO clustering creates an environment where space sustainability policy, spectrum management, and debris mitigation are no longer forward-looking concerns — they are immediate operational requirements.
