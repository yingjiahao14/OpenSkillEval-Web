03Major Constellation & Operator Breakdown

Removing Starlink from the dataset leaves 4,792 other satellites, which is itself larger than the entire active catalog at any point before ~2020. The next-largest operators — OneWeb, Planet Labs, and the government GNSS constellations (GPS, BeiDou, Galileo, GLONASS) — each contribute only a small fraction of the fleet by comparison.
Policy Implication: Starlink's dominance means that any single-operator regulatory decision (e.g., collision-avoidance protocols, deorbit timelines, spectrum allocation) has outsized impact on the entire orbital environment. Policymakers should consider whether constellation-licensing frameworks designed for a few-dozen-satellite fleets remain appropriate at this scale.

04Launch Year Trends

The launch-year analysis reveals the single most important story in this catalog: the post-2020 explosion in satellite deployments. Of the 14,875 active satellites, 13,461 (90%) were launched from 2020 onward. Prior to 2020, the cumulative active fleet grew at a roughly linear pace over decades. The curve inflects sharply at 2020, driven by SpaceX's Starlink launches (beginning with the first operational v1.0 launch in November 2019) and, to a lesser extent, OneWeb's deployment campaign. Annual launch counts for 2022–2025 far exceed any previous year in the space age.
Key Trend: The growth curve suggests the active satellite population could double again within the next 3–5 years if current launch cadences are maintained. This has direct implications for conjunction-risk management, orbital debris mitigation, and spectrum coordination.

06Key Takeaways & Outlook

The orbital environment has entered a new era. The 2026 active-satellite catalog reveals a domain transformed by a single operator, a single orbital regime, and a five-year launch surge without historical precedent.
  1. LEO is the new center of gravity. At 86.5% of all active satellites, LEO hosts the vast majority of the operational fleet. This concentration demands updated approaches to conjunction assessment, debris mitigation, and traffic coordination — especially given the altitude overlap between mega-constellations and legacy science/defense missions.
  2. Starlink's dominance reshapes every statistical picture. With 10,083 satellites (67.8% of the fleet), Starlink alone is larger than the entire combined catalog from any prior decade. Any policy framework that does not account for single-operator concentration risks will be incomplete.
  3. The launch cadence is still accelerating. 90% of currently active satellites were launched after 2020. If current trends continue, the catalog could exceed 25,000–30,000 active objects by decade's end — most of them in a few narrow altitude shells in LEO.
  4. Orbital niches remain viable. Despite LEO dominance, GEO persists as the platform for high-throughput broadcast and weather services, while MEO hosts critical GNSS infrastructure (GPS, Galileo, BeiDou). HEO serves specialized high-latitude communications roles.
  5. Spectrum and debris policy must evolve. The density of active satellites at common altitudes and inclinations means that spectrum sharing, collision avoidance, and post-mission disposal are no longer edge cases — they are operational necessities for every constellation operator.
For Policymakers: Consider introducing altitude-band occupancy limits and aggregate collision-probability budgets for orbital shells above a certain object density. The current per-satellite safety framework does not scale to a post-Starlink environment where a single operator can deploy thousands of spacecraft in coordinated planes.
