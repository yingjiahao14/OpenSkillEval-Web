SECTION 01

Catalog Overview & Key Figures

The CelesTrak/NORAD active-satellite catalog captures 14,875 operational spacecraft as of 2026-02-26 – 2026-03-30. This snapshot encompasses the full spectrum of Earth-orbital missions — from mega-constellations to singular scientific probes — and forms the authoritative basis for all figures in this report.

Data coverage: Every metric, chart, and percentage in this report is computed directly from the 14,875-row dataset. No values have been imputed or externally sourced. Orbital classification uses MEAN_MOTION thresholds as described in Section 2.

Fleet Composition at a Glance

LEO dominates the fleet at 86.5% of all active satellites, driven overwhelmingly by Starlink. GEO hosts the traditional communications and weather satellite fleet. MEO carries navigation constellations (GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou). HEO represents a small minority of highly elliptical surveillance and communication missions.

Starlink effect: 10,083 of 14,875 active satellites (67.8%) belong to SpaceX's Starlink mega-constellation. This single operator skews every aggregate statistic — inclination, mean motion, LEO share — dramatically toward its chosen shell parameters (~53° inclination, ~15 rev/day). All subsequent analysis segments results with this context in mind.

EPOCH & Data Freshness

The EPOCH field records when each orbital element set was last updated. The full range spans 2026-02-26 – 2026-03-30, indicating that the vast majority of TLE records are current within a narrow recent window — consistent with the CelesTrak practice of refreshing elements daily for active payloads.

SECTION 02

Orbital Regime Classification

Satellites are classified into four regimes using MEAN_MOTION (orbital revolutions per day) and ECCENTRICITY thresholds derived from the TLE catalog. The thresholds applied are:

Classification thresholds: LEO = MEAN_MOTION > 14.0 rev/day · GEO = 0.9 ≤ MEAN_MOTION ≤ 1.1 rev/day · MEO = 2.0 < MEAN_MOTION ≤ 14.0 rev/day (non-GEO, non-HEO) · HEO = ECCENTRICITY > 0.25 and MEAN_MOTION ≤ 2.0 rev/day

SECTION 04

Launch Year Trends

Launch year is extracted from the first four characters of OBJECT_ID (format: YYYY-NNNXXX). This analysis counts currently active satellites by the year they were launched — representing fleet survival rather than total historical launch volume.

Post-2020 explosion: 13,461 satellites (90.5%) of the entire active fleet were launched in 2020 or later — a stunning compression of orbital history. The active fleet effectively doubled, then doubled again, within a five-year window driven almost entirely by Starlink v1/v2 deployments.

SECTION 06

Key Takeaways & Outlook

The following findings synthesize the data analysis into actionable observations for spectrum policy, orbital debris governance, and constellation licensing.

1 — LEO Crowding Is a Single-Operator Problem

86.5% of active satellites operate in LEO, and 67.8% of all satellites are Starlink alone. The conjunctions, spectrum coordination challenges, and debris mitigation requirements in the 500–600 km shell are, to an unusual degree, concentrated around a single operator's decisions. Licensing frameworks should address operator-level concentration, not just aggregate orbital-regime population limits.

2 — The Post-2020 Fleet Is Unprecedented

90.5% of the active fleet was launched after 2019. No historical orbital precedent exists for this rate of change. Deorbit planning, frequency coordination, and interference modeling all depend on stable assumptions about fleet size — assumptions that no longer hold. Regulators should build adaptive review cycles into constellation licenses rather than fixed-term grants.

3 — Inclination Clusters Signal Interference Choke Points

The extreme concentration of satellites near 53° inclination (Starlink primary shell) creates dense interference zones for ground stations operating in that latitude band. Secondary cluster near 90° (SSO) similarly concentrates RF traffic at polar latitudes. Spectrum coordination should model inclination density, not just orbital altitude.

4 — Navigation Constellation Stability

GPS, GLONASS, BeiDou, and Galileo maintain stable MEO populations. These constellations have predictable replacement cycles and stable inclinations — providing a fixed reference against which LEO dynamics should be measured. Their orbital shells remain largely uncrowded by commercial entrants, though O3b MEO broadband activity warrants monitoring.

5 — Near-Circular Orbits Confirm Active Station-Keeping

99.0% of satellites maintain near-circular orbits (ecc < 0.01), indicating active ion or chemical propulsion. This is relevant for debris planning: propulsive satellites can deorbit on command, while the 0.2% in high-eccentricity HEO orbits present longer atmospheric residence times and require case-by-case end-of-life planning.

Bottom line for policy: The 2026 orbital environment is not a gradual evolution of the 2015 environment — it is a structural discontinuity driven by a single commercial actor's mega-constellation. Any spectrum allocation, debris liability, or orbital slot governance framework that does not explicitly account for mega-constellation dynamics will be structurally inadequate within the licensing window of any satellite approved today.
