Catalog Overview & Key Figures

The CelesTrak/NORAD catalog snapshot (epoch window: Feb 26 – Mar 30, 2026) contains 14,875 active, trackable spacecraft. This figure represents objects with valid TLE elements — payloads, not debris or rocket bodies. The catalog spans six decades of spaceflight, from CALSPHERE 1 (launched 1964) to satellites deployed weeks before the snapshot date.

Orbit shapes are overwhelmingly circular: 98.96% of satellites have eccentricity below 0.01, consistent with operational station-keeping requirements. Only 0.28% occupy highly elliptical orbits (eccentricity > 0.1), a category historically used for wide-area coverage (Molniya) and now comparatively rare.

Launch Year Trends — The Acceleration Story

Launch year is extracted from the first four characters of the OBJECT_ID field (format: YYYY-NNNXXX). This reflects the year each currently-active satellite was launched — not total annual launch count, since many historical satellites have since deorbited or failed.

The headline finding: 13,461 of 14,875 active satellites (90.5%) were launched in 2020 or later. The remaining ~9.5% represents six decades of prior launches. The fleet has effectively been rebuilt from scratch within five years — almost entirely by commercial mega-constellation operators.

Key Takeaways & Outlook

The 2026 catalog presents a radically transformed orbital environment relative to any prior decade. Five structural findings merit direct policy attention:

1. Single-operator concentration risk. One commercial operator (SpaceX/Starlink) controls 67.78% of all active tracked satellites. Standard spectrum-coordination frameworks, debris-liability regimes, and conjunction-risk models were not designed for this degree of concentration. Any Starlink operational anomaly or policy change affects the majority of the cataloged fleet.
2. LEO is effectively full of one constellation. 86.45% of all active satellites are in LEO, and 83% of all LEO satellites are Starlink. The ~540–570 km altitude band around 53° inclination is the most congested orbital shell in history. Frequency coordination and conjunction screening in this band now requires bespoke treatment.
3. The 2020–2025 launch surge is structural, not cyclical. 90.5% of the active fleet launched in the last five years. The 2025 cohort (4,329 satellites) alone exceeds the entire pre-2020 fleet. New entrants (Amazon Kuiper, 210 satellites and growing) indicate this trend will not reverse without regulatory intervention.
4. GEO is stable but marginal. Only 668 satellites (4.49%) operate at GEO. This traditional broadcast/communications band faces spectrum-crowding pressure from above (Starlink V-band proposals) and below (growing MEO navigation constellations). Its share of the active fleet will continue to shrink as LEO services displace GEO broadband.
5. Near-circular orbits dominate; HEO nearly extinct. 98.96% of satellites have eccentricity below 0.01 — reflecting the commercial preference for stable, manageable circular shells. Only 11 objects qualify as HEO under the MEAN_MOTION threshold. This simplifies some debris-modeling assumptions but also concentrates risk in narrow altitude bands.

Policy implications: Orbital carrying-capacity assessments, spectrum coordination timelines, and debris-liability frameworks must be recalibrated for an environment where a single operator can launch more satellites in one year (2025: 4,329) than existed in total across all operators prior to 2017. The current trajectory points toward a 20,000-satellite active fleet within 24–36 months at observed launch rates — a density that existing international coordination instruments were not designed to govern.
