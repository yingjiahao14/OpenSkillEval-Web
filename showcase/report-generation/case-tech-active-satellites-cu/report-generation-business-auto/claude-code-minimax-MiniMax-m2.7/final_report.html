Catalog Overview & Key Figures

The CelesTrak/NORAD TLE catalog captures 14,875 active satellites as of March–April 2026. Epoch dates concentrated in March 2026 confirm data freshness. The catalog excludes debris and non-operational objects. Mean motion ranges from 0.28 rev/day (HEO) to 16.30 rev/day (lowest LEO), with 86.5% of all objects in LEO (mean motion > 14.0) and a median inclination of 53.2°.

Major Constellation & Operator Breakdown

SpaceX's Starlink constellation dominates the active satellite population, accounting for 67.8% of all active satellites. The remaining ~32% is split across commercial, civil, and government operators. This extreme concentration fundamentally skews orbital statistics toward LEO and low-inclination shells.

Starlink Shell Strategy. SpaceX operates multiple orbital shells differentiated by inclination: the 53.2° shell (~4,840 sats) covers mid-latitude global coverage; the 40° shell (~3,656 sats) adds polar coverage; and the 97.6° shell (~872 sats) provides high-latitude reinforcement. Shells at 70° (~506 sats) and 65° (~209 sats) address regional coverage gaps. This multi-shell approach makes Starlink's inclination distribution multimodal, producing the overall LEO median inclination of ~53°.

Launch Year Trends — The Great Acceleration

Of the 14,875 active satellites, 13,461 (90.5%) were launched since 2020. The 2025 active fleet alone accounts for 4,329 satellites — more than the combined total of all spacecraft launched before 2018. The growth from 498 (2020) to 4,329 (2025) represents an 8.7× increase in five years.

Policy Implication. The launch cadence has shifted from ~50–100 new active satellites/year throughout the 2000s–2010s to thousands per year, driven almost entirely by commercial LEO constellations. Regulatory frameworks, spectrum allocation, and orbital debris mitigation rules written before 2018 face a fundamentally different operational environment. If 2025's pace continues, the active LEO population could exceed 20,000 by 2030.

Inclination Modal Structure. The inclination distribution is distinctly bimodal: the peak at 50°–60° (5,399 sats, 36.3%) reflects Starlink's dominant 53.2° shell; a secondary peak at 40°–50° (3,915 sats, 26.3%) blends Starlink's 40° shell with OneWeb's ~86° polar constellation. The ~0°–10° band (584 sats) is dominated by GEO-belt and equatorial-orbit satellites. High-inclination HEO satellites (Molniya-class) appear in the 60°–70° band.

Key Takeaways & Outlook

  • 1
    Mega-constellations have fundamentally restructured Earth orbit. A single operator — SpaceX Starlink — accounts for 67.8% of all active satellites. The top five operators (Starlink, OneWeb, Planet, Cosmos, Iridium) represent ~74% of the active population. Spectrum allocation and orbital congestion policy cannot be discussed without Starlink as the central case.
  • 2
    LEO is the operational frontier. With 86.5% of active satellites in LEO, medium and high Earth orbit regimes remain relatively uncongested. The crowding challenge is almost exclusively a LEO problem — concentrated in two inclination bands (40°–60°) at altitudes between ~550 km and ~1,200 km.
  • 3
    The launch growth curve is near-vertical since 2020. From 498 active satellites launched in 2020 to 4,329 in 2025 — an 8.7× increase in five years. This rate of change outpaces the revision cycles of ITU spectrum regulations and national licensing frameworks.
  • 4
    Debris risk is increasingly a Starlink-specific concern. With 10,083 active Starlink satellites, SpaceX's own conjunction management practices effectively define the debris environment in heavily used shells. The FCC's 2022 five-year post-mission disposal rule for LEO satellites below 2,000 km is a significant step, but enforcement and international harmonization remain open questions.
  • 5
    Inclination stratification creates distinct congestion zones. Starlink's multi-shell strategy (40°, 53°, 65°, 70°, 97° shells) deliberately fills different latitudinal coverage bands — but specific inclination corridors face disproportionate conjunction density. Operators planning new constellations face a de facto zoning problem: certain shells are becoming crowded while others remain available.
