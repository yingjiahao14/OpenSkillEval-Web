Catalog Overview & Key Figures

The CelesTrak active-satellite catalog contains 14,875 operational spacecraft as of late March 2026. Orbital elements span epoch dates from February 26 to March 30, 2026, confirming the catalog is a near-real-time snapshot of the active fleet. The dataset covers everything from 1960s-era calibration spheres still transmitting data to the latest Starlink V2 Mini satellites deployed in early 2026.

Orbital Regime Classification

Classification Method: Orbital regimes are assigned using MEAN_MOTION thresholds: LEO > 14.0 rev/day (period < 103 min, altitude roughly < 2,000 km); GEO between 0.9 and 1.1 rev/day (~35,786 km); MEO between 1.1 and 14.0 rev/day; HEO assigned to objects in the MEO/other band with eccentricity > 0.25 (highly elliptical orbits such as Molniya).

LEO overwhelmingly dominates the active fleet. The 596 GEO satellites — many of them high-value communications and weather platforms — represent just 4.0% of the count but serve a disproportionate share of global communications, broadcast, and meteorological coverage. MEO hosts navigation constellations (GPS, Galileo, BeiDou, GLONASS) and broadband systems like O3b/SES.

Starlink Effect: Removing Starlink's 10,083 LEO satellites would reduce the LEO population to 2,777, dropping LEO's share from 86.5% to 57.9%. A single operator accounts for more than three-quarters of all LEO objects.

Orbital Parameter Deep Dive

Inclination Distribution

Orbital inclination reveals the preferred orbital planes. Constellation operators cluster their satellites at specific inclinations to maximize coverage geometry.

Inclination Distribution — All Satellites vs. Starlink

Three dominant inclination bands emerge:

  • 50–55° (5,324 satellites): Starlink's primary shell at 53° accounts for the vast majority. This inclination provides coverage to most populated latitudes.
  • 40–45° (3,744 satellites): Starlink's secondary V2 Mini shell, optimizing for mid-latitude capacity.
  • 95–100° (2,803 satellites): Sun-synchronous orbits (SSO), heavily used by Earth-observation platforms and some communications constellations including OneWeb (87.9°) and imaging satellites.

Key Takeaways & Outlook

1. Unprecedented Growth, Accelerating

The active satellite population has grown more than 10× since 2019 (1,414 → 14,875). Over 90% of the current fleet was launched in the last six years. If 2026 deployment continues at the 2025 pace, the catalog could surpass 18,000 active satellites by year-end.

2. Single-Operator Dominance Reshapes the Environment

Starlink's 10,083 satellites constitute 67.8% of all active objects. This concentration skews every metric — orbital regime shares, inclination distributions, mean motion profiles, and launch statistics. Regulators and space-situational-awareness providers must account for this when setting policy or evaluating collision risk.

3. LEO Congestion Is the Central Challenge

With 86.5% of active satellites in LEO and the vast majority clustered between 500–600 km altitude, conjunction rates and close-approach events will continue to rise. The 550 km shell is becoming the single most congested region in orbital history.

4. New Entrants Are Scaling Rapidly

Amazon's Kuiper (210 active), China's Hulianwang (159) and Qianfan (108) constellations are still in early deployment phases. Their planned fleet sizes (3,200+ for Kuiper; thousands for each Chinese constellation) will add substantial new populations to an already crowded LEO.

5. Policy Implications

  • Spectrum management: Mega-constellations dominate specific frequency bands. Coordination between operators in the Ku/Ka bands is increasingly complex.
  • Debris mitigation: With fleet sizes exceeding 10,000, even a low per-satellite failure rate generates meaningful debris risk. Post-mission disposal compliance is critical.
  • Space traffic management: Automated conjunction assessment and collision-avoidance maneuvering are no longer optional — they are operational necessities at current fleet scales.
  • Equitable access: Orbital slots and spectrum are finite resources. The current "first-mover" dynamic risks foreclosing access for later entrants, including developing nations.
