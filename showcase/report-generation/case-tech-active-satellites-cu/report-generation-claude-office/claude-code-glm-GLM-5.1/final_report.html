Orbital Regime Classification

Satellites are classified into orbital regimes using MEAN_MOTION thresholds derived from Kepler's third law and standard orbital mechanics conventions:

  • LEO (Low Earth Orbit): MEAN_MOTION > 11.25 rev/day (≈ 160–2,000 km altitude)
  • MEO (Medium Earth Orbit): 2.0 ≤ MEAN_MOTION ≤ 11.25 rev/day, excluding GEO band
  • GEO (Geostationary Orbit): 0.99 ≤ MEAN_MOTION ≤ 1.01 rev/day (≈ 35,786 km altitude)
  • HEO (Highly Elliptical Orbit): ECCENTRICITY > 0.1 and MEAN_MOTION < 10 rev/day (e.g., Molniya orbits)

Orbital Parameter Deep Dive

This section examines the statistical distributions of the three core orbital parameters: inclination, eccentricity, and mean motion. These distributions reveal the most populated orbital shells and highlight outlier populations.

Inclination

The inclination distribution is bimodal: a large peak near 53° (Starlink's shell) and another near 97–98° (sun-synchronous orbits for Earth observation). The median across all satellites is 53.2°.

Eccentricity

The vast majority of active satellites operate in near-circular orbits. The distribution is heavily right-skewed, with most values below 0.01. A small but notable population of HEO satellites (e.g., Molniya orbits) occupies the tail above 0.5.

Mean Motion

Mean motion distribution shows a dominant LEO peak (above 11.25 rev/day), a sparse MEO region (navigation satellites at ~2 rev/day and ~6.4 rev/day for some geodetic satellites), and a distinct GEO cluster at ~1.0 rev/day.

Key Takeaways & Outlook

1. LEO is the new bottleneck. With 94.6% of all active satellites in Low Earth Orbit, orbital congestion is concentrated in a narrow altitude band. Conjunction assessments, collision avoidance, and debris mitigation must prioritize the LEO regime.
2. A single operator reshapes the environment. Starlink's 10,083 satellites (67.8% of the global fleet) skew inclination distributions toward 53° and inflate LEO occupancy. Policy frameworks for spectrum allocation and orbital slot management must account for mega-constellation dominance.
3. The fleet is young and growing fast. 90.5% of active satellites were launched since 2020. Replacement cycles are shortening, and deorbiting compliance will increasingly determine the long-term debris population. Without reliable post-mission disposal, the current growth rate poses compounding collision risk.
4. GEO remains strategically important but numerically small. Only 583 satellites (3.9%) occupy the geostationary belt, but they carry disproportionate economic and strategic value in communications and weather monitoring. Slot coordination and interference management remain critical.
5. HEO and MEO represent specialized niches. With 40 HEO and 171 MEO satellites, these regimes serve targeted missions (signals intelligence, navigation, scientific observation). Growth here is measured and unlikely to produce the congestion pressures seen in LEO.
