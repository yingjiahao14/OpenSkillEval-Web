1. Catalog Overview & Key Figures

The CelesTrak active-satellite catalog contains 14,875 operational spacecraft as of early 2026. Orbital element epochs span 2026-02-26 to 2026-03-30, confirming the data reflects recently propagated trajectories. The catalog is overwhelmingly dominated by Low Earth Orbit (LEO) assets, which account for 94.6% of the population.

The median orbital inclination of 53.2° is heavily skewed by SpaceX's Starlink constellation, which operates in shells near 53° and 43°. Excluding Starlink, the median inclination rises to 87.9°, reflecting the large population of polar-orbiting Earth-observation and scientific satellites.

2. Major Constellation & Operator Breakdown

Starlink is the defining feature of the modern orbital landscape. With 10,083 active satellites, SpaceX's broadband constellation alone represents 67.8% of all active spacecraft and 71.6% of everything in LEO. No other operator comes within an order of magnitude.

Statistical skew: Because Starlink is so large, any aggregate metric—mean inclination, mean motion, or launch-year distribution—is effectively a weighted average between Starlink and "everything else." Analysts should always segment by operator when drawing policy conclusions.

3. Launch Year Trends

The growth curve of active satellites is the most dramatic story in this catalog. Before 2020, the active fleet grew gradually; since then, deployment has accelerated exponentially. In 2025 alone, 4,329 currently-active satellites were launched—nearly 29× the entire active fleet that existed in 2019.

Acceleration milestone: 13,461 satellites (90.5% of the catalog) were launched in 2020 or later. The five-year window 2020–2024 added 8,170 active spacecraft; 2025–2026 (partial) has already added 5,291 more.

4. Orbital Parameter Deep Dive

The orbital parameters in the catalog reveal distinct engineering choices by regime and operator. Starlink's narrow mean-motion and inclination bands create tight orbital shells, while legacy operators show far wider dispersion.

Inclination Distribution

Inclination is the parameter most visibly split by Starlink. The constellation's primary shells at ~53° and ~43° create the two dominant peaks in the overall distribution. Non-Starlink satellites cluster near 90° (polar orbits favored by Earth-observation missions) and 0° (equatorial GEO assets).

Mean Motion by Regime

Mean motion (orbits per day) is the primary discriminator for orbital altitude. LEO satellites cluster tightly around 15.0–15.3 rev/day, corresponding to altitudes of roughly 530–600 km. GEO satellites are tightly packed near 1.0 rev/day. The MEO band shows bimodal structure: navigation constellations (GPS, Galileo, GLONASS) near 2.0 rev/day, and O3b communications satellites near 5.0 rev/day.

Eccentricity

Nearly all active satellites follow near-circular orbits. The median eccentricity is 0.00016, and 92.1% of the catalog has e < 0.001. Only 29 objects exceed e = 0.5; these are classified as Highly Elliptical Orbit (HEO) and include scientific missions such as CLUSTER, THEMIS, and XMM-Newton.

5. Key Takeaways & Outlook

  • LEO is now a single-operator domain. Starlink's 10,083 satellites represent 71.6% of LEO. Policy frameworks for spectrum allocation and debris mitigation must account for this concentration of assets under one operator.
  • Launch velocity has entered a new regime. More than 90% of the active fleet was launched in the last six years. The annual launch rate in 2025 (4,329 active satellites) dwarfs any prior year, suggesting the 20,000-satellite threshold could be crossed within 12–18 months if deployment continues at current cadence.
  • Orbital congestion is an LEO problem. With 94.6% of active satellites in LEO and the vast majority below 600 km altitude, collision-risk modeling and active debris-removal prioritization should focus on sun-synchronous and mid-inclination shells.
  • Constellation diversity is shallow. Beyond Starlink, only OneWeb (651) and Kuiper (210) have constellation-scale deployments. The remaining population is fragmented across dozens of small operators, national governments, and scientific agencies.
  • Data freshness is high. Epoch dates concentrated in February–March 2026 indicate the catalog is actively maintained and suitable for near-real-time policy analysis.
Bottom line for policymakers: The orbital environment has shifted from a diversified, government-led ecosystem to a commercial, constellation-dominated one. Regulatory tools designed for the 1990s—when a few hundred GEO satellites constituted the bulk of the catalog—need urgent recalibration for a world where one company can add 4,000+ satellites in a single year.
