1. Catalog Overview & Key Figures

The CelesTrak active-satellite catalog contains 14,875 operational spacecraft with orbital element sets generated between 26 February and 30 March 2026. The catalog spans launch years from 1964 through early 2026, though the fleet is overwhelmingly modern: 90.5% of all active satellites were launched in 2020 or later (13,461 of 14,875).

2. Orbital Regime Classification

Classification method: Satellites are classified using MEAN_MOTION thresholds: LEO = mean motion > 11 rev/day (~2,000 km altitude and below); GEO = 0.85–1.5 rev/day (~35,786 km); MEO = 1.5–11 rev/day; HEO = eccentricity > 0.25 and mean motion < 11 (highly elliptical orbits such as Molniya).

5.3 Mean Motion / Altitude Shells

Within LEO, the mean-motion distribution shows a sharp peak at ~15.0–15.5 rev/day (~540–570 km altitude), corresponding to Starlink's primary operational shell. A secondary cluster near 14.3–14.5 rev/day (~700–800 km) reflects OneWeb, Iridium, and Earth-observation constellations. Navigation satellites (GPS, BeiDou, Galileo, GLONASS) cluster tightly around 2.0–2.1 rev/day (MEO, ~20,000 km altitude).

6. Key Takeaways & Outlook

Orbital Congestion

  • LEO is the pressure point. At 14,073 active satellites (94.6% of the fleet), low Earth orbit is far more crowded than GEO or MEO. The 550 km shell alone hosts over 10,000 spacecraft.
  • Specific shells are densely populated. The Starlink constellation clusters in narrow altitude and inclination bands, creating dense orbital planes that amplify conjunction risks for co-altitude missions.

Single-Operator Concentration

  • SpaceX operates 67.8% of all active satellites. This concentration creates systemic risk: any operational anomaly (mass deorbit event, firmware failure) affecting Starlink would alter the orbital environment for thousands of other operators.
  • Regulatory implications: Spectrum allocation, orbital slot coordination, and debris mitigation frameworks must account for the reality that one company's decisions now shape the statistical properties of the entire fleet.

Growth Trajectory

  • The fleet has grown 10.5× in six years. From ~1,400 satellites in 2019 to nearly 15,000 in early 2026 — an annualized growth rate of approximately 48%.
  • Non-Starlink growth is also accelerating. Even excluding Starlink, the rest of the fleet has grown from ~1,400 (2019) to ~4,800 (2026), a 3.4× increase driven by Earth-observation constellations, SAR operators, and IoT platforms.
  • 2025 was a record year, with 4,329 active satellites launched — nearly doubling the prior record of 2,477 (2024).

Implications for Policy

  • Space traffic management frameworks must scale to handle an environment where thousands of satellites occupy the same altitude bands and conjunction alerts are routine.
  • Debris mitigation compliance and end-of-life deorbit enforcement are increasingly critical as LEO density grows.
  • Spectrum coordination for megaconstellations needs to account for the operational reality that a single filing can represent thousands of transmitters in orbit.
