1. Catalog Overview & Key Figures

The CelesTrak active-satellite catalog contains 14,875 operational spacecraft as of early 2026. The orbital element sets (EPOCHs) span 2026-02-26 to 2026-03-30, confirming that the data reflects a near-real-time snapshot rather than a historical archive.

The catalog is dominated by Low Earth Orbit (LEO) assets: 12,860 satellites (86.5%) reside in LEO, while Medium Earth Orbit (MEO) hosts 1,386 (9.3%), Geostationary Orbit (GEO) holds 597 (4.0%), and Highly Elliptical Orbit (HEO) accounts for just 32 objects (0.22%).

Data freshness: With EPOCHs concentrated in late February through March 2026, the catalog captures the fleet as it existed weeks before this report. The median orbital inclination of 53.16° is driven almost entirely by Starlink’s ~53° shell, which alone contains 4,840 satellites.

2. Orbital Regime Classification

Regimes were classified using MEAN_MOTION thresholds derived directly from the TLE data:

  • LEO — Mean Motion > 14 rev/day (~160–2,000 km altitude)
  • MEO — Mean Motion between 1.1 and 14 rev/day
  • GEO — Mean Motion between 0.9 and 1.1 rev/day (~35,786 km)
  • HEO — Eccentricity > 0.5 (highly elliptical orbits, e.g., Molniya)

LEO’s 86.5% share is unprecedented in the space age. Excluding Starlink, LEO still holds 2,777 active satellites — more than the entire active fleet of just five years ago. GEO remains the preserve of traditional communications and weather operators, with a median inclination of only 0.06°, reflecting the near-equatorial plane required for geostationary positioning. MEO is dominated by navigation constellations (GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou), which cluster at inclinations near 87.89°.

3. Major Constellation & Operator Breakdown

Starlink is not merely the largest constellation — it is the defining feature of the modern catalog. With 10,083 active satellites, Starlink alone represents 67.8% of all active spacecraft and 78.4% of LEO. The next-largest operator, OneWeb, operates 651 satellites, roughly one-fifteenth the size.

Concentration risk: The top five named constellations (Starlink, OneWeb, Kuiper, Yaogan, Hulianwang) plus the aggregated “Other” category account for 90.2% of the entire catalog. A single operator’s launch cadence or anomaly response therefore has outsized implications for conjunction risk and spectrum coordination.

4. Launch Year Trends

The growth curve of the active fleet is the headline story. Before 2020, the cumulative catalog grew gradually; since then, it has nearly septupled. In 2025 alone, 4,329 satellites were launched and remain active — more than the entire pre-2020 fleet combined.

Key inflection points:

  • 2020: The cumulative active fleet crossed 1,912 as Starlink v1.0 deployments began in earnest.
  • 2022–2023: Annual additions exceeded 1,800 and 2,300 respectively, driven by Starlink Group launches and the onset of Amazon Kuiper deployments.
  • 2024–2025: The curve steepened further. By end-2025, the catalog stood at 13,913 active satellites — a 4,329-satellite net increase in a single year.
  • 2026 (partial): The first three months of 2026 have already added 962 active entries, suggesting the pace is not slowing.
Policy implication: 13,461 satellites — 90.5% of the total — were launched in the last six years. Regulatory frameworks designed for a population of a few thousand objects are now operating in a regime an order of magnitude larger.

5. Orbital Parameter Deep Dive

Mean Motion

The bimodal distribution of mean motion reflects the physical separation between LEO, MEO, and GEO. The sharp peak above 14 rev/day corresponds to the Starlink and OneWeb shells, while the smaller peaks near 1 rev/day and ~2 rev/day mark GEO and GNSS orbits respectively.

Inclination

Without Starlink, the inclination distribution would show strong peaks near 0° (GEO), ~55–65° (many LEO remote-sensing constellations), and ~86–98° (polar and sun-synchronous orbits). Starlink’s ~53° and ~43° shells create the dominant modes visible in the combined histogram.

Eccentricity

More than 99% of active satellites have eccentricities below 0.01, indicating near-circular orbits optimized for stable communications coverage and minimal station-keeping. The 32 HEO outliers (eccentricity > 0.5) are largely legacy Molniya-class communications and early-warning satellites.

6. Key Takeaways & Outlook

  • LEO is no longer a niche regime. At 86.5% of the active fleet, Low Earth Orbit has become the default destination for new spacecraft. Spectrum and debris policies must treat LEO as a congested environment, not an open frontier.
  • Starlink’s scale rewrites aggregate statistics. Any analysis that does not segment by constellation risks conflating a 10,000-satellite broadband network with a few hundred legacy GEO relays. Median inclination, mean motion, and even launch-year trends are all Starlink-driven.
  • Growth is accelerating, not plateauing. The post-2020 curve is exponential in character. Even if launch rates stabilize, the sheer mass of objects already on orbit guarantees rising conjunction frequencies and increased demand for space situational awareness (SSA) services.
  • Orbital diversity is narrowing. While HEO and GEO retain strategic value for communications and weather, nearly all new entrants are targeting a handful of LEO inclination shells. This clustering increases intra-shell collision risk and complicates spectrum reuse.

For policymakers and industry analysts, the imperative is clear: licensing regimes, debris-mitigation standards, and spectrum-allocation frameworks must be sized for a catalog of 15,000+ objects today — and the trajectory points to 20,000+ within the next few years.
