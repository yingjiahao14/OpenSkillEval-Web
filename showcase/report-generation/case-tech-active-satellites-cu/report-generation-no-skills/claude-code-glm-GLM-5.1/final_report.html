Orbit classification method: Regimes are determined from MEAN_MOTION (rev/day) thresholds — LEO: MM > 11.25 (< ~2,000 km altitude), MEO: 2.0 < MM ≤ 11.25, GEO: 0.9 ≤ MM ≤ 1.1 and eccentricity < 0.1, HEO: all remaining objects (typically high-eccentricity orbits). These thresholds align with the traditional altitude boundaries used by IADC and COPUOS.

Launch Year Trends

Launch year extracted from the first four characters of OBJECT_ID. The growth curve shows an inflection beginning in 2020, driven almost entirely by mega-constellation deployments.

Post-2020 acceleration: 13,461 of the 14,875 currently active satellites (90.5%) were launched in 2020 or later. The annual launch rate has grown 8.7× from 2019 (151) to 2025 (4,329). The 2025 figure represents the single largest annual cohort of active satellites ever recorded.

Cumulative Growth of the Active Fleet

Doubling time: The active satellite population doubled between mid-2021 and late 2023 (roughly 2.5 years), and doubled again between late 2023 and early 2026 (roughly 2.3 years). At current deployment rates, the active fleet is on track to surpass 20,000 by late 2027.

Orbital Parameter Deep Dive

Distributions of inclination, mean motion, and eccentricity reveal the most populated orbital shells and characterize outliers.

Inclination Distribution

Dominant peaks: The inclination distribution is bimodal. The largest peak at ~53° reflects Starlink's primary shell; a second cluster near 97° corresponds to Sun-synchronous orbits used by Earth-observation platforms (Planet Labs, Spire, weather satellites). A smaller peak near 87° reflects the OneWeb and Iridium polar constellations.

Mean Motion Distribution

Two concentration zones: The overwhelming majority of satellites have mean motions between 14–16 rev/day (roughly 350–700 km altitude), dominated by Starlink. A secondary cluster around 13.0–13.3 rev/day (~1,200 km) includes OneWeb and Hulianwang. The GEO cluster at ~1.0 rev/day is clearly visible but small in absolute count (595 satellites).

Key Takeaways & Outlook

Synthesis of findings for spectrum allocation, orbital debris policy, and constellation licensing decisions.

1. The LEO Regime Is Overwhelmingly Dominated by a Single Operator

SpaceX's Starlink accounts for 67.8% of all active satellites and 71.6% of all LEO satellites. This single-operator dominance has no precedent in the history of space activity and fundamentally reshapes the statistical profile of the orbital environment. Any regulatory or policy analysis that treats LEO as a homogeneous regime must account for the fact that the "average" LEO satellite is a Starlink satellite at ~550 km, 53° inclination.

2. Launch Activity Has Entered an Exponential Phase

The 90.5% of currently active satellites launched since 2020 reflects a step-change in deployment rates. Annual active-satellite additions grew from 151 (2019) to 4,329 (2025) — a 28.7× increase in six years. This trajectory is driven by mega-constellation deployment and is expected to continue as Project Kuiper scales, China's Qianfan and Hulianwang constellations expand, and Starlink adds further shells.

3. Orbital Congestion Is Concentrated in Narrow Shells

The mean-motion distribution shows that the vast majority of satellites occupy a narrow band between 15.0–15.5 rev/day (~540–580 km altitude). This concentration creates intersection risk for any object transiting this shell. The Sun-synchronous band (~97°, 14.0–14.3 rev/day, ~800–900 km) represents a secondary congestion zone with high traffic from EO constellations and weather satellites.

4. The Non-Starlink Fleet Is Diverse but Small

Excluding Starlink, the remaining 4,792 satellites span all orbital regimes (83.2% LEO, 12.4% GEO, 2.6% MEO, 1.8% HEO). The "Other/Unclassified" category (2,438 satellites) contains a long tail of national missions, commercial comsats, and rideshare payloads. No single non-Starlink operator exceeds 5% of the total fleet.

5. Implications for Policy

  • Spectrum allocation: The clustering of thousands of satellites in adjacent orbital shells raises spectrum interference questions that ITAU coordination processes must address at constellation scale, not individual satellite scale.
  • Debris mitigation: The concentration of 10,000+ satellites in a narrow altitude band means any fragmentation event would have outsized consequences. Post-mission disposal compliance rates are the single most important metric for long-term sustainability.
  • Licensing parity: New entrants (Kuiper, Qianfan) face an environment where the most desirable orbital shells are already heavily populated. Licensing frameworks may need to consider incumbent density when evaluating new constellation applications.
