1. Catalog Overview & Key Figures

The CelesTrak active-satellite catalog contains 14,875 operational spacecraft as of the latest Two-Line Element (TLE) update. The data spans launch years from 1964 to 2026, with the overwhelming majority of currently active assets launched in the past five years.

Orbital parameters show a strong concentration in near-circular, low-inclination orbits typical of commercial broadband constellations. The median eccentricity across the entire catalog is 0.00016 (essentially circular), while the median mean motion is 15.27 revolutions per day — firmly in the LEO regime.

Data Freshness: EPOCH timestamps in the catalog are concentrated in March 2026, indicating the dataset is highly current. The oldest still-active objects date to the mid-1960s (e.g., CALSPHERE 1, launched 1964).

2. Orbital Regime Classification

Satellites were classified into four orbital regimes using mean-motion (MM) thresholds derived from Keplerian mechanics:

  • LEO (Low Earth Orbit): MM > 14 rev/day (~160–2,000 km altitude)
  • MEO (Medium Earth Orbit): 1.1 < MM ≤ 14 rev/day (navigation constellations, science missions)
  • GEO (Geostationary Orbit): 0.9 ≤ MM ≤ 1.1 rev/day (~35,786 km)
  • HEO (Highly Elliptical Orbit): Eccentricity > 0.5, regardless of mean motion (Molniya, scientific elliptical orbits)

4. Launch Year Trends

The growth curve of currently active satellites is the defining story of this catalog. Prior to 2020, the active fleet grew gradually; since 2020, the curve has turned nearly vertical.

Figure 3. Annual launch count (bars) and cumulative active-satellite population (line) by launch year.
Post-2020 Acceleration: Of the 14,875 active satellites, 13,461 (90.5%) were launched in 2020 or later. The peak single year is 2025 with 4,329 active satellites still on orbit. Only 1,414 active satellites (9.5%) pre-date 2020.

This acceleration is not a temporary spike; it reflects the operational ramp of Starlink (beginning large-scale deployment in 2019–2020) and the entry of competing constellations such as OneWeb and Kuiper. For spectrum regulators and debris-policy planners, the implication is clear: the orbital environment is changing faster than historical licensing and mitigation frameworks were designed to handle.

5. Orbital Parameter Deep Dive

5.1 Inclination Distribution

Inclination reveals the geographic coverage strategy of operators. Starlink's shell is tightly clustered around 53° (with a smaller polar shell near 97°), producing a bimodal distribution when viewed against the rest of the population.

Figure 4. Histogram of orbital inclination: Starlink (orange) versus all other operators (blue).

The non-Starlink population shows a much broader spread, with significant clusters near 0° (equatorial / GEO), 55–65° (GPS / navigation), and 90° (polar science and reconnaissance orbits). The overall median inclination of 53.2° is therefore a Starlink-weighted figure; excluding Starlink, the median rises to 87.9°.

5.2 Eccentricity Distribution

The vast majority of active satellites follow near-circular orbits. The median eccentricity is 0.00016, and only 32 objects exceed 0.5 eccentricity (classified here as HEO). High-eccentricity outliers include scientific missions such as CLUSTER, THEMIS, and heritage Molniya communication satellites.

5.3 Mean Motion Distribution

Mean motion directly encodes orbital altitude. The distribution is strongly bimodal: a massive peak above 14 rev/day (LEO) and a smaller, sharp peak near 1 rev/day (GEO). The gap between 1.1 and ~11 rev/day is the MEO navigation belt.

6. Key Takeaways & Outlook

  • LEO is the new normal. With 86.5% of active satellites in low Earth orbit, policy and spectrum frameworks must prioritize LEO congestion management over legacy GEO-centric models.
  • Single-operator dominance reshapes risk profiles. Starlink's 67.8% share means that a single operator's launch cadence, de-orbit strategy, and collision-avoidance practices have outsized impact on aggregate orbital debris risk.
  • Growth is accelerating, not plateauing. 90.5% of the active fleet was launched after 2020. Licensing regimes and debris-mitigation guidelines written for a slower era need recalibration.
  • Orbital parameters are becoming homogeneous. The Starlink-driven concentration near 53° inclination and ~15 rev/day mean motion creates a dense shell that increases conjunction risk within a narrow band of altitude and inclination.
  • GEO remains strategically important but numerically minor. At 596 active satellites (4.0%), GEO assets are high-value (communications, weather, early warning) but no longer representative of the overall population.

For analysts and policymakers, the actionable priority is clear: invest in improved space situational awareness (SSA) coverage of the LEO shell, update constellation licensing to require transparent ephemeris sharing, and ensure that debris-mitigation rules keep pace with a launch rate that has increased by an order of magnitude in less than a decade.
