4. Launch Year Trends

Launch year is extracted from the first four characters of OBJECT_ID (international designator). This represents the year each satellite was launched — not when it became operational. Only currently active satellites are counted; deorbited or failed spacecraft are excluded.

The inflection point is unmistakable. Prior to 2019, annual active-satellite launches rarely exceeded a few hundred. By 2025, the annual count surged to 4,329 — with Starlink constituting the majority of that volume. The cumulative curve shows a classic hockey-stick acceleration.

6. Key Takeaways & Outlook

Finding 1: Single-Operator Dominance

Starlink's 10,083 satellites (67.8% of the fleet) represent an unprecedented concentration. No previous operator has controlled more than a few hundred active spacecraft. This has profound implications for spectrum coordination, collision avoidance responsibility, and the statistical interpretation of any fleet-wide metric.

Finding 2: LEO Congestion Is Accelerating

LEO now hosts 14,073 active satellites (94.6% of the catalog). With approved FCC and ITU filings for tens of thousands of additional satellites from multiple operators (Starlink Gen2, Kuiper, Guowang, Lightspeed), the 500–600 km altitude band is becoming the most contested orbital regime. Conjunction rates and debris generation risk scale non-linearly with population density.

Finding 3: Post-2020 Surge Reshapes the Fleet

90.5% of all active satellites were launched since 2020. The fleet has effectively been rebuilt in under six years. This pace shows no sign of slowing — if anything, the ramp steepens as launch cadence increases and new mega-constellations begin deployment.

Finding 4: GEO and MEO Remain Strategically Vital

Despite LEO's numerical dominance, GEO (624 satellites) and MEO (160 satellites) host critical communications, navigation (GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, Beidou), and early-warning assets. These regimes face different sustainability challenges — limited orbital slots in GEO, and radiation environment concerns in MEO.

Policy Implications

The data support several policy priorities: (1) updating orbital debris mitigation guidelines for mega-constellation scale; (2) revisiting spectrum allocation frameworks to account for single-operator dominance; (3) investing in space situational awareness capacity proportional to fleet growth; and (4) establishing international coordination mechanisms for the most congested altitude bands.
