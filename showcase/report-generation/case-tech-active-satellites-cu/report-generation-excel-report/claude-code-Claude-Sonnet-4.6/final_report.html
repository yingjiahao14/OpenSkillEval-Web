Mean Motion Distribution

Bimodal structure: The mean motion histogram shows an overwhelming spike at ~15 rev/day (LEO Starlink shells at ~540–570 km), a secondary cluster near 14 rev/day (other LEO constellations), and a small GEO peak at ~1.0 rev/day. MEO is a thin middle band. The 11.25 rev/day LEO threshold clearly separates the two dominant populations.

Eccentricity Distribution

Near-circular orbits are the overwhelming norm: 99.0% of active satellites have eccentricity below 0.01, consistent with operational communications and Earth-observation constellations that maintain circular orbits for consistent ground coverage geometry. Highly eccentric orbits (e > 0.25 — the HEO classification threshold) represent a small but specialized population including Molniya and other elliptical communication orbits serving high-latitude regions.

Key Takeaways & Policy Outlook

1. LEO is critically crowded.
94.6% of all active satellites — 14,073 spacecraft — operate in Low Earth Orbit. At the current trajectory, conjunctions, frequency interference, and debris risk will intensify substantially before orbital capacity limits are formally defined or regulated.
2. One operator defines the landscape.
Starlink (10,083 satellites, 67.8% share) is not just the largest constellation — it is the statistical majority of the entire active fleet. Any aggregate metric — inclination, altitude, mean motion — is primarily a Starlink metric. Regulatory frameworks must explicitly segment mega-constellation data.
3. The acceleration is structural, not cyclical.
90.5% of today's active fleet was launched in the past six years. This is driven by launch cost reductions, rideshare programs, and commercial broadband demand — forces that will persist. Spectrum allocation and orbital slot coordination must be forward-looking, not reactive.
4. Inclination clustering creates interference hotspots.
The concentration of satellites in 53° and 97° inclination shells creates predictable RF coordination challenges. ITU coordination zones and national licensing authorities should prioritize these shells for pre-coordination agreements and automated spectrum monitoring.
5. Debris sustainability. With 14,073 active LEO satellites and thousands more planned by Starlink Gen2, Amazon Kuiper, and other entrants, deorbit compliance (IADC's 5-year / FCC's 5-year post-mission disposal rule) becomes a critical policy lever. A small non-compliance rate at this scale can meaningfully raise background debris flux in critical orbital shells.
