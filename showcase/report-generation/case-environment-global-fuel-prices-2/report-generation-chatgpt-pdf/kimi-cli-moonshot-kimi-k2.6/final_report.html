1. Global Fuel Price Overview

Across 84 countries and 327 weekly observations from January 2020 to April 2026, global retail fuel markets exhibit a clear hierarchy: petrol commands the highest average price, followed by diesel and LPG. Brent Crude, the global benchmark, averaged $106.69 per barrel over the period, ranging from a COVID-19 low of $47.97 (April 2020) to a 2022 crisis peak of $130.00 (March 2022).

2.2 Shock Events

COVID-19 Demand Shock (Mar–Apr 2020)
Brent Crude fell from ~$71 to $47.97/bbl (−32%). Global average petrol prices dropped 33.0%, from $1.53/L to $1.02/L. The pass-through was immediate and symmetric, with retail prices tracking crude declines within weeks.
2022 Energy Crisis (Feb–Mar 2022)
Brent spiked to an all-time high of $130.00/bbl on March 28, 2022. Global average petrol reached $2.63/L during the crisis window. Unlike the COVID collapse, the upward shock was partially absorbed in high-subsidy regimes (see Section 4).

2.3 Brent-to-Retail Correlation

At the global weekly aggregate level, the Pearson correlation between Brent Crude and retail prices is exceptionally strong:

  • Brent ↔ Petrol: r = 1.000
  • Brent ↔ Diesel: r = 1.000
  • Brent ↔ LPG: r = 1.000

At the individual-country level, correlations remain high for most markets (median r ≈ 0.999), but diverge sharply in heavily subsidized or politically managed economies. Venezuela (r = 0.081), Iran (r = 0.336), and Libya (r = 0.472) show the weakest pass-through, indicating effective (if costly) price isolation from global markets.

Weekly volatility (standard deviation of percentage changes) is 1.55% for petrol in low-subsidy markets, closely mirroring Brent's 1.54%. In high-subsidy markets, petrol volatility is paradoxically higher at 2.18%, driven by discrete policy adjustments rather than smooth market tracking.

4.2 Pass-Through Differences

Subsidies do more than lower price levels — they alter the dynamics of price transmission:

  • Low-subsidy markets: Weekly Brent-to-petrol change correlation = 0.996. These markets function as efficient pass-through mechanisms.
  • High-subsidy markets: Weekly change correlation drops to 0.712. Governments actively smooth or delay crude price movements.

However, this stabilization comes with a cost: high-subsidy markets exhibit higher measured volatility (2.18% weekly std. dev.) than low-subsidy markets (1.55%), because administered prices adjust in discrete, politically timed steps rather than tracking continuous market movements.

4.3 Subsidy Distribution by Income Level

Subsidies are not concentrated in low-income countries. In fact, high-income nations have the lowest share of high-subsidy regimes (12.5%), while middle-income and low-income countries show similar rates (~34–35%). This pattern reflects the concentration of oil-producing Gulf states (high-income, high-subsidy) and the fiscal constraints facing many developing economies.

5.2 Energy Equity Considerations

Low-income countries impose a tax burden (22.9%) comparable to middle-income countries (21.1%), despite lower average incomes. This suggests that fuel taxation in the poorest nations is already near a fiscal ceiling — further increases risk political resistance and demand destruction.

Price stability, measured by the coefficient of variation (CV) of weekly petrol prices, is uniform across income levels (CV ≈ 0.178) because global crude movements dominate local dynamics. The real difference lies in who bears the risk: in high-income, low-subsidy countries, consumers face market prices directly; in low-income, high-subsidy countries, governments absorb price volatility through fiscal outlays.

Equity Insight: The cheapest fuel in absolute terms is found in subsidized low-income markets (e.g., Libya, Venezuela, Nigeria), but these are often the countries least able to sustain fiscal transfers. The subsidy burden falls on government budgets that could otherwise fund health, education, or infrastructure.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

The following findings synthesize the quantitative evidence into actionable insights for energy policymakers.

  1. Brent Crude is the dominant price driver, but policy creates massive divergence.
    Global aggregate correlations between Brent and retail prices approach 1.000, yet the gap between the most expensive region (Europe, $3.70/L) and the cheapest (Middle East, $1.23/L) has widened to $2.87/L. Implication: Policymakers cannot control crude prices, but they have substantial levers — taxation and subsidy design — to determine where their country sits on the global price distribution.
  2. Subsidies are highly effective at dampening prices — and highly expensive.
    High-subsidy regimes reduce average petrol prices by 84.5% relative to low-subsidy markets. However, weekly price volatility is actually higher in subsidized markets (2.18% vs. 1.55%) due to discrete administrative adjustments. Implication: Consider targeted cash transfers or price-smoothing funds instead of blanket price controls. These preserve consumer protection while reducing fiscal exposure and market distortion.
  3. Low-subsidy markets offer near-perfect crude price pass-through.
    Weekly Brent-to-retail change correlations reach 0.996 in low-subsidy regimes. This efficiency supports market-based allocation but exposes consumers to full volatility. Implication: In deregulated markets, introduce automatic stabilizers (e.g., variable excise taxes that rise when crude falls and fall when crude spikes) to buffer consumers without destroying price signals.
  4. High-income countries tax fuel heavily; low-income countries subsidize it heavily.
    High-income nations average 34.7% fuel taxes, while 34.8% of low-income country observations fall under high-subsidy regimes. This creates a regressive global pattern where the poorest governments bear the largest fiscal burden to keep prices low. Implication: Low-income countries should prioritize subsidy reform paired with social safety nets. International financial institutions can support this transition with bridge financing.
  5. The regional price spread is widening — and will continue to widen without coordination.
    The gap between highest- and lowest-priced regions grew 78% between 2020 and 2026 (from $1.61/L to $2.87/L). Divergent carbon-tax trajectories in Europe and persistent subsidies in the Middle East are structural drivers. Implication: Regional trade blocs should harmonize fuel taxation to prevent smuggling, arbitrage, and competitive distortions. Cross-border fuel trafficking is already a documented problem in high-spread regions.
  6. Tax policy in low-income countries is near a ceiling.
    Low-income countries already impose a 22.9% average tax rate — only modestly below middle-income levels (21.1%) and far below high-income levels (34.7%). With limited fiscal space, further tax increases may be politically and economically infeasible. Implication: Low-income governments should focus on efficiency (reducing distribution losses, improving refinery utilization) rather than revenue extraction from fuel.
  7. The COVID-19 and 2022 crises revealed the fragility of both high- and low-subsidy regimes.
    During COVID-19, low-subsidy markets delivered rapid price relief (petrol −33%), while high-subsidy markets saw no consumer benefit because prices were already below cost. During the 2022 spike, high-subsidy governments faced explosive fiscal costs to maintain price caps. Implication: Design hybrid regimes with a price corridor — a floating band with automatic adjustments — that protects consumers from extreme spikes while allowing some pass-through during normal fluctuations.
Bottom Line for Policymakers: There is no single optimal fuel pricing model. The evidence shows that pure market pricing exposes consumers to volatility, while pure subsidy regimes impose unsustainable fiscal burdens and create their own form of instability. The most resilient approach is a managed float — market-based pricing with automatic stabilizers, targeted social protection for vulnerable households, and transparent tax rules that adjust counter-cyclically to crude price movements.

Methodology & Data Notes

This report is based on 27,468 weekly observations across 84 countries from January 2020 to April 2026. Brent Crude prices are global benchmarks and identical across all countries for a given week. Subsidy levels are categorized as High (including Very High) and Low (including Medium) for analytical clarity. Tax percentages are estimated and show week-to-week variation within countries, likely reflecting methodological estimation rather than actual policy changes. All monetary values are expressed in USD. Correlations and averages are computed directly from the dataset without imputation or fabrication.
