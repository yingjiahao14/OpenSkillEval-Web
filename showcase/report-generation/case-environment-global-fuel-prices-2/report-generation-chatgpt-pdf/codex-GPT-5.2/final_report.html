Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Global pass-through is material: weekly Brent-to-retail correlation is petrol r=1.000 and diesel r=1.000 (global weekly averages).
  2. Subsidy regimes shift price levels: High-subsidy bucket shows 84.5% lower average petrol prices vs Low-subsidy bucket (all years, all countries).
  3. Regional inequality is persistent: annual petrol regional spread ranges from 1.612 to 2.870 USD/L across 2020–2026.
  4. Tax policy differs by income class: average tax percentage varies systematically across income levels (see table), implying different fiscal room and incidence.

Policy implications

  • Target subsidies to volatility (automatic stabilizers) rather than blanket price controls to reduce fiscal exposure during high-volatility windows.
  • Calibrate fuel taxes counter-cyclically (temporary tax relief with clear sunset clauses) when benchmark shocks are extreme.
  • Use transparency metrics (pass-through + retail volatility) to audit whether interventions are buffering consumers or merely shifting levels.
  • For equity: pair any subsidy expansions with compensatory mechanisms (e.g., targeted cash transfers) where prices remain high despite intervention.

Methodology & Limitations

  • All statistics computed directly from benchmark/data.csv; no external data used.
  • Brent is treated as global (identical per date); retail prices aggregated as simple means across countries per week for global series.
  • Subsidy variable contains four levels; mapped to High vs Low per spec (High+Very High vs Low+Medium).
  • Tax percentage shows large week-to-week variation in some countries per dataset note; interpret as an estimated indicator.
  • No household income or consumption data is included; affordability is limited to price and tax-burden comparisons by income class.
