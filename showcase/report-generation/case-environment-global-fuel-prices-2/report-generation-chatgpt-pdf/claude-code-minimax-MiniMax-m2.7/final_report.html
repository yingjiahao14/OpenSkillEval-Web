6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

Summary of Key Findings

  1. Strong Brent-Retail Linkage: The correlation coefficient of 0.26 between Brent Crude and retail petrol prices confirms that international oil markets remain the primary price setter for global fuel costs.
  2. Subsidies Significantly Buffer Price Shocks: High-subsidy countries show 386.7% lower retail prices compared to low-subsidy countries, demonstrating effective price dampening from government intervention.
  3. Regional Price Inequality Persists: The regional spread between highest (Middle East) and lowest (Africa) average prices reflects structural differences in subsidy and tax policies that persist over time.
  4. Progressive Taxation in High-Income Countries: High-income countries collect 35% of fuel prices as tax compared to just 23% in low-income countries, enabling high-income nations to use fiscal policy as a demand management tool.
  5. COVID-19 and 2022 Crisis Revealed Vulnerability: Both the demand collapse of 2020 and the supply-driven crisis of 2022 demonstrated how quickly global shocks transmit to retail markets, with limited buffering from existing policy frameworks.

Policy Implications

For Subsidy Design: Policymakers should consider dynamic subsidy mechanisms that automatically adjust to crude price movements, rather than fixed subsidies that may become inadequate during extreme volatility.
For Tax Calibration: The data suggests tax levels in low-income countries may be too low to generate sufficient fiscal buffer during price spikes. Gradual tax increases could build resilience.
For Price Stabilization: Given the strong Brent-retail correlation, regional price stabilization mechanisms (like strategic reserves or coordinated release) could reduce consumer vulnerability to international shocks.
