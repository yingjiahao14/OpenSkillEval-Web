1. Global Fuel Price Overview

Across 27,468 weekly observations from 84 countries, the global average retail petrol price stood at $2.282/liter, with diesel at $2.134/liter and LPG at $1.711/liter. Prices exhibited extreme dispersion: petrol ranged from a low of $0.010/liter (Venezuela) to $6.779/liter (Hong Kong), a 678x spread driven primarily by subsidy regimes and taxation.

Brent Crude oil — the global benchmark — averaged $106.69/barrel over the period, ranging from a COVID-era low of $47.97/barrel (April 2020) to a peak of $130.00/barrel during 2022. This 2.7x swing in the underlying commodity forms the backdrop for all retail price analysis.

2. Temporal Price Trends & Volatility

The 2020–2026 period spans three distinct energy market phases. The COVID-19 demand shock drove Brent Crude to a low of $47.97/barrel by late April 2020, pulling global average petrol to $1.485/liter for the year. The post-pandemic recovery saw Brent surge 53.5% to average $106.59 in 2021, while the 2022 energy crisis pushed Brent to a high of $130.00/barrel and retail petrol averaged $2.505/liter globally — the peak annual figure until 2026.

The Brent-to-retail correlation is remarkably strong: r = 1.0000 for petrol, r = 1.0000 for diesel, and r = 1.0000 for LPG. These near-perfect correlations at the aggregate weekly level indicate that crude oil price is the dominant determinant of global average retail fuel prices, though country-level pass-through varies substantially by policy regime.

3. Regional Price Disparities

Regional fuel prices exhibit persistent and widening disparities. Europe consistently leads with the highest average petrol price at $3.702/liter, followed closely by Oceania at $3.655/liter. At the other extreme, the Middle East averages just $1.226/liter — a 3x differential driven by production subsidies, low taxation, and proximity to extraction.

The regional price spread (difference between highest and lowest regional averages) has widened from $1.61 in 2020 to $2.87 in 2026, indicating that divergent policy regimes are amplifying rather than narrowing global fuel price inequality over time.

5. Fuel Affordability by Income Level

Paradoxically, high-income countries pay the most for fuel — averaging $3.547/liter for petrol — while low-income countries average just $1.389/liter. This counterintuitive pattern reflects two forces: high-income nations impose heavy fuel taxes (avg 34.7%) and are predominantly low-subsidy, while many low-income nations maintain subsidies as a social protection mechanism.

While low-income countries pay lower absolute prices, this does not imply greater affordability. Fuel as a share of household income is typically much higher in low-income nations. This dataset does not include purchasing-power-adjusted prices, which is a limitation for true affordability analysis.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

Finding 1: Crude Oil Dominates Retail Prices. The Brent-to-retail petrol correlation of r = 1.0000 at the aggregate level confirms that international commodity markets are the primary determinant of what consumers pay. Domestic policy operates within the margin, not as a replacement for market forces.
Finding 2: Subsidies Compress Levels but Not Volatility. High and Very High subsidy countries reduce average retail petrol prices by 86.8% compared to low-subsidy peers. However, even "Very High" subsidy regimes maintain a Brent correlation of 0.975 — crude price swings still propagate, just at lower absolute levels.
Policy implication: Policymakers seeking price stability (not just low prices) need mechanisms beyond subsidies — such as price smoothing funds, strategic reserves, or hedging programs.
Finding 3: Regional Price Inequality Is Widening. The gap between the most and least expensive regions grew 78% from $1.61 (2020) to $2.87 (2026). Europe and Oceania consumers increasingly diverge from Middle East and South American peers, driven by compounding effects of taxation policy.
Policy implication: International coordination on fuel taxation floors or ceilings may be worth exploring to prevent extreme divergence.
Finding 4: Low-Income Countries Face a Double Burden. Despite lower absolute prices, low-income nations exhibit the highest price volatility (CV = 0.644 vs. 0.424 for high-income). Their stabilization mechanisms are less effective, exposing vulnerable populations to sharper price swings.
Policy implication: International development support for fuel price stabilization infrastructure (hedging capacity, strategic reserves) would disproportionately benefit the countries most exposed to volatility.
Finding 5: Taxation Drives the High-Income Price Premium. High-income countries average a 34.7% tax rate on fuel — 60% higher than middle- and low-income nations (~21–23%). This fiscal overhead, not commodity costs, explains why wealthy nations pay 2.5x more per liter.
Policy implication: Tax design matters. Revenue-recycling mechanisms (e.g., returning carbon tax revenue as dividends) can maintain fiscal incentives while managing consumer impact.
Finding 6: The Subsidy-Tax Nexus Is the Key Policy Lever. The data reveals a clear spectrum: Very High subsidy / ~15% tax countries (petrol ~$0.15/L) to Low subsidy / ~38% tax countries (petrol ~$3.45/L). The 23x price ratio between the extremes is almost entirely policy-driven, not commodity-driven.
Policy implication: Subsidy reform and tax calibration are the most powerful tools governments have to influence consumer fuel costs. Gradual, transparent adjustments with targeted transfers to vulnerable groups represent the consensus best-practice approach.
Finding 7: 2020 Provided a Natural Experiment. The COVID-19 demand shock (Brent falling 31% from January to April 2020) revealed that subsidy countries delayed or muted retail price drops — consistent with "sticky downward" administered pricing. Low-subsidy countries tracked the decline much more closely, delivering faster relief to consumers.
Policy implication: Subsidy regimes should incorporate symmetric adjustment mechanisms that pass through crude price decreases as effectively as they buffer increases.

Data Limitations

  • Tax percentage variability: The tax_percentage column shows high week-to-week variation within individual countries (e.g., US ranging 0–65%), likely reflecting estimation methodology. Country-level tax analysis should be interpreted as indicative rather than precise.
  • No purchasing-power adjustment: All prices are in nominal USD. Lower absolute prices in low-income countries do not necessarily imply greater affordability.
  • Binary vs. gradient subsidies: The dataset uses categorical subsidy levels rather than continuous subsidy amounts, limiting granular analysis of subsidy efficiency.
  • Data through April 2026 only: The 2026 data year is incomplete (Jan–Apr), and statistics for 2026 should be compared cautiously with full-year figures.
