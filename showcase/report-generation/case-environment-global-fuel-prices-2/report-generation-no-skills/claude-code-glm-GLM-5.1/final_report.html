6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

1. Brent Crude is the dominant long-run driver of retail prices, but short-term pass-through is highly heterogeneous.

Global-average weekly petrol prices correlate with Brent at r = 1.0, and week-over-week changes show r = 0.995. However, at the individual country level, the mean first-difference correlation is only r = 0.617 — meaning domestic policy choices create substantial variation in how quickly crude price changes reach consumers. Policymakers should treat the global-average correlation as an upper bound; actual pass-through depends on national fiscal architecture.

2. Subsidies dramatically reduce consumer prices — by up to 95.7% — and dampen volatility.

Very High-subsidy countries average $0.148/L vs. $3.451/L in Low-subsidy nations. The first-difference Brent correlation declines systematically from Low to Very High subsidy levels, confirming that subsidies decouple retail prices from crude volatility. However, subsidies also weaken the price signals that incentivize fuel efficiency, creating a policy trade-off between consumer protection and demand-side efficiency.

3. The subsidy buffer is most visible during crises — but at high fiscal cost.

During the 2022 energy crisis, the petrol price gap between Low-subsidy and Very High-subsidy countries reached $3.626/L. This demonstrates the protective effect of subsidies during extreme events, but sustaining such gaps requires substantial public expenditure. Targeted, time-limited subsidies triggered by price thresholds offer better fiscal sustainability than blanket subsidies.

4. Retail prices are asymmetrically rigid: they fall more slowly than crude during demand collapses.

During COVID-19, Brent Crude dropped 17.5% but retail petrol fell only 17.4%. Fixed tax components and subsidy floor effects create downward price rigidity, meaning consumers benefit less from crude price declines than they suffer from increases. This asymmetry particularly disadvantages low-income consumers who spend a larger share of income on fuel.

5. Tax policy diverges sharply by income level, creating equity concerns.

High-income countries impose average fuel taxes of 34.72% vs. 22.86% in low-income countries. While low-income consumers pay lower absolute prices (partly due to subsidies), they may still face a higher affordability burden relative to income. Tax calibration should account for purchasing power parity, not just absolute price levels.

6. Regional pricing inequality persists and widens during disruptions.

The regional price spread ranged from $1.612 to $2.869 USD/L. Crises amplify inequality as high-tax, low-subsidy regions see disproportionate increases. International coordination on strategic reserves and regional stabilization mechanisms can narrow this gap.

Policy Recommendations

  1. Implement automatic stabilizer mechanisms: Design subsidy programs with built-in triggers that increase support when Brent Crude exceeds a defined threshold and phase out as prices normalize, reducing fiscal drag.
  2. Calibrate taxes to affordability: Shift from flat percentage-based fuel taxes toward structures that consider GNI per capita, ensuring fuel costs remain a proportionate share of household expenditure across income levels.
  3. Establish regional price stabilization funds: Pool resources at the regional level (especially in regions with the widest spreads) to buffer against extreme crude price shocks without requiring permanent subsidies.
  4. Protect market signals during non-crisis periods: Allow retail prices to reflect crude movements during normal conditions, reserving subsidy intervention for crisis-level thresholds. This preserves demand-side efficiency incentives.
  5. Address downward price rigidity: When crude prices fall, ensure tax and subsidy structures allow retail prices to follow — not just on the upside. Consider tax rebates or temporary subsidy reductions when Brent falls below a floor, passing savings to consumers.
