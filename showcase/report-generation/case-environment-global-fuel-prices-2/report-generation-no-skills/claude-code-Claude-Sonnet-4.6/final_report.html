Executive Summary

Global retail fuel prices experienced severe structural disruption across the 2020–2026 period, shaped by three dominant forces: the COVID-19 demand collapse, the 2022 energy supply crisis, and divergent national subsidy and taxation regimes. Brent crude tracked a trajectory from a trough of $47.97/bbl in April 2020 to a ceiling of $130.00/bbl during the Russia-Ukraine supply shock in early 2022, and has remained elevated at $123.7/bbl through April 2026.

The global weekly average petrol price demonstrates near-perfect correlation with Brent crude (r = 1.0000 at the aggregate level), confirming that crude oil markets remain the primary transmission mechanism for retail pump prices. However, cross-country variation is substantial: the country-level Brent-petrol correlation ranges from 0.08 to 1.00 (mean 0.96), reflecting the considerable buffering power of subsidy programs and tax policy. Countries maintaining very high subsidy levels kept average petrol at just $0.148/L — a 95.7% dampening effect relative to low-subsidy markets averaging $3.451/L.

Europe and Oceania consistently register the highest retail prices (overall averages $3.70/L and $3.66/L respectively), driven by high excise tax burdens averaging 34.6–38.7% of pump price. The Middle East, by contrast, averages $1.23/L, sustained by explicit producer subsidies. These structural disparities have significant implications for energy transition policy, fiscal space for subsidy reform, and cross-border fuel arbitrage.

Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Crude-to-Retail Transmission is Near-Perfect at the Aggregate Level

    The Pearson correlation between weekly global-average Brent crude and retail petrol/diesel prices is 1.0000 — indicating complete pass-through at the aggregate level. At the country level, the mean correlation of 0.9602 (median 0.9987) confirms that subsidy regimes, while substantial in dampening absolute price levels, do not decouple price trajectories from crude fundamentals over multi-year horizons. Even Very High subsidy countries move in directional lockstep with crude markets; they simply compress the magnitude.

    Policy Implication: Subsidy Reform

    Any fiscal reform strategy that relies on prolonged crude price stability is structurally vulnerable. Policy frameworks should incorporate crude price scenario bands, not point estimates.

  2. The 2022 Energy Crisis Produced Historically Unprecedented Retail Prices

    Brent touching $130.00/bbl in March–June 2022 drove global average petrol to $2.782/L — a 142% increase over the April 2020 COVID trough of $1.056/L. The 2022 crisis also elevated cross-country price dispersion (StdDev = 1.630 in 2022 vs 1.012 in 2020), as subsidy-equipped governments partially shielded consumers while open-market economies absorbed the full pass-through. European petrol peaked at $4.064/L in 2022.

    Policy Implication: Energy Security

    Supply-side geopolitical disruptions now represent a larger systematic risk than demand-side shocks (COVID demonstrated rapid recovery; supply shocks have proven more sustained). Strategic reserve and diversification policies should be calibrated accordingly.

  3. Subsidy Programs Exert Profound but Fiscally Costly Price Dampening

    Very High subsidy markets maintain petrol at $0.148/L — 95.7% below the $3.451/L baseline in Low subsidy markets. At April 2026 Brent prices of $123.7/bbl, the implied fiscal cost of maintaining this gap is approximately $3.30/L. For a country consuming even 10 billion litres annually, this represents a fiscal outlay approaching $33 billion per year. High subsidy countries ($0.709/L) still achieve 79.5% price dampening — likely a more fiscally sustainable equilibrium for many emerging-market economies.

    Policy Implication: Subsidy Rationalization

    Graduated subsidy reform — targeting reductions from Very High to High subsidy levels rather than immediate full liberalization — would release meaningful fiscal resources while avoiding the consumer price shock of complete decontrol. A $0.56/L price increase (Very High to High) is far more manageable than a $3.30/L adjustment to market rates.

  4. European and Oceanian Fuel Taxation Levels Signal Transition-Ready Fiscal Infrastructure

    With average tax shares of 34.6% (Europe) and 38.7% (Oceania) and petrol prices of $3.70+/L, these regions have already built consumer-facing price signals consistent with accelerating EV adoption. The excise structure simultaneously generates fiscal revenue for green transition investment. The $2.476/L price spread between Europe and the Middle East represents both a structural equity question and, from a climate perspective, a differential carbon pricing signal.

    Policy Implication: Energy Transition

    High-tax, high-price regions should leverage existing infrastructure for carbon-adjusted fuel levies or hypothecated EV transition funds. Subsidy-dependent regions face the inverse challenge: transition incentives are structurally undermined by prices that make fossil fuels appear artificially cheap.

  5. The Post-COVID Price Plateau Suggests Structural, Not Cyclical, Price Elevation

    Following the 2022 crisis peak, prices did not revert to 2020 levels. Global petrol averages in 2023 ($2.234/L), 2024 ($2.545/L), 2025 ($2.540/L), and 2026 (YTD $2.647/L) all exceed the 2020 baseline ($1.485/L) by 50–78%. Brent has maintained a $104–$124/bbl range through 2023–2026, compared to a 2020 average of $69.4/bbl. This structural uplift has persisted through multiple annual cycles, suggesting demand recovery, constrained spare OPEC+ capacity, and geopolitical risk premiums are now embedded in crude pricing.

    Policy Implication: Long-Term Planning

    Energy ministries and transport regulators should baseline medium-term planning on Brent in the $110–$130/bbl range, treating $70–$80/bbl scenarios as stress-test downside rather than base case. Infrastructure investment decisions, fuel tax rates, and subsidy budgets all require recalibration to this new normal.

  6. Nominal Price Gaps by Income Level Mask Real Affordability Crises in Low-Income Nations

    Low-income countries pay $1.389/L — less than 40% of high-income country prices ($3.547/L). However, with per-capita incomes potentially 20–40x lower, the real fuel burden as a share of household income is substantially higher. The 22.9% tax rate in low-income countries (vs. 34.7% in high-income) indicates modest fiscal extraction, but even at $1.389/L, rural and transportation-dependent populations in low-income nations face acute energy poverty when crude prices rise.

    Policy Implication: Equity & Targeted Relief

    Universal fuel subsidies in low-income countries are an inefficient mechanism for equity — they disproportionately benefit vehicle owners and commercial operators. Targeted cash transfer programs or means-tested energy vouchers would deliver equivalent consumer protection at lower fiscal cost, while allowing market price signals to function for commercial demand.
