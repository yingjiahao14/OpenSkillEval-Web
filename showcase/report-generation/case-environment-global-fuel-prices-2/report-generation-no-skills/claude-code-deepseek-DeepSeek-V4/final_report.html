6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

Synthesized, actionable insights for energy policy analysts and decision-makers.

1

Brent Crude Is a Weak Predictor of Retail Prices at the Country Level

The Pearson correlation between Brent Crude and individual country retail petrol prices is only r = 0.38, meaning global crude explains just 14% of the variation consumers see. The remaining 86% is driven by domestic policy choices — taxes, subsidies, and market structure. Policymakers should not assume that falling crude will translate into proportional consumer relief; in many countries, tax floors and subsidy ceilings break the transmission mechanism entirely.

Recommendation: When calibrating tax formulas or fuel price stabilization funds, model the Brent-to-retail pass-through elasticity specific to your country's subsidy and tax structure rather than using a global average.
2

Subsidies Are the Dominant Price Driver — and a Fiscal Time Bomb

The 86.8% subsidy dampening effect is the single largest determinant of retail fuel prices. Very High subsidy countries sell petrol at an average $0.148/L — barely above the cost of water in some markets. These subsidies insulate consumers from volatility but at immense fiscal cost, and they eliminate the price signal needed for energy efficiency and the energy transition. Countries like Venezuela ($0.016/L) and Iran ($0.040/L) effectively gift fuel to consumers.

Recommendation: Phase universal fuel subsidies toward targeted cash transfers that protect vulnerable households while restoring market price signals. The data shows that even moving from "Very High" to "High" subsidy raises the average price from $0.148 to $0.709 — still far below global averages but with substantially reduced fiscal exposure.
3

Regional Price Divergence Is Accelerating, Not Converging

The Europe–Middle East spread grew from $1.612/L in 2020 to $2.870/L in 2026 — a 78% increase. Europe's high-tax model and the Middle East's high-subsidy model are pulling prices in opposite directions. This divergence complicates cross-border trade, creates arbitrage incentives, and undermines global climate coordination since carbon price signals vary by an order of magnitude.

Recommendation: International coordination bodies (IEA, OPEC, World Bank) should track and publish a Global Fuel Price Divergence Index to raise awareness of widening disparities and their implications for trade and climate policy alignment.
4

High-Income Countries Tax Fuel Heavily But Enjoy Greater Price Stability

High-income countries average 34.7% tax on fuel — 1.6× the Middle-income rate and 1.5× the Low-income rate. Paradoxically, despite (and partly because of) high taxes, these countries experience more predictable pricing: the fuel tax acts as a buffer that dampens crude price swings as a percentage of the final price. Low-income countries, by contrast, see raw commodity volatility pass through more directly to consumers.

Recommendation: Low- and middle-income countries should consider adopting moderate, stable fuel taxes (even at 15–20%) as a price stabilization mechanism — the tax component is fixed and predictable, reducing the percentage impact of crude swings on the final consumer price.
5

The 2020 COVID Shock Reshaped the Baseline; 2022 Locked In a Higher Plateau

Average petrol prices fell to $1.485/L in 2020 (COVID demand destruction) but surged to $2.505/L by 2022 and have never returned to pre-2020 levels. Even post-crisis years (2023–2026) show prices stabilizing around $2.50–2.65/L — roughly 70% above the 2020 trough. The structural upward shift in the Brent baseline (from ~$69/bbl in 2020 to ~$119–124/bbl in 2024–2026) suggests the era of cheap oil that characterized early 2020 was an anomaly, not a new normal.

Recommendation: Budget planners should use the 2023–2026 average Brent of ~$116/bbl as the "new normal" baseline for medium-term fiscal planning, rather than the 2020 trough or even pre-2020 averages. Fuel subsidy budgets in particular should be stress-tested at $130+/bbl.
6

LPG Represents an Underleveraged Policy Tool for Energy Access

LPG averages $1.711/L globally — 25% below petrol — and is the most affordable modern cooking and heating fuel. In Low-income countries, LPG averages just $0.787/L. Targeted LPG subsidy programs could simultaneously reduce deforestation (by displacing charcoal/wood), improve indoor air quality, and provide a bridge fuel toward electrification without the fiscal burden of petrol or diesel subsidies.

Recommendation: International development agencies should prioritize LPG access programs over petrol/diesel subsidy support in low-income countries, as the fiscal cost per unit of energy access delivered is substantially lower and the co-benefits (health, environment) are higher.
