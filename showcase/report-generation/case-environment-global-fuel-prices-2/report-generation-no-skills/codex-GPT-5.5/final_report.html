Data Scope & Integrity Notes

The analysis uses 27,468 complete weekly records covering 84 countries, 7 regions, and 2020-01-06 to 2026-04-06. All prices are from data.csv and are reported in USD per liter, while Brent Crude is USD per barrel.

Dataset caveat: the source brief describes subsidy level as binary High/Low, but the actual file contains four categories: Low, Medium, High, Very High. The policy comparison therefore reports all four categories and separately groups High + Very High against Low when calculating the subsidy price dampening effect.

4. Impact of Subsidies and Taxation on Retail Prices

The subsidy signal is strong in average price levels. Countries classified as High or Very High subsidy averaged $0.45/L for petrol versus $3.45/L in Low-subsidy countries, a -86.8% difference. The equivalent differences were -87.2% for diesel and -86.8% for LPG.

Pass-through is measured two ways: level correlation with Brent and a weekly-change beta showing the average retail USD/L movement associated with a $1/bbl Brent weekly move. High subsidies lower consumer price levels clearly, but pass-through varies by income group and subsidy tier rather than disappearing entirely.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Global crude prices explain much of retail fuel direction. Weekly Brent-price correlations are 1.000 for petrol, 1.000 for diesel, and 1.000 for LPG. Policymakers should treat Brent-linked shock scenarios as central inputs for fuel-price stress testing.
  2. Retail prices smooth short-run Brent shocks. Weekly-change correlations are materially lower than level correlations, showing that taxes, subsidies, administered pricing, inventories, and distribution costs dampen or delay week-to-week transmission.
  3. Subsidies reduce pump-price levels but do not guarantee insulation from volatility. High/Very High subsidy countries paid 86.8% less for petrol than Low-subsidy countries on average. Stabilization mechanisms should therefore be evaluated on both level relief and pass-through beta.
  4. Regional inequality is large and persistent. The highest-priced region, Europe, averaged $3.70/L for petrol compared with $1.23/L in Middle East. Tax calibration and targeted transfers may be more efficient than uniform price controls in high-price regions.
  5. Tax burden differs by income level. Average tax rates range from 21.1% in Middle-income countries to 34.7% in High-income countries. Tax reforms should preserve revenue where fiscal capacity is high while using targeted relief for vulnerable consumers.
  6. Data limitation matters for policy design. The dataset supports price, tax, subsidy, and pass-through analysis, but not household affordability burden or fiscal cost of subsidies. Analysts should pair this report with consumption, budgetary, and income-distribution data before setting subsidy scale.

Recommended policy actions

  • Use automatic stabilizers: index excise taxes or subsidy rates to Brent bands so relief expands during price spikes and retracts during normalization.
  • Target rather than universalize: prioritize low-income consumers, public transport, and essential freight where affordability exposure is highest.
  • Monitor pass-through beta: track weekly retail response per $1/bbl Brent move by fuel and income group; use it as an early-warning indicator for consumer-price pressure.
  • Publish fiscal cost dashboards: combine pump-price relief with budgetary cost to avoid opaque subsidy liabilities during crisis periods.
