Key Volatility Events

COVID-19 Crash (2020): Brent dropped from ~$66/barrel (Jan) to ~$51/barrel (May), a 23% decline. Global average petrol fell from $1.42 to $1.09/L (-23%).
2022 Energy Crisis: Brent surged from ~$95/barrel (Jan) to $130/barrel (Apr-Jun), plateauing at historical highs. Retail petrol rose from ~$2.05 to ~$2.78/L (+36%).
Pass-Through Rate: Every $10/barrel Brent swing translates to approximately $0.21/liter change in retail petrol.

High-subsidy countries pay 79.5% less for petrol than low-subsidy countries ($0.71 vs $3.45/L average). This intervention dramatically buffers consumers from international price volatility.
Tax burden is inverted: Low-subsidy countries collect 37.5% in taxes, while high-subsidy countries collect only ~15%. This suggests subsidies are funded through reduced tax collection rather than explicit transfers.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

1. Crude oil remains the dominant driver of retail fuel prices. The median country-level correlation between Brent Crude and petrol is 0.96, with 90% of countries showing correlations above 0.80. International market movements propagate to consumers within weeks regardless of national policy frameworks.
2. Subsidy programs provide substantial but uneven consumer protection. High-subsidy countries average $0.71/L versus $3.45/L in low-subsidy markets—a 79.5% reduction. However, only 10 countries maintain "Very High" subsidies, leaving most low- and middle-income nations exposed to price volatility.
3. Regional pricing inequality is widening. The Europe-Middle East price gap has grown from $1.61/L (2020) to $2.87/L (2026). European consumers pay approximately 3× Middle Eastern pump prices, reflecting fundamentally different energy policy philosophies.
4. Fuel taxation is regressive across income levels. High-income countries collect 34.7% tax on fuel versus 21-23% in low- and middle-income countries. While this provides fiscal revenue, it disproportionately burdens lower-income households in countries that lack comprehensive social transfer programs.
5. Crisis periods expose subsidy program fragility. Countries with very high subsidies (e.g., Venezuela, Iran) show weak Brent pass-through correlations (r=0.08–0.34), but face chronic supply shortages, smuggling, and fiscal strain during prolonged high-price environments.

Policy Recommendations

For Subsidy-Designing Policymakers:

  • Consider graduated subsidy tiers that scale with crude price levels to preserve fiscal sustainability
  • Target subsidies toward lower-income households rather than universal fuel price controls
  • Implement automatic stabilizers that adjust subsidy levels based on Brent price thresholds

For Tax-Calibrating Authorities:

  • Review regressive fuel tax structures, especially in countries with limited social safety nets
  • Use fuel tax revenues to fund targeted energy assistance programs for vulnerable populations
  • Consider variable tax rates that decline during high-volatility periods to dampen price spikes
