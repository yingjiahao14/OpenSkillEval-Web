Global Fuel Price Overview

Between January 2020 and April 2026, global retail fuel prices underwent dramatic shifts driven by pandemic-era demand destruction, post-recovery supply constraints, and the 2022 energy crisis. The dataset spans 84 countries across 7 regions with 327 weekly observations per country.

Temporal Price Trends & Volatility

The 2020–2026 window captures three distinct market regimes: the COVID-19 demand shock (2020), the post-pandemic surge and 2022 energy crisis, and subsequent stabilization. Brent Crude fell to $47.97/bbl in late April 2020, then climbed steadily to peak near $130/bbl, a level it reached by late 2024 and maintained through early 2026.

Brent–Retail Correlation: The weekly Pearson correlation between Brent Crude and global average retail petrol is r = 1.00, and between Brent and diesel is also r = 1.00. This near-perfect co-movement means that, at the global aggregate level, crude price shocks transmit to the pump with minimal delay or dampening. Country-level deviations from this relationship are driven by subsidies and taxation, explored in Section 4.

Two volatility peaks stand out: 2020 (Brent σ=$13.53/bbl) driven by the COVID demand collapse and OPEC+ responses, and 2022 (Brent σ=$10.85/bbl) reflecting the geopolitical supply disruption. Since 2024, volatility has declined markedly as Brent stabilized in the $118–130 range.

Impact of Subsidies and Taxation on Retail Prices

The dataset classifies countries into four subsidy tiers: Very High (10 countries), High (12 countries), Medium (18 countries), and Low (44 countries). The subsidy dampening effect is the most striking finding in this analysis — high and very-high subsidy regimes reduce retail petrol prices by 79.5% and 95.7% respectively relative to low-subsidy countries.

Price Pass-Through Analysis

The Brent-to-retail pass-through ratio quantifies how much of a crude price change reaches consumers. Low-subsidy countries show near-complete pass-through (1.00), meaning a 10% Brent increase translates to ~10% retail increase. Very-high-subsidy countries show a pass-through of only 0.324, absorbing roughly two-thirds of crude volatility through government intervention.

Fuel Affordability by Income Level

Countries are classified into three income tiers: High (32 countries, 10,464 observations), Middle (29 countries, 9,483 observations), and Low (23 countries, 7,521 observations). Income level is the strongest predictor of retail fuel price after subsidy status.

The affordability paradox: High-income countries pay 2.6× the retail petrol price of low-income countries ($3.55 vs. $1.39), driven primarily by higher taxation (34.7% vs. 22.9%). However, all three income groups exhibit nearly identical price volatility (CV ≈ 17.8%), meaning low-income consumers face proportionally similar price instability but with far less fiscal capacity to absorb shocks.
Tax progressivity across income levels

High-income countries levy an average fuel tax of 34.7%, compared to 21.1% for middle-income and 22.9% for low-income countries. The 13.6 percentage-point gap between high and middle income reflects deliberate fiscal policy: wealthier nations use fuel taxes to fund infrastructure and incentivize decarbonization, while lower-income nations keep tax burdens low to preserve affordability.

Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Crude-to-retail transmission is near-total at the global level

    Weekly Brent–petrol correlation of r=1.00 means consumers in low-subsidy countries bear the full burden of commodity volatility. Retail prices rose 78% over the period, matching Brent exactly. Policymakers in these countries have limited natural insulation from global price shocks without active intervention.

  2. Subsidies are the dominant price-setting mechanism, dwarfing all other factors

    Very-high-subsidy countries deliver retail petrol at $0.15/L vs. $3.45/L in low-subsidy countries — a 95.7% reduction. This effect is stable across years and crude price levels. However, with Brent now sustained above $120/bbl, the fiscal cost of maintaining these subsidies is proportionally higher than during the 2020 trough.

  3. Very-high subsidies successfully decouple retail from crude, but at high fiscal cost

    The pass-through ratio for very-high-subsidy countries is 0.324, meaning only one-third of Brent price movements reach consumers. Medium-subsidy countries show near-complete pass-through (0.99), suggesting that moderate intervention provides minimal price buffering.

  4. The regional price gap is widening, not converging

    The Europe–Middle East spread grew from $1.61/L (2020) to $2.87/L (2026), a 78% increase. This divergence implies that existing policy regimes are amplifying rather than reducing global fuel price inequality across regions.

  5. Low-income countries face the same price volatility as high-income countries

    All three income tiers show a coefficient of variation of ~17.8%. While absolute prices are lower in low-income countries ($1.39/L vs. $3.55/L), proportional instability is identical — and these countries have the least fiscal capacity to implement stabilization mechanisms.

  6. Taxation is progressive across income levels but regressive within them

    High-income countries tax fuel at 34.7% vs. 21–23% in middle/low-income countries. This creates a global progressive structure. However, within any given country, fuel taxation is inherently regressive as lower-income households spend a larger share of income on fuel.

  7. Medium-subsidy regimes offer the weakest policy return

    With a pass-through ratio of 0.99 (virtually complete), medium-subsidy countries incur fiscal expenditure on subsidies while providing almost no consumer price protection. Policymakers in these 18 countries should either deepen subsidy commitments or redirect spending to targeted cash transfers.

Policy Recommendation Summary

For high-subsidy producers: Maintain current subsidy architecture but establish fiscal stress-testing against sustained $130+/bbl scenarios. Begin gradual transition to targeted subsidies (means-tested) to preserve fiscal sustainability.
For medium-subsidy countries: Current spending yields negligible consumer benefit (pass-through ≈ 1.0). Redirect subsidy budgets toward direct income support or strategic petroleum reserves.
For low-subsidy, high-tax countries: Consider implementing automatic stabilizer mechanisms (variable tax rates that flex with crude prices) to smooth retail price volatility during supply shocks, as practiced by some European jurisdictions during the 2022 crisis.
For low-income countries: Prioritize building strategic buffer funds during low-price periods. The identical volatility exposure (CV=17.8%) combined with limited fiscal capacity makes these economies most vulnerable to the next supply shock.

Data Limitations & Methodology Notes

  • Tax percentage shows high within-country weekly variability (e.g., US ranges from 14.1% to 65.0%), which may reflect estimation methodology rather than actual policy changes. Averages should be interpreted with caution.
  • Subsidy classification is binary/categorical (four levels) and static across the period, meaning countries that changed subsidy policy mid-period are not captured.
  • 2026 data covers only Q1 (through April 6), making year-over-year comparisons for 2026 partial.
  • All prices are in USD, meaning exchange rate fluctuations are embedded in the retail price changes observed for non-USD economies.
  • Brent Crude is uniform across countries for a given week, serving as a benchmark rather than the actual crude procurement cost for any specific country.
