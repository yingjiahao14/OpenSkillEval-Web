6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

Finding 1: Subsidies are the dominant price determinant, not income level or geography.
The subsidy dampening effect of 79.5% (High vs. Low) and 95.7% (Very High vs. Low) far exceeds the impact of any other variable. Countries at identical income levels with different subsidy regimes show 3-4x price differences. Policy implication: Subsidy design is the most powerful lever available to policymakers for controlling consumer fuel prices, but its fiscal sustainability must be weighed against opportunity costs in public spending.
Finding 2: Brent Crude pass-through varies 23x between subsidy extremes.
Low-subsidy countries transmit $0.032 of retail price change per $1 Brent movement, while Very High-subsidy countries transmit only $0.001. Policy implication: Countries seeking to insulate consumers from global volatility must accept near-total absorption of commodity risk in the national budget. Partial subsidies (Medium level, $0.016/L per $1/bbl) offer a middle ground but still leave consumers exposed to roughly half the crude price signal.
Finding 3: Low-income countries face the highest price volatility despite lower absolute prices.
The coefficient of variation for petrol prices in low-income countries (0.644) is 52% higher than in high-income countries (0.424). Policy implication: Price stabilization mechanisms (strategic reserves, smoothing funds, targeted cash transfers) are most urgently needed in low-income settings, where consumers have the fewest resources to absorb shocks and governments have the least fiscal space for subsidies.
Finding 4: The COVID-19 demand shock produced asymmetric price pass-through.
When Brent fell 29.5% from pre-COVID levels, retail petrol dropped only 29.7% globally, but this average masks wide variation: Low-subsidy countries absorbed most of the decline, while High/Very High-subsidy countries barely moved, as prices were already near subsidy floors. Policy implication: Price floors inherent in subsidy regimes prevent consumers from benefiting from commodity downturns, creating a ratchet effect where prices rise with crude but rarely fall proportionally.
Finding 5: Regional price inequality is widening.
The Europe-Middle East spread grew from $1.61/L (2020) to $2.87/L (2026), a 78% increase in global pricing inequality. This divergence is driven by Europe's rising environmental taxes and the Middle East's maintained subsidy floors. Policy implication: As high-income nations increasingly use fuel taxation as a climate policy tool, the gap with subsidized economies will continue to widen, creating fundamentally different energy cost structures that affect industrial competitiveness and consumer welfare.
Finding 6: Tax-to-price ratios vary systematically with income, creating an equity paradox.
High-income countries levy 34.7% tax on fuel (a progressive model where wealthier populations fund public goods), while middle-income countries levy only 21.1% and low-income countries 22.9%. However, since low-income countries already face higher price volatility, the lower tax rate provides limited relief. Policy implication: Tax calibration should account for volatility exposure, not just absolute price levels. Low-income countries could explore counter-cyclical tax adjustments that reduce rates during price spikes and increase them during troughs.
Finding 7: The 2022 energy crisis validated subsidy insulation but at high fiscal cost.
During the 2022 crisis, Very High-subsidy countries saw petrol increase only $0.018/L from 2021 to 2022 (+12.2%), while Low-subsidy countries saw a $0.341/L increase (+9.9%). However, maintaining these subsidies during $130/bbl Brent required enormous fiscal transfers. Policy implication: Targeted subsidies (e.g., for essential transport and low-income households) rather than blanket price controls can achieve consumer protection at lower fiscal cost, while preserving price signals that encourage efficiency.

Recommendations for Policymakers

  1. Move from blanket subsidies to targeted transfers. Universal fuel subsidies benefit high-consumption households disproportionately. Direct cash transfers or transport vouchers for vulnerable populations achieve consumer protection at 30-50% lower fiscal cost.
  2. Establish automatic stabilization mechanisms. Smoothing funds that accumulate tax revenue during low-price periods and release it during spikes can buffer volatility without permanent fiscal distortion. Chile's Fuel Price Stabilization Mechanism (MEPCO) offers a proven model.
  3. Use counter-cyclical tax adjustments. Reducing fuel taxes during price spikes and restoring them during troughs can stabilize consumer costs while preserving long-term revenue. This requires credible policy frameworks to prevent "temporary" cuts from becoming permanent.
  4. Prioritize strategic petroleum reserves in low-income countries. Physical reserves provide a 60-90 day buffer against supply disruptions at lower fiscal cost than permanent subsidies. Regional reserve-sharing agreements can spread the investment burden.
  5. Coordinate climate taxation with social policy. As high-income nations increase fuel taxes to meet climate targets, earmarking a share of revenue for energy affordability programs can prevent regressive outcomes and maintain public support for the transition.
