Executive Summary

Retail fuel prices in the dataset move almost one-for-one with the Brent crude benchmark in timing, but policy regimes strongly alter the level and magnitude of consumer exposure. The global average petrol price across all countries and weeks was $2.28/L, compared with $2.13/L for diesel and $1.71/L for LPG.

Data integrity note: the task brief describes subsidy level as High/Low, but data.csv contains four observed categories: High, Low, Medium, and Very High. This report presents all four categories and explicitly reports High-vs-Low comparisons where required.

1. Global Fuel Price Overview

Across 84 countries, Brent averaged $106.69/bbl, with weekly values ranging from $47.97 on 2020-04-27 to $130.00 on 2022-03-28. Retail fuel price ranges are wide, reflecting both market exposure and fiscal policy: petrol spans $0.01–$6.78/L.

4. Impact of Subsidies and Taxation on Retail Prices

Subsidy categories show clear price separation. Low-subsidy countries averaged $3.45/L for petrol; high-subsidy countries averaged $0.71/L, a 79.5% lower pump price. Very-high-subsidy countries averaged $0.15/L, or 95.7% below low-subsidy countries.

Petrol pass-through slope by subsidy level

Pass-through is where the policy effect is most visible: each $1/bbl Brent increase is associated with a $0.0324/L increase in low-subsidy average petrol, compared with $0.0067/L in high-subsidy markets and $0.0014/L in very-high-subsidy markets. That implies a 79.4% lower pass-through slope for high subsidies and 95.7% lower for very-high subsidies versus low-subsidy markets.

5. Fuel Affordability by Income Level

Income segmentation shows that high-income markets have the highest tax-inclusive prices, while low- and middle-income groups have lower prices and lower average tax percentages. High-income countries averaged $3.55/L petrol and 34.7% tax, versus low-income countries at $1.39/L and 22.9%.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Global crude movements dominate retail timing. Weekly Brent and average retail petrol levels correlate at 1.000; weekly changes remain tightly linked at r=0.995. Price-stabilization policy therefore needs automatic triggers tied to crude benchmarks, not annual adjustments.
  2. COVID-era prices were the low point; 2022 reset the baseline. Brent averaged $69.44/bbl in 2020 and $117.12/bbl in 2022, while average petrol rose from $1.48/L to $2.51/L. Temporary relief calibrated to demand shocks should unwind as crude recovers.
  3. Subsidy design materially changes consumer exposure. High-subsidy countries averaged $0.71/L for petrol versus $3.45/L in low-subsidy countries, a 79.5% lower price level. Petrol pass-through slope was 0.0067 $/L per $/bbl in high-subsidy markets versus 0.0324 in low-subsidy markets.
  4. Very-high subsidies buffer most, but create very low pump prices. Very-high-subsidy countries averaged $0.15/L and showed a 95.7% lower petrol pass-through slope than low-subsidy countries. Analysts should pair such buffers with explicit fiscal cost tracking, which is not available in this dataset.
  5. Regional inequality widened with higher crude prices. The annual regional petrol spread expanded from $1.61/L in 2020 to $2.87/L in 2026. Harmonized tax floors or targeted transfers may reduce cross-border distortions without suppressing all price signals.
  6. High-income markets carry the highest tax-inclusive prices. High-income countries averaged $3.55/L petrol and 34.7% tax, compared with $1.39/L and 22.9% in low-income countries. Equity analysis should separate affordability from pump-price level alone.

Methodology & Data Limitations

All statistics are computed directly from /app/benchmark/data.csv. Dates are parsed as weekly observations. Global retail time series are country-week averages; Brent is de-duplicated by week because it is a global benchmark repeated across countries. Correlations are Pearson correlations. Pass-through slope is an ordinary least squares equivalent slope of segment-average retail price on Brent crude price, expressed as USD/liter per USD/barrel. No external data or fabricated estimates are used.

Limitations: the dataset does not report subsidy fiscal cost, fuel consumption volumes, household income, exchange-rate uncertainty, or explicit policy dates. The tax percentage varies weekly within countries, so tax results should be interpreted as estimated tax share rather than statutory tax rates.
