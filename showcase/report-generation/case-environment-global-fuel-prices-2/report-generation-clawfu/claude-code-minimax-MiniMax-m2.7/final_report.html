Key Volatility Events

COVID-19 Demand Shock (Q1–Q2 2020)
Brent collapsed from ~$70 to ~$47 (–33%), while retail petrol dropped only 18% (from $1.48 to ~$1.20 average). High-subsidy countries insulated consumers entirely, with Brent-to-petrol correlation breaking down to near zero.
2022 Energy Crisis
Brent surged to $130/bbl (April–May 2022), a 67% increase from 2020 lows. Weekly volatility reached σ=10.85. Retail petrol rose only 69% in the same period globally, but the pass-through varied dramatically by subsidy regime.
Brent-to-Retail Correlation Analysis: Overall Pearson correlation between Brent and retail petrol is 0.26—surprisingly weak given the commodity nature of fuel. However, this varies significantly by subsidy level: high-subsidy countries show r=0.65 correlation (government absorbs shocks), while low-subsidy countries show r=0.55. The lower correlation in low-subsidy markets suggests other factors (tax policy, currency fluctuations, retail margins) play substantial roles.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

1 Subsidies remain the dominant policy lever for price stabilization. High-subsidy countries maintained an 79.4% price dampening effect throughout the 2020–2026 period, regardless of Brent crude fluctuations. However, this comes at significant fiscal cost and creates market distortions that discourage efficiency improvements.
2 Global fuel pricing inequality is widening. The regional price spread grew from $1.61/L in 2020 to $2.87/L in 2026, driven by divergent policy responses to the 2022 energy crisis. Middle East and some African nations kept prices low through subsidies, while European nations allowed prices to rise with markets.
3 Tax policy in high-income countries acts as an automatic stabilizer. With an average 34.7% tax burden, high-income countries can modulate pump prices through tax adjustments (as seen during COVID-19 relief periods). Low-income countries' lower tax bases (21-23%) provide less fiscal room for such interventions.
4 Brent crude explains only 26% of retail price variance. The weak correlation (r=0.26) indicates that fuel pricing is predominantly determined by national policy choices rather than international market forces. Policymakers should focus on subsidy design and tax calibration over which they have direct control.
5 Low-income consumers face disproportionate volatility exposure. Despite lower absolute prices, low-income countries show similar absolute price ranges to middle-income countries ($3.17 vs $3.82), representing a higher proportion of household expenditure. Subsidy programs in these countries should consider volatility protection mechanisms.

Policy Recommendations

For Low-Income Countries: Implement targeted subsidy mechanisms that protect vulnerable populations while preserving price signals for economic efficiency. Consider volatility smoothing funds funded by windfall gains during low-price periods.
For Middle-Income Countries: Gradual tax-based pricing with automatic stabilizers tied to Brent price thresholds. This provides fiscal buffers while maintaining market alignment.
For High-Income Countries: Use tax modulation proactively during crises rather than reactive price controls. Maintain minimum retail price floors to discourage consumption while ensuring affordability for low-income commuters.
