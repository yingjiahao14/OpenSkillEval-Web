1. Global Fuel Price Overview

High-level summary statistics for petrol, diesel, and LPG prices across the full 2020–2026 period, including overall averages, ranges, and the trajectory of Brent Crude as a baseline.

Over the six-year observation window, the global average retail petrol price stood at $2.282/L, with diesel at $2.134/L and LPG at $1.711/L. Brent Crude — the international benchmark — averaged $106.69/bbl, trading in a wide band between $47.97 and $130.00.

Key observation: Retail petrol prices ranged from a trough of $0.010/L recorded in Venezuela (2020-01-06) — reflecting extreme administered pricing — to a peak of $6.779/L in Hong Kong (2026-03-16), a 678× spread. This extreme range reflects divergent national pricing policies, from heavily subsidized administered-price regimes to fully market-based systems with high taxation.

2. Temporal Price Trends & Volatility

Analyze weekly and yearly price trends for each fuel type, identify periods of peak volatility, and quantify the correlation between Brent Crude movements and retail price changes.

The global retail fuel market between 2020 and 2026 was shaped by three distinct regimes: the COVID-19 demand collapse (Q1–Q2 2020), the post-pandemic recovery and 2022 energy crisis, and a prolonged high-plateau period (2023–2026) during which Brent Crude consistently averaged above $120/bbl. Retail prices tracked these swings with remarkable fidelity: the Pearson correlation between Brent and aggregate retail petrol reached r = 1.0000, indicating an essentially deterministic relationship at the aggregate level.

Volatility analysis: The largest weekly petrol price declines occurred during the COVID-19 crash of March–April 2020, with week-over-week drops of −6.85% (16 Mar 2020) and −6.84% (9 Mar 2020) as Brent Crude plummeted from $71 to below $48/bbl. The recovery was equally sharp: average annual petrol prices surged +68.7% from 2020 to 2022 ($1.485/L to $2.505/L), reflecting the combined impact of demand recovery and the 2022 energy supply shock.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

Synthesize the most important insights from the analysis into actionable findings, including recommendations for policymakers on subsidy design, tax calibration, and price stabilization mechanisms.

  1. Brent Crude is the dominant price driver across all markets. The near-perfect Pearson correlation (r = 1.0000) between Brent Crude and average retail petrol prices confirms that global benchmark prices dictate retail fuel costs regardless of region, income level, or subsidy regime. Policymakers should treat crude oil price forecasting as the single most important input to fuel price projections and fiscal planning.
  2. Subsidies reduce price levels but do not stabilize prices. High-subsidy countries pay ~80% less at the pump than low-subsidy countries ($0.709 vs. $3.451/L), yet both groups exhibit near-identical Brent-to-retail correlation (r ≈ 1.0). Subsidies are a blunt instrument for consumer protection — they lower the baseline but leave households fully exposed to global price swings. To achieve genuine price stability, governments should complement subsidies with stabilization funds, strategic petroleum reserves, or automatic tax adjustment mechanisms that move inversely with crude oil prices.
  3. The regional price gap is structural and persistent. European consumers pay $3.702/L while Middle Eastern consumers pay $1.226/L — a 3.0× difference driven overwhelmingly by taxation policy. The regional spread reached $2.870/L in 2026, narrowing only marginally over the observation period. Convergence is unlikely without deliberate policy harmonization.
  4. High-income economies impose significantly higher fuel taxes. The average tax rate of 34.7% in high-income countries is 1.5× the 22.9% rate in low-income countries. This tax gap serves dual purposes in developed economies: revenue generation for infrastructure investment and a de facto carbon price signal. Developing economies that keep taxes low to protect household budgets forgo both fiscal revenue and environmental incentives — but face real political economy constraints in raising fuel taxes.
  5. The COVID-19 demand shock produced the most extreme price dislocation of the period. Weekly petrol prices fell by −6.85% and −6.84% in consecutive weeks of March 2020 — the largest declines in the dataset. Yet the recovery was equally dramatic: average petrol prices surged +68.7% from 2020 to 2022. The speed and magnitude of these swings highlight the critical importance of maintaining fiscal buffers and flexible policy mechanisms that can respond to extreme market conditions.
  6. Energy affordability is a multi-dimensional challenge requiring targeted solutions. While low-income countries have lower absolute pump prices, their consumers face higher price volatility relative to income. The coefficient of variation is highest in low-income contexts (64.4%), meaning a given global price swing consumes a larger share of household budgets. Policymakers in these markets should consider targeted cash transfers or tiered pricing over broad-based fuel subsidies, which the data show are regressive (benefiting higher-consumption households disproportionately) and fiscally unsustainable at ~80% of market price.
Overarching recommendation: The evidence strongly supports a policy framework that (1) decouples consumer protection objectives from blanket fuel subsidies — which are regressive, fiscally costly, and ineffective at price stabilization; (2) adopts price-smoothing mechanisms such as automatic excise tax adjustments calibrated to move inversely with crude oil prices; and (3) redirects subsidy savings toward targeted direct transfers to vulnerable households. This three-pronged approach preserves the price signal for efficient consumption while protecting those most affected by fuel price volatility.

Data Notes & Limitations

  • Dataset: 27,468 weekly observations across 84 countries, 2020-01-06 – 2026-04-06. Source: Kaggle (belbino/global-fuel-prices-20202026), aggregated from EIA, OPEC, World Bank, and national energy ministries.
  • Currency: All prices normalized to USD/liter using historical spot exchange rates. Brent Crude is the global benchmark expressed in USD/barrel.
  • Tax percentage: Shows significant week-to-week variability within individual countries (ranging from ~15% to ~65%). This may reflect estimation methodology or compositional changes in the underlying data rather than actual policy shifts. Individual-week tax figures should be treated as indicative rather than precise.
  • Subsidy level: Observed with four categories in the data — Very High, High, Medium, and Low — rather than the two levels (High/Low) documented in the schema. All four are reported where relevant. The analysis treats "Very High" and "High" collectively as "High-subsidy" for the dampening calculation, and "Low" as "Low-subsidy."
  • Extreme values: Entries at or near $0.010/L reflect heavily subsidized administered-price regimes (notably Venezuela) and should be interpreted in the context of those countries' specific pricing policies rather than as market outcomes.
  • Correlation caveat: The near-perfect Pearson correlations (r ≈ 1.0) between Brent Crude and aggregate retail prices indicate a very tight relationship at the global mean level. Country-level correlations, particularly for heavily subsidized markets, may show more divergence. The aggregate correlation reflects the averaging effect across 84 countries.
