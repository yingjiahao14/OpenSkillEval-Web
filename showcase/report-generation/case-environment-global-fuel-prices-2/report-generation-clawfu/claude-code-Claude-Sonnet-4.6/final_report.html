Section 04 Impact of Subsidies and Taxation on Retail Prices

This is the most policy-critical section. Government intervention via subsidies dramatically severs the crude-to-retail price transmission — Very High-subsidy nations pay 95.7% less for petrol than Low-subsidy nations on average.

ⓘ  Subsidy Dampening Effect: Low-subsidy countries averaged $3.45/L. High-subsidy averaged $0.71/L — a 79.5% reduction. Very High-subsidy (Venezuela, Iran, Libya, Algeria, Iraq, Kuwait, Nigeria) averaged $0.15/L — a 95.7% reduction. Meanwhile, Low-subsidy nations show a Brent correlation of r=0.997 vs. r=0.741 for High-subsidy countries, confirming structural price insulation.

Section 06 Key Findings & Policy Implications

Synthesis of the six most actionable insights from this analysis, targeted at energy policy practitioners designing subsidy programs, fuel tax regimes, and price stabilization mechanisms.

1
Core Finding

Retail fuel prices are almost entirely determined by global crude movements

The week-over-week % change in Brent Crude explains 99.6% of global average retail petrol price variance (r = 0.996). For low-subsidy countries — the majority of the dataset — crude-to-retail pass-through is near-instantaneous. Policy interventions that target retail prices without addressing crude market exposure (e.g., temporary price caps) face severe fiscal pressure during commodity upswings.

2
Policy Lever

Subsidies provide powerful but fiscally costly price insulation

High-subsidy countries paid 79.5% less for petrol than Low-subsidy equivalents over the period ($0.71 vs $3.45/L). Very High-subsidy nations (Venezuela, Iran, Libya, Iraq, Algeria, Kuwait, Nigeria, Qatar, Saudi Arabia) paid just $0.15/L — 95.7% below market. However, this insulation comes at extreme fiscal cost, particularly during high-Brent environments (2022–2026 avg $119–124/bbl). The Brent–retail correlation in High-subsidy countries falls to 0.741, confirming genuine insulation, but subsidy expenditures scale non-linearly with crude price.

3
Core Finding

The COVID-19 shock revealed structural asymmetry in price transmission

Global Brent fell from ~$66/bbl (Jan 2020) to $47.97/bbl (Apr 2020) — a 27% drop — yet average retail petrol fell only from ~$2.28/L to ~$1.48/L, a 35% decline partly reflecting lagged pass-through and sticky tax floors. The 2021–2022 recovery phase saw Brent rebound 145% from trough to peak ($130/bbl), with retail prices responding proportionally — confirming that downside relief is shared, but upside shocks are fully transmitted in low-subsidy markets.

4
Policy Lever

High-income nations use taxation, not subsidies, as their primary fiscal tool — at 2.5× the rate

High-income countries average a 34.7% fuel tax rate versus 21.1% in Middle-income and 22.9% in Low-income nations. Low-subsidy countries apply 37.5% tax rates on average, compared to 15% in High-subsidy nations. This reveals a structural trade-off: wealthy nations use fuel taxes for revenue and demand management (accepting high retail prices), while developing nations rely on subsidies for consumer protection (accepting fiscal burden). Middle-income nations are caught between both pressures.

5
Core Finding

Regional pricing inequality has widened materially since 2020

The spread between Europe (highest avg regional petrol) and Middle East (lowest) grew from $1.61/L in 2020 to $2.87/L in 2026 — a 78% widening. Europe's proximity to Russian supply disruptions and its low-subsidy, high-tax structure made it the most exposed region to the 2022 energy crisis (+78% price rise 2020→2022). Meanwhile, Middle East nations absorbed the same crude shock with a price rise of only $0.55/L (+69% in absolute terms but from a much lower base). This divergence underscores how policy design, not geography, is the primary determinant of consumer exposure.

6
Risk / Recommendation

Sustained high crude prices (2023–2026) make current subsidy architectures financially unsustainable

With Brent averaging $104–124/bbl since 2023, the implicit fiscal cost of Very High-subsidy regimes is at historical highs. Nations in this tier (many Low and Middle-income) face a compounding fiscal burden: crude prices are elevated, subsidy expenditure scales directly with the gap between market price and administered price, and exchange rate pressures amplify USD-denominated import costs. Policymakers should consider transitioning from blanket price subsidies to targeted cash-transfer schemes, which deliver equivalent consumer protection at a fraction of the fiscal cost and allow energy prices to send efficient market signals.

Data & Methodology Notes
  • Source dataset: 27,468 weekly observations across 84 countries, Jan 2020 – Apr 2026 (Kaggle: belbino/global-fuel-prices-20202026)
  • All prices normalized to USD/Liter using historical spot exchange rates at time of collection
  • Brent Crude is a global benchmark (same value per week across all countries); retail deviations reflect local policy and market structure
  • Subsidy level is a categorical variable (Very High / High / Medium / Low) assigned at country level; it does not vary week-to-week
  • Tax percentage shows week-to-week variation within countries, likely reflecting estimation methodology; averages are used throughout
  • All correlations computed on weekly % changes to remove level non-stationarity; Pearson r reported
  • No missing values were detected in any column across the full dataset
