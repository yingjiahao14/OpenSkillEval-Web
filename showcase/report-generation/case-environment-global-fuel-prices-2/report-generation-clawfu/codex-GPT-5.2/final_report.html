Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Brent-to-retail co-movement is extremely tight at the global mean: weekly Pearson correlation is 0.999986 (petrol) and 0.999983 (diesel). Implication: absent strong interventions, retail prices track Brent closely.
  2. Volatility peaks in 2020: Brent volatility (std of weekly % changes) is 0.02723 in 2020—higher than any other year. Implication: stabilization tools should be stress-tested for abrupt demand shocks.
  3. Regional inequality widens over time: petrol regional spread rises from 1.612 USD/L (2020) to 2.870 USD/L (2026). Implication: global crises can amplify cross-region affordability gaps.
  4. Subsidy regime dominates price levels: high-subsidy group averages 0.996 USD/L petrol vs 3.451 USD/L in low-subsidy group (~71.1% lower). Implication: subsidy design materially changes the consumer price level.
  5. Pass-through is much lower in high-subsidy group (proxy): petrol slope is 0.00935 USD/L per 1 USD/bbl Brent vs 0.03235 in low-subsidy. Implication: price caps/subsidies can dampen sensitivity to Brent, at fiscal cost.
Policy actions suggested by the observed patterns (data-driven): (1) Calibrate taxes counter-cyclically to reduce consumer volatility during crises; (2) if subsidies are used, pair them with targeting (income/sector) to avoid large, persistent price gaps; (3) use automatic stabilizers with transparent triggers (e.g., moving-average Brent bands) to reduce discretionary delays.
Note: This dataset supports robust descriptive inference; it does not identify causal effects of subsidy/tax changes without additional instruments (policy timing, fiscal data, or counterfactual controls).
