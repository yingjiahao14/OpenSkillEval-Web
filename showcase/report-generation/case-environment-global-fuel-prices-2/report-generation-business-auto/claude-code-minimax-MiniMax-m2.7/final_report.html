Volatility Analysis

The 2020–2026 period witnessed significant price volatility. Key observations:

  • COVID-19 Shock (Q1-Q2 2020): Brent crashed from ~$65/bbl to ~$52/bbl in April 2020, representing a 20% decline. Retail petrol followed with a lag, dropping from ~$1.42/L to ~$1.09/L.
  • 2022 Energy Crisis: Brent peaked at $130/bbl in April-May 2022 (geopolitical tensions). Global petrol averages surged to ~$2.78/L, creating significant consumer pressure.
  • 2023 Stabilization: Prices moderated as supply chains adjusted, with Brent settling around $100-110/bbl.
  • 2024-2026: Brent resumed an upward trajectory, averaging ~$119/bbl in 2024-2025 and reaching ~$124/bbl in early 2026.

Energy Equity Analysis

Key Finding: Low-income countries experienced a 78% price increase from 2020 to 2026 in absolute terms ($0.90/L to $1.61/L), while High-income countries saw a 78% increase ($2.31/L to $4.11/L). However, as a percentage of per-capita income, the burden on low-income countries is substantially higher.

The data suggests that low-income countries face a paradox: they pay lower absolute fuel prices but bear a higher relative economic burden. Additionally, low-income countries have less fiscal capacity to maintain subsidy programs during crude price spikes, forcing painful price adjustments.

Key Findings & Policy Implications

1. Moderate Global Brent-to-Retail Correlation (0.26)

Retail petrol prices show only a moderate correlation with Brent Crude, indicating that national policies—taxation and subsidies—substantially decouple local prices from global crude movements. Policymakers should recognize that consumer prices are highly policy-dependent.

2. Subsidies Provide Significant but Variable Protection

High/very-high subsidy countries buffer consumers effectively, paying 79% less than low-subsidy countries on average. However, medium-subsidy countries show the highest Brent correlation (0.78), suggesting inconsistent protection that may fail precisely when needed most.

3. Regional Price Inequality Persists

The spread between highest (Europe: $3.70/L) and lowest (Middle East: $1.23/L) regional averages is $2.48/L. This inequality has remained relatively stable, indicating structural factors beyond crude prices that policymakers must address.

4. High-Income Countries Bear Higher Tax Burdens

High-income countries average 34.7% fuel taxation versus 22.9% for low-income countries. This inverted structure means high-income nations use fuel taxes as demand management tools, while low-income nations forgo this fiscal instrument.

5. 2022 Energy Crisis Revealed Policy Gaps

The 2022 price spike to $130/bbl Brent exposed vulnerabilities: medium-subsidy countries saw prices surge alongside crude, while very-high subsidy nations maintained price stability. Policymakers should evaluate whether their subsidy framework provides consistent protection across price scenarios.

Policy Recommendations

  1. Establish Volatility Stabilization Mechanisms: Consider automatic price bands or levies that activate when Brent exceeds thresholds, preventing sudden consumer shocks.
  2. Differentiate Subsidy Strategies by Income Group: Low-income countries may benefit from targeted subsidies for transport/vulnerable sectors rather than broad fuel subsidies, which often disproportionately benefit wealthier consumers.
  3. Monitor Medium-Subsidy Consistency: Countries with medium subsidy levels show the highest correlation with Brent movements, suggesting their interventions may be ad-hoc. Formalize automatic stabilizers.
  4. Leverage Tax Policy Equitably: High-income countries can use fuel taxation for both demand management and revenue generation; low-income countries should consider graduated tax structures that protect essential transport while capturing windfall revenues.

Data Notes & Methodology

  • Dataset: 84 countries, weekly data, January 2020 – April 2026 (27,468 observations)
  • Source: Kaggle dataset (belbino/global-fuel-prices-20202026), aggregated from energy ministries, EIA, OPEC, and World Bank
  • Currency: All prices normalized to USD per liter for cross-country comparability
  • Subsidy Classification: Binary categorical (High/Low) in source, expanded to Very High/High/Medium/Low based on data distribution
  • Tax Percentage: Varies week-to-week within countries, reflecting estimation methodology rather than policy changes

This analysis is based exclusively on observed data. No values have been estimated or projected beyond the data range (through April 2026).
