Impact of Subsidies and Taxation on Retail Prices

The requested High-vs-Low subsidy comparison shows a clear dampening effect: average petrol in High-subsidy countries was $0.71/L versus $3.45/L in Low-subsidy countries, a 79.5% reduction relative to Low-subsidy prices. Average three-fuel retail price was 79.6% lower on the same basis.

Pass-through result: Low-subsidy petrol prices rose by approximately $0.032/L for each $1/bbl Brent movement, compared with $0.007/L in High-subsidy countries. This implies a 79.4% lower Brent-to-petrol slope under High subsidy classification. Very High subsidy countries had an even lower slope of $0.001/L, but the original task specification only named High and Low categories.

Key Findings & Policy Implications

The evidence points to strong global crude pass-through, substantial regional price inequality, and materially lower price levels and pass-through in subsidized regimes. Policy design should focus on targeted dampening rather than broad untargeted price suppression.

  1. Crude movements dominate temporal retail trends. Weekly Brent-to-retail correlations are 1.000 for petrol, 1.000 for diesel, and 1.000 for LPG. Price stabilization rules should therefore be calibrated to explicit Brent movement triggers.
  2. COVID and 2022 shocks transmitted almost one-for-one in global averages. During the 2020 trough, Brent declined -27.0% and petrol declined -27.2%; from trough to 2022 peak, Brent rose +171.0% and petrol rose +171.6%. Automatic smoothing mechanisms would have been most valuable during these two intervals.
  3. High subsidies materially dampened consumer prices and pass-through. High-subsidy petrol averaged $0.71/L versus $3.45/L under Low subsidies, and the Brent-to-petrol slope was 79.4% lower. Policymakers should preserve dampening capacity for shock periods, while avoiding permanent broad subsidies where fiscal space is constrained.
  4. Tax systems amplify cross-country price dispersion. Low-subsidy countries averaged 37.5% tax versus 15.1% in High-subsidy countries. Tax calibration should distinguish between normal-market revenue collection and crisis-period consumer protection.
  5. Regional inequality widened over time. The regional petrol spread increased from $1.61/L in 2020 to $2.87/L in 2026, with Europe consistently highest and the Middle East lowest. Regional benchmarking can help identify where retail prices reflect policy choices more than commodity costs.
  6. Affordability analysis requires additional household data. High-income countries show the highest nominal prices and taxes, but `data.csv` lacks income, expenditure, or purchasing-power-adjusted consumption data. For equity targeting, combine this price dataset with household fuel expenditure shares before redesigning subsidies.
