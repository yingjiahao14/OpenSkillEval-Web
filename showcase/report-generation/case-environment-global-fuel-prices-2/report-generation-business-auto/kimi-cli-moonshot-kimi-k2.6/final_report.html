1. Global Fuel Price Overview

Across 27,468 weekly observations from 84 countries, global mean retail prices stand at $2.282/L for petrol, $2.134/L for diesel, and $1.711/L for LPG. Brent Crude averaged $106.69/barrel over the period, with a range from $47.97 (early COVID crash) to $130.00 (2022 energy crisis peak).

The dataset exhibits no missing values across any of the 10 columns, providing a complete panel for time-series and cross-sectional analysis.

2. Temporal Price Trends & Volatility

The weekly global average time series reveals three distinct phases: (1) the COVID-19 demand collapse in Q1–Q2 2020, (2) the post-pandemic recovery and 2022 energy crisis, and (3) the 2023–2026 stabilization at elevated levels.

Volatility Events

  • COVID Shock (Feb–May 2020): Brent swung from $48.0 to $71.6; retail petrol moved from $1.024 to $1.529.
  • 2022 Crisis (Jan–Jul): Brent reached $130.0; retail petrol peaked at $2.784.
  • Peak-to-Trough Ratio: The 2022 petrol peak was 2.72× the 2020 trough.

4. Impact of Subsidies and Taxation on Retail Prices

Subsidy level is the single most powerful predictor of retail price levels. Countries with Low subsidy regimes pay an average of $3.45/L for petrol, while High-subsidy countries pay $0.71/L — a 79.5% price dampening effect. Very-High-subsidy nations push this even further, averaging just $0.15/L for petrol.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

1. Brent Crude is the overwhelming driver of global retail fuel prices.
Weekly correlations exceed 0.999 for petrol, diesel, and LPG. Policymakers should treat crude price forecasts as the baseline scenario for retail cost projections; domestic policy levers (tax, subsidy) determine the level and spread, not the direction.
2. Subsidies deliver massive price dampening but do not eliminate volatility pass-through.
High-subsidy countries cut retail petrol prices by ~79% relative to Low-subsidy peers, yet their Brent–retail correlation remains 0.999. This means consumers in subsidized markets still experience proportional swings — just from a lower base. Recommendation: Pair subsidy caps with strategic reserves or price-smoothing funds to dampen volatility, not just absolute levels.
3. Regional inequality in fuel pricing is widening.
The spread between the most expensive region (Europe) and the cheapest (Middle East) grew from $1.61/L in 2020 to $2.87/L in 2026. Divergent tax and subsidy trajectories are amplifying cross-border energy cost disparities, with implications for trade competitiveness and energy equity.
4. High-income countries tax fuel heavily; Low-income countries rely more on subsidies.
Average tax shares are 34.7% in High-income countries versus 21.1–22.9% in Low/Middle-income groups. Conversely, 75% of Very-High-subsidy observations occur in Low/Middle-income nations. Recommendation: Low-income policymakers should evaluate targeted cash transfers as an alternative to blanket fuel subsidies, which disproportionately benefit higher-consumption wealthier households and strain fiscal balances.
5. The 2022 energy crisis proved that even robust subsidy regimes face fiscal limits.
When Brent spiked to $130/bbl, global retail petrol peaked at $2.784/L — a 2.7× multiple over the 2020 COVID trough. Countries with rigid subsidy frameworks saw ballooning fiscal deficits; those with flexible tax adjustments maintained revenue neutrality but exposed consumers to sharper shocks. Recommendation: Adopt automatic stabilizer mechanisms (e.g., variable excise duties that contract when crude rises and expand when it falls) to balance fiscal sustainability with consumer protection.
6. Diesel and LPG track Brent as tightly as petrol, but policy treatment differs.
Diesel and LPG show virtually identical Brent correlations to petrol, yet LPG often receives lighter tax treatment (lower average tax share in several regions). Policymakers should ensure that subsidy and tax design accounts for fuel-substitution effects; under-taxing LPG relative to petrol can distort household and industrial energy choices.
