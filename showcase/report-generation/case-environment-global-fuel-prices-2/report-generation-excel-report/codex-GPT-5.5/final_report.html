2. Temporal Price Trends & Volatility

Temporal tracking is the dominant pattern: the weekly global retail series moves nearly one-for-one with Brent in levels. Pearson correlations with Brent were 1.000 for petrol, 1.000 for diesel, and 1.000 for LPG. Correlations of week-over-week percentage changes were also high at 0.996, 0.995, and 0.991, respectively.

COVID-19 demand shock

In 2020, Brent ranged from $47.97 to $99.49/bbl, while global average petrol ranged from $1.02 to $2.13/L. The lowest Brent and petrol observations both occurred on 2020-04-27.

2022 energy crisis

In 2022, Brent averaged $117.12/bbl and peaked at $130.00/bbl. Petrol averaged $2.51/L and peaked at $2.78/L on 2022-04-18.

4. Impact of Subsidies and Taxation on Retail Prices

The subsidy signal is strong. Countries classified as High subsidy averaged $0.71/L for petrol versus $3.45/L in Low subsidy countries, a -79.5% difference. Diesel and LPG showed similar High-vs-Low gaps of -80.0% and -79.5%.

Pass-through also declined with subsidy intensity. Low-subsidy petrol prices moved $0.324/L for each $10/bbl Brent change, compared with $0.067/L for High-subsidy countries and $0.014/L for Very High-subsidy countries.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Retail prices were almost perfectly synchronized with Brent in this dataset.Weekly global average petrol and diesel level correlations with Brent were 1.000 and 1.000; week-over-week movement correlations remained very high at 0.996 and 0.995.
  2. 2020 was the highest volatility year, while 2022 delivered the highest absolute Brent shock.Brent fell to $47.97/bbl on 2020-04-27, then peaked at $130.00/bbl on 2022-03-28; global average petrol peaked three weeks later at $2.78/L.
  3. Subsidies materially dampened consumer price exposure.High-subsidy countries averaged 79.5% lower petrol prices than low-subsidy countries; grouping Very High + High versus Medium + Low implies an 84.5% lower average petrol price.
  4. The pass-through gradient is policy-sensitive.Low-subsidy petrol prices rose $0.324/L for each $10/bbl Brent increase, versus $0.067/L in High-subsidy and $0.014/L in Very High-subsidy settings.
  5. Regional price inequality remained large and persistent.Europe was the highest-price region every year and the Middle East the lowest; the annual regional petrol spread widened from $1.61/L in 2020 to $2.87/L in 2026 YTD.
  6. Tax calibration differs sharply by income classification.High-income countries averaged a 34.7% fuel tax share versus 21.1% in middle-income and 22.9% in low-income countries.

Policy implications

Subsidy design: targeted and rule-based subsidies can materially dampen crude-price pass-through, but the dataset does not contain fiscal cost, so subsidy sustainability should be evaluated before scaling intervention.

Tax calibration: high-tax regions and high-income countries have greater retail price cushions but can amplify consumer price levels. Variable excise mechanisms or temporary tax smoothing may reduce crisis-period burdens without abandoning long-run revenue and climate objectives.

Stabilization strategy: because week-over-week retail movements closely track Brent, early-warning triggers based on Brent thresholds and volatility bands would align well with observed retail risk transmission.

Methodology and Limitations

Metrics were calculated directly from data.csv. Weekly global averages use equal country-week weighting. Correlations are Pearson correlations between weekly average Brent crude and weekly average retail fuel prices. Pass-through slopes are ordinary least squares slopes expressed as USD/liter retail price change per $10/bbl Brent change.

Limitations: Brent is global and repeated by country-week, subsidy categories are categorical rather than fiscal-cost measures, and tax percentages vary week to week within countries. The 2026 period is year-to-date through 2026-04-06; 2026 annual comparisons should be interpreted as partial-year results.
