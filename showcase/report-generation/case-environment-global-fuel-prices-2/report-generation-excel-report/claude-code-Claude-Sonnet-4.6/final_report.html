Price Trajectory

Global average petrol prices more than doubled from $1.08/liter at their 2020 trough (week of April 27) to $2.78/liter at the 2022 peak (March–April 2022), driven by Brent Crude rising from $47.97 to $130.00/barrel over the same period.

Fuel Type Spread

Diesel averages 6.5% below petrol globally ($2.13 vs $2.28/liter), while LPG sits 25% below petrol ($1.71/liter). This spread is consistent across years, suggesting structural pricing differentials independent of crude movements.

2026 Acceleration

Preliminary 2026 data (Jan–Apr) shows petrol averaging $2.65/liter — the highest annual figure in the dataset — with Brent at $123.75/barrel, suggesting continued upward pressure heading into the second half of 2026.

Extreme Price Disparity

The gap between the world's most expensive fuel market (Hong Kong: $5.54/liter) and cheapest (Venezuela: $0.016/liter) is 346x — almost entirely a function of subsidy depth and tax policy rather than proximity to crude production.

COVID-19 Demand Shock (Q1–Q2 2020)

Brent Crude collapsed from $70.53/barrel (March 2, 2020) to $47.97/barrel (April 27, 2020) — a 32% decline in 8 weeks. Global average petrol fell from $1.51 to $1.02/liter. The COVID shock is the sharpest single-quarter price drop in the 2020–2026 window.

2022 Energy Crisis Peak

Brent reached the dataset maximum of $130.00/barrel in the week of March 28, 2022, following the Russian invasion of Ukraine (Feb 24, 2022). Global petrol averaged $2.78/liter at the peak — a 173% increase over the April 2020 trough. The $130 ceiling persisted for 13 consecutive weeks (March–June 2022).

Recovery & Structural Shift (2023–2026)

2023 saw a mild correction (annual avg: $2.23/liter petrol, Brent $104.43). But 2024–2026 resumed an upward path, with 2026 YTD establishing new highs. The post-2022 floor appears structurally higher than pre-2021 levels — suggesting permanent demand-supply rebalancing.

Volatility Pattern

Cross-sectional standard deviation in petrol prices peaked in 2022 ($1.63), reflecting the wide divergence between high-subsidy (price-insulated) and low-subsidy (fully exposed) countries during the energy crisis. The 2020 shock had lower std dev ($1.01) as even unsubsidized markets saw moderated declines from pre-existing price floors.

Europe–Middle East Gap Widening

The spread between Europe and Middle East regional averages grew from $1.61/liter in 2020 to $2.87/liter by 2026 — a 78% widening of the gap over six years. The divergence accelerated most sharply during 2022 when European prices hit $4.06 while Middle East prices (heavily subsidized) barely moved to $1.35.

Europe & Oceania: Structurally High

Europe ($3.70) and Oceania ($3.66) are in a price tier 70%+ above the global average ($2.28). Both regions are characterized by high income levels, low subsidy regimes, and heavy fuel taxation. European prices reflect VAT and excise duties that constitute 30–50% of pump prices.

Crude Price Pass-Through: Low vs. Very High Subsidy

During the 2022 crisis (Brent +171% from Apr 2020 trough), Low-subsidy country petrol prices rose from ~$2.25 to $3.79/liter (+68%). Very-High-subsidy countries moved from $0.096 to $0.162/liter (+69%) — a similar percentage change but only $0.07/liter absolute impact on consumers, versus $1.54/liter in unsubsidized markets.

High Income + Low Subsidy = Maximum Exposure

High-income, low-subsidy countries (Europe, Oceania, Hong Kong, Singapore) bore the full brunt of the 2022 crisis. European petrol hit $4.06/liter in 2022 — a 69% increase from 2020 ($2.41). By contrast, Middle Eastern Very-High-subsidy countries (regardless of income) saw only $0.55–0.68/liter petrol even at the height of the crisis.

Inverse Tax–Income Relationship at Low End

Low-income countries apply 22.9% average tax — higher than Middle-income (21.1%), despite lower purchasing power. This suggests fuel taxes in low-income markets are revenue-driven rather than Pigouvian, and may impose disproportionate burdens on poor households who rely on fuel for livelihoods.

Tax Note: Within-Country Variability

The tax_percentage field shows high within-country variability (e.g., U.S. ranges 11–65% across weeks). This likely reflects estimation methodology or compositional shifts in the metric. Aggregate income-level averages remain analytically meaningful, but individual country tax trajectories should be treated with caution.

Proportional Burden on Low-Income Populations

While high-income countries pay $3.55/liter vs. $1.39/liter in low-income countries, the relative affordability is inverted. In low-income countries, fuel costs represent a far larger share of household income. A doubling of fuel prices creates existential pressure in poor countries but a manageable inconvenience in wealthy ones.

Uniform % Price Growth Across Income Tiers

All three income-level groups saw near-identical proportional growth from 2020 to 2026: ~78–79%. This indicates crude oil price movements transmitted fairly uniformly in percentage terms across income classifications — subsidy insulation (not income level per se) is the dominant variable determining absolute price change.

High Income: Greatest Absolute Exposure

High-income countries experienced the largest absolute price increase from 2020 to 2026: +$1.80/liter (from $2.31 to $4.11). For countries like Norway ($4.76 avg) and the Netherlands ($4.66 avg), this reflects both low subsidies and deliberate high-tax policy to reduce consumption and fund climate transition.

LPG as an Affordability Option

LPG offers the most price-stable alternative across all income levels, averaging 25% below petrol globally. In low-income settings where LPG infrastructure exists, this can meaningfully reduce household energy costs. Targeted LPG subsidy programs may offer a more fiscally efficient alternative to blanket petrol subsidies.

Section 06

Key Findings & Policy Implications

Synthesizes the most important insights from the analysis into actionable findings, with recommendations for policymakers on subsidy design, tax calibration, and price stabilization mechanisms.

  • 1
    Core Finding

    Brent Crude Is the Dominant Price Driver — Structural Pass-Through Is Near-Total in Unsubsidized Markets

    The Pearson correlation between weekly Brent Crude and global-average retail prices is r = 1.00, confirming that unsubsidized retail fuel prices move in near-perfect lockstep with crude benchmarks. Between April 2020 and March 2022, Brent rose 171% and global average petrol rose 172%. Policymakers in low-subsidy regimes cannot meaningfully shield consumers from commodity shocks without fiscal intervention.

  • 2
    Policy Implication

    Subsidies Provide Powerful Price Dampening — But at Highly Variable Fiscal Efficiency

    The 79.5% subsidy dampening effect is striking but misleading as a policy benchmark. Very-High-subsidy countries (Venezuela, Iran, Iraq, Libya, Algeria) achieve $0.01–$0.16/liter prices through direct fiscal transfers or administered pricing that can amount to 10–20% of GDP in subsidy cost. High-subsidy countries (e.g., GCC states) benefit from domestic crude ownership that reduces fiscal cost. Middle-income countries face the worst trade-off: large subsidy bills without comparable resource wealth. Policymakers should assess subsidy cost-efficiency by income level rather than treating subsidy as a binary good.

  • 3
    Core Finding

    The 2022 Energy Crisis Was the Single Largest Consumer Shock — and Its Structural Effects Persist

    Brent hit $130/barrel in March 2022 and stayed at that ceiling for 13 weeks. The post-2022 global petrol average has not returned to 2020 levels and 2026 YTD ($2.65/liter) exceeds the 2022 annual average ($2.51). This persistent re-basing suggests that pre-2021 prices are unlikely to return — policymakers should calibrate subsidy and stabilization programs against this new higher baseline rather than historical averages.

  • 4
    Risk Factor

    Europe and Oceania Face the Highest Absolute Exposure — and Declining Policy Room

    European petrol averaged $4.06/liter at the 2022 crisis peak — the highest regional price in any year. With existing tax rates of 30–50% of pump price, governments face limited downside tax-reduction headroom without foregoing major revenue. The 2022 EU windfall profit tax on energy companies was a one-time measure; systematic stabilization mechanisms (fuel price floors/ceilings, strategic reserve releases) require institutional design before the next shock.

  • 5
    Equity Concern

    Fuel Affordability Is an Equity Crisis in Low-Income Countries — Disproportionate Tax Burden Compounds the Problem

    Low-income countries pay $1.39/liter average petrol (vs. $3.55 in high-income) but face a 22.9% average tax rate — higher than middle-income countries (21.1%). At the same time, they lack the subsidy depth of very-high-subsidy petrostates. The 2020–2026 price increase of 79% in low-income markets represents a severe and compounding affordability stress that standard economic analysis (focused on absolute price levels) underestimates. Targeted cash transfer mechanisms or LPG subsidies may offer more fiscally efficient protection than generalized petrol subsidies.

  • 6
    Policy Implication

    Regional Spread is Widening — Inter-Country Policy Divergence Is Accelerating

    The spread between the highest (Europe: $4.29) and lowest (Middle East: $1.42) regional average petrol prices in 2026 is $2.87/liter — up 78% from the 2020 spread of $1.61. This widening reflects divergent subsidy and tax trajectories. As crude prices remain elevated, subsidy-maintaining governments will face ever-larger fiscal deficits, while tax-heavy governments will face political pressure to cut rates. Policy coordination at the G20/IEA level on subsidy reform timelines and stabilization fund design would reduce global price inequality.

  • 7
    Policy Recommendation

    Graduated Subsidy Reform Tied to Income-Targeted Transfers Is the Optimal Transition Path

    The data strongly support a graduated subsidy removal strategy: maintain very-high subsidy regimes only where domestic crude production makes them fiscally self-sustaining (GCC); implement targeted cash transfers to replace blanket petrol subsidies in middle- and low-income countries (the Indonesia/Egypt model); use the price gap between LPG ($1.71 global avg) and petrol ($2.28) to prioritize LPG transition support in low-income markets where cooking/transport substitution is feasible; and design automatic price stabilization corridors (e.g., consumer price held to ±20% of 3-year rolling average Brent-equivalent) to limit future volatility exposure without permanent fiscal commitment.
