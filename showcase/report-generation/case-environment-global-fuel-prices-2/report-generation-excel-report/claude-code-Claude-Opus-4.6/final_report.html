1. Global Fuel Price Overview

Between January 2020 and April 2026, the global average retail petrol price stood at $2.28/L, with diesel at $2.13/L and LPG at $1.71/L. Prices ranged from as low as $0.01/L (heavily subsidized markets) to $6.78/L (high-tax European economies), reflecting the enormous policy-driven variance across the 84 countries in this dataset.

Brent Crude, the global benchmark, averaged $106.69/barrel over the period, with a low of $47.97 (COVID-19 trough) and a ceiling of $130.00 during the 2022 energy crisis and again in early 2026.

Yearly Average Fuel Prices & Brent Crude

The yearly trajectory reveals the pandemic effect starkly: 2020 petrol averaged just $1.48/L — 35% below the period mean — before surging 53.5% to $2.28 in 2021 as demand recovered and supply tightened. Prices peaked in 2022 at $2.51/L, briefly retreated in 2023 ($2.23/L), then climbed again to $2.54–2.65 in 2024–2026 as Brent hovered above $118/bbl.

2. Temporal Price Trends & Volatility

This section traces the monthly evolution of retail fuel prices alongside the Brent Crude benchmark, then measures how much of the crude price signal passes through to consumers.

Monthly Brent Crude vs. Retail Fuel Prices (2020–2026)

Key inflection points: Brent bottomed at ~$51/bbl in Apr–May 2020 during COVID-19 lockdowns, doubled to $114/bbl by late 2021, then spiked to $130/bbl in Apr–May 2022 amid geopolitical supply shocks. After retreating to ~$95/bbl in early 2023, it re-climbed steadily to $130/bbl by March–April 2026.

Volatility — measured as the standard deviation of weekly percentage price changes across all countries — peaked in 2025 (0.231) and 2020 (0.221), with both years featuring rapid directional shifts in Brent Crude. The calmest year was 2024 (0.157), when crude traded in a relatively narrow band.

3. Regional Price Disparities

Fuel prices vary dramatically across the seven geographic regions, driven by local taxation, subsidy policy, refining capacity, and logistics costs.

Average Retail Fuel Prices by Region (2020–2026)

Europe and Oceania are consistently the most expensive regions for all three fuel types, with petrol averaging $3.70/L and $3.66/L respectively — roughly triple the Middle East average of $1.23/L. The Middle East and South America are the cheapest, reflecting heavy subsidies and lower tax burdens.

The subsidy dampening effect is massive: consumers in high/very-high subsidy countries pay 79.4% less for petrol than those in low-subsidy countries. Even "medium" subsidy regimes cut prices by roughly half (52%) compared to low-subsidy markets.

Subsidy Group Petrol Prices Over Time (Monthly)

The chart above shows how subsidy regimes translate crude price swings into retail price movements. Low/no-subsidy countries see retail petrol track Brent closely, with clear spikes during the 2022 crisis. High/very-high subsidy countries maintain nearly flat retail prices regardless of crude movements — demonstrating effective price insulation but at significant fiscal cost.

Subsidy Dampening Consistency by Year

The dampening effect has remained remarkably stable at approximately 79.4–79.5% across all seven years — suggesting that subsidy mechanisms have scaled proportionally with crude price movements rather than being fixed at an absolute level.

Brent Correlation by Subsidy Regime

Paradoxically, high-subsidy countries show a stronger Brent correlation (r = 0.65) than low-subsidy countries (r = 0.55). This suggests that while subsidies reduce the level of prices dramatically, the remaining price signal still tracks crude oil movements — subsidies dampen amplitude, not direction.

Income classification fundamentally shapes fuel pricing. High-income countries (32 nations) average $3.55/L for petrol — 2.2× the middle-income average ($1.59/L) and 2.6× the low-income average ($1.39/L).

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

1 Subsidies are the single largest determinant of retail fuel prices. Policy
The 79.4% price dampening effect between high-subsidy and low-subsidy countries dwarfs the impact of income level, region, or taxation. Very high-subsidy countries maintain petrol at $0.15/L regardless of crude prices — effectively decoupling consumer costs from global commodity markets. Implication: Any subsidy reform must be gradual and paired with targeted cash transfers to avoid consumer shock.
2 The regional price gap has widened 78% since 2020 and shows no sign of converging. Data
The spread between Europe ($4.29/L) and the Middle East ($1.42/L) reached $2.87/L in 2026 — up from $1.61/L in 2020. This reflects divergent policy trajectories: European carbon pricing and excise taxes push prices higher while Middle Eastern subsidies hold prices down. Implication: Global carbon pricing mechanisms should account for these structural asymmetries to avoid competitiveness distortions.
3 Brent-to-retail pass-through is weaker than expected (r = 0.26 overall) due to cross-country policy heterogeneity. Data
Within subsidy groups, correlations are substantial (0.55–0.65), confirming that crude prices do drive retail costs — but the signal is heavily modulated by national policy. Implication: Models predicting retail fuel prices from crude benchmarks alone will be systematically wrong; subsidy and tax parameters must be included.
4 Subsidies dampen price amplitude but not direction — and the dampening ratio has been constant at ~79.4% for six years. Policy
This suggests subsidies are implemented as proportional markdowns rather than fixed-price floors. While this provides consistent relative relief, it means subsidy fiscal costs scale directly with crude prices. Implication: Governments spending on fuel subsidies should budget for commodity-linked exposure; a return to $130+ Brent (as in early 2026) mechanically increases subsidy outlays.
5 High-income nations tax fuel at 65% higher rates than middle-income nations (34.7% vs. 21.1%). Policy
This gap reflects fundamentally different fiscal strategies: high-income countries treat fuel tax as a revenue and environmental policy tool, while lower-income countries prioritize affordability. Notably, low-income countries tax at 22.9% — higher than middle-income (21.1%) — possibly reflecting revenue dependency. Implication: Middle-income nations considering carbon taxation have the most fiscal headroom but also the most political sensitivity around fuel costs.
6 2020 and 2025 were the most volatile years for retail fuel prices. Risk
The COVID-19 demand shock (2020) and renewed crude price escalation (2025) each produced the highest weekly price volatility. The intervening period (2024) was comparatively calm. Implication: Price stabilization mechanisms — strategic reserves, automatic tax adjustors, targeted subsidies — should be stress-tested against demand shock and supply shock scenarios independently.
7 Affordability is worst where it matters most: low-income, low-subsidy countries pay $2.35/L. Risk
Eight low-income countries have no significant subsidies, exposing their consumers to near-global retail prices despite dramatically lower purchasing power. These countries represent the most acute fuel affordability crisis in the dataset. Implication: International development financing should prioritize targeted fuel subsidy mechanisms in low-income, low-subsidy nations where the energy equity gap is largest.
