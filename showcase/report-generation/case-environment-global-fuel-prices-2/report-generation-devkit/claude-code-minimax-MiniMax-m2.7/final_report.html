6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

Executive Summary: Key Findings

1. Brent-to-Retail Correlation is Weak but Context-Dependent
The average correlation between Brent Crude and retail petrol prices is only r = 0.105, but this varies dramatically by year. During the 2020 COVID-19 shock, correlation peaked at r = 0.283 as markets moved in unison. Post-pandemic policy divergence reduced correlation to r = 0.029 by 2025. Policymakers should not assume that crude price changes translate directly into retail price changes.
2. Subsidy Frameworks Show Clear Price Dampening Effects
High-subsidy countries demonstrate a 79.5% lower average petrol price compared to low-subsidy countries, with volatility reduced by a factor of ~5 (σ = 0.195 vs σ = 1.107). Very High subsidy countries maintain prices below $0.15/L on average. However, this protection comes at significant fiscal cost and is most sustainable in resource-rich nations.
3. Regional Price Inequality is Persistent and Growing
The gap between the highest-paying region (Europe, $3.702/L) and lowest-paying region (Middle East, $1.226/L) represents a 3:1 price ratio. This regional spread has grown from $1.612 in 2020 to $2.87 in 2026—a 78% increase in inequality. Energy-poor regions face structurally higher burdens.
4. High-Income Countries Tax More, Low-Income Countries Subsidize More
High-income nations collect an average of 34.72% of fuel prices as tax, while low-income nations collect only 22.86%. This reflects different fiscal policy paradigms: high-income countries rely on fuel taxation for revenue, while low-income countries use subsidies to protect citizens from global price shocks.
5. The 2022 Energy Crisis Caused a Structural Break in Pricing
Brent Crude jumped from $69.44/bbl (2020) to $117.12/bbl (2022)—a 69% increase. Retail petrol followed with a 69% increase ($1.485 to $2.505/L), but the pass-through was uneven. Countries with high subsidies managed to limit increases to 20-30%, while unsubsidized markets absorbed the full shock.

Policy Recommendations

  1. Design Tiered Subsidy Systems: Rather than binary high/low subsidies, implement progressive subsidy tiers that protect low-income households while allowing market pricing for higher consumption levels. This preserves price stability for vulnerable populations without unlimited fiscal exposure.
  2. Build Volatility Stabilization Funds: During low-crude-price periods, capture windfall revenues into stabilization funds that can subsidize prices during spikes. This avoids the fiscal whiplash of sudden subsidy expansions.
  3. Calibrate Tax Policy Proactively: Given the high tax-to-price ratio in high-income countries (34.72%), there is room to temporarily reduce fuel taxes during energy crises to cushion consumer impact—without eliminating taxes entirely.
  4. Address Regional Inequality Through Trade Agreements: Persistent 3:1 regional price ratios suggest structural barriers to price arbitrage. Regional energy trade agreements could help equalize access to affordable fuel.
  5. Monitor Subsidy Sustainability Metrics: With very-high-subsidy countries maintaining sub-$0.15/L prices, long-term fiscal sustainability should be assessed. International institutions should develop subsidy health indicators.
Analytical Caveat: This analysis is based on the available dataset from January 2020 to April 2026. Tax percentage data shows notable week-to-week variability within countries, suggesting potential methodological differences in estimation. Subsidy levels are categorical (High, Low, Medium, Very High) rather than continuous, limiting the precision of subsidy-quantity analyses.
