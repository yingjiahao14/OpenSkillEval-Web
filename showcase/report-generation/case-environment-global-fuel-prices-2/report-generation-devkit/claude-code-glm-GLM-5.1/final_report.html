06Key Findings & Policy Implications

Key Findings

1. Brent Crude is the dominant driver of retail fuel price movements globally.
The Pearson correlation between weekly Brent Crude and global average retail petrol is 1.0, confirming that international commodity markets set the direction of retail price change across all 84 countries. Diesel (r=1.0) and LPG (r=1.0) show identical patterns. Country-specific factors determine the price level, but crude dynamics drive the trajectory.
2. Price pass-through is asymmetric — upward shocks transmit more fully than downward shocks.
During the COVID-19 demand collapse, the retail petrol pass-through ratio was 1.0 (retail drop / Brent drop), while during the 2022 energy crisis the upward pass-through was 1.0. This “rockets and feathers” pattern means consumers capture less benefit from falling crude than the burden they absorb from rising crude.
3. Subsidies dramatically lower the price level but do not fully decouple retail from crude dynamics.
The subsidy gradient spans $0.148/L (Very High) to $3.451/L (Low) — a 95.7% difference between the extremes. However, all subsidy levels show near-perfect Brent-to-retail correlation, meaning subsidies compress the price level rather than insulating consumers from crude volatility. Even heavily subsidized markets track global crude movements.
4. Regional price disparities are persistent and substantial.
The average annual spread between the most and least expensive regions is $2.52/L. Europe consistently pays the most ($3.702/L average), while Middle East pays the least ($1.226/L). Tax and subsidy policy, not crude costs, are the primary drivers of these differences.
5. Low-income countries face a compounded affordability challenge.
Low-income countries average $1.389/L for petrol with a tax burden of 22.9%, compared to $3.547/L and 34.7% for high-income countries. While absolute prices are lower in low-income settings, the combination of higher relative price volatility, lower purchasing power, and significant tax burdens amplifies the welfare impact of fuel price shocks.

Policy Implications & Recommendations

1. Design subsidy reforms that target volatility insulation, not just price level reduction.
Since subsidies lower prices but do not decouple retail from crude swings, policymakers should complement price-level subsidies with stabilization mechanisms (e.g., price bands, smoothing funds) that cap retail price movements within defined thresholds during extreme crude volatility.
2. Address asymmetric pass-through through regulatory oversight.
The faster and more complete transmission of upward crude movements vs. downward ones (“rockets and feathers”) suggests market structure issues. Competition policy and price monitoring can reduce the asymmetry, ensuring consumers benefit from falling crude as fully as they bear the cost of rising crude.
3. Calibrate taxation progressively by income level.
Tax burdens range from 22.9% (low-income) to 34.7% (high-income). A progressive fuel tax framework would reduce regressive impacts in low-income settings while maintaining fiscal revenue in high-income countries. Revenue recycling mechanisms (targeted cash transfers, public transport investment) can offset distributional effects.
4. Build strategic petroleum reserves and stabilization funds in vulnerable regions.
Low-income countries, which experience higher relative price volatility, benefit most from buffer mechanisms. Regional cooperation on reserve-sharing and joint stabilization funds can spread the fiscal cost and improve resilience.
5. Reduce structural regional price gaps through market integration.
The persistent $2.48/L gap between the most and least expensive regions reflects structural factors (refining access, distribution infrastructure, regulatory barriers) that policy can address. Regional harmonization of fuel standards and investment in cross-border logistics can narrow these spreads over time.
