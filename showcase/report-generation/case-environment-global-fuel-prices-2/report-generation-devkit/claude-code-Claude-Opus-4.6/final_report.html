1. Executive Summary & KPIs

Across the 27,468 weekly observations, the global mean retail petrol price was USD 2.282/L, diesel USD 2.134/L, and LPG USD 1.711/L. Brent Crude averaged USD 106.69/bbl, swinging from a COVID-era trough of $47.97 (Apr 2020) to a ceiling of $130.00 first hit in March 2022 and revisited in early 2026. Once retail prices are aggregated to a single weekly global mean, they track Brent almost perfectly (r ≈ 1.00); however, at the country-week level the relationship is far noisier (r ≈ 0.26–0.42), reflecting wide policy heterogeneity.

2020: COVID demand shock

Brent collapsed from $70.5/bbl in early March to a trough of $47.97 on Apr 27, 2020 — a 32% drawdown in eight weeks. Global mean petrol fell from $1.51 to $1.05/L over the same window, a 30% pass-through. By December 2020 both had more than fully recovered as supply was withdrawn.

2022: Energy crisis peak

Brent first hit the $130 ceiling on Mar 28, 2022 and stayed elevated through Q2. Global mean petrol peaked above $2.78/L in April–May 2022 — the highest weekly value on record across the period — before easing as inventories rebuilt and demand rationed.

Energy equity observations

Tax progressivity

High-income countries average tax shares of 34.7% — more than 13 percentage points above Middle (21.1%) and 12 points above Low (22.9%). Fuel taxes in wealthy nations serve dual revenue/emissions objectives; in lower-income nations, political economy constraints cap tax rates.

Price-to-income burden

While Low-income countries pay lower absolute prices ($1.39/L), these prices consume a higher share of household income. Meanwhile, the 28 High-income / Low-subsidy countries bear the steepest prices at $3.997/L — accepted because purchasing power offsets the burden.

Volatility shielding varies by wealth

The Brent-to-retail correlation for High-income countries (r = 0.42) exceeds Low-income (r = 0.28), suggesting richer nations have more market-transparent pricing, while lower-income nations rely on administrative price controls that dampen transmission.

Middle-income dynamics

Middle-income countries sit between both extremes on price ($1.59/L) and tax (21.1%), but host the largest share of Medium-subsidy regimes (11 of 18). This group may face the hardest trade-offs in transitioning from subsidies to market pricing.

7. Key Findings & Policy Implications

The following findings synthesise the statistical evidence above into actionable insights for energy policymakers evaluating subsidy design, tax calibration, and price stabilisation strategies.

1. Subsidies are overwhelmingly effective at price dampening — but at escalating fiscal cost

Very-High-subsidy countries suppressed retail petrol to $0.15/L — a 95.7% discount to unsubsidised markets. Year-over-year price volatility was just $0.03/L vs. $0.60/L for Low-subsidy peers. However, as Brent has settled at a structural plateau above $110/bbl since 2021, the fiscal burden of maintaining these subsidies has grown commensurately. Policymakers in these countries should stress-test budgets against sustained $120+ oil scenarios.

2. The aggregate Brent-to-retail price transmission is near-perfect; country-level divergence reflects policy, not market failure

Weekly global mean retail petrol correlated at r = 1.00 with Brent Crude. At the country level, r drops to 0.26 — this ~0.74 gap is entirely attributable to heterogeneous fiscal and subsidy interventions. There is no "leakage" from crude to pump at the global level; differences are policy choices.

3. Regional price inequality is widening, not narrowing

The Europe–Middle East price spread grew from $1.61/L (2020) to $2.87/L (2026 YTD), a 78% increase. This trajectory has accelerated during high-crude-price periods, suggesting that regional fiscal policies amplify — rather than buffer — global price shocks for consumers in high-tax markets.

4. High-income countries rely on fuel tax as a policy lever; lower-income countries cannot

With an average tax share of 34.7%, High-income nations treat fuel taxation as both a revenue source and an emissions-reduction mechanism. Middle- and Low-income nations average 21–23% — constrained by political pressure and the regressive nature of fuel taxes on poorer populations. Any global push for carbon taxation on fuels must account for this asymmetry.

5. The 2022 energy crisis demonstrated both the value and the fragility of subsidy regimes

While Brent spiked 37% from January to March 2022, Very-High-subsidy countries saw retail petrol rise only from $0.15 to $0.16/L — a near-total buffer. But this success comes with an unseen cost: the implied subsidy per litre rose sharply. Countries in the High and Medium tiers showed more price pass-through (Brent-petrol r of 0.65 and 0.78 respectively), indicating partial-subsidy regimes provide weaker shields.

6. Medium-subsidy countries face the most difficult transition

The 18 Medium-subsidy nations (mostly Middle- and Low-income) show the strongest Brent–retail correlation (r = 0.78) among subsidised groups, meaning price shocks already transmit significantly. Yet their average petrol price ($1.66/L) remains well below market rates. These countries are neither fully shielded nor fully exposed — the least stable equilibrium for policymakers.

7. Post-crisis Brent has settled into a new structural range ($110–$130/bbl) — policy must adapt

Since 2021, Brent has averaged $112+/bbl with decreasing volatility (σ dropped from $13.5 in 2020 to $2.2 in 2025). The market has repriced. Subsidy regimes designed for $60–$80 oil face permanent budget pressure. Tax regimes calibrated to moderate-price environments may need recalibration to avoid consumer backlash at the new structural level.
