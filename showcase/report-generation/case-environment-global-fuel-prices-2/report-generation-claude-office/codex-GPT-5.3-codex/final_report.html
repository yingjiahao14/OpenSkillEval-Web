1) Global Fuel Price Overview

Across 84 countries and 27,468 weekly-country observations (2020-01-06 to 2026-04-06), average retail prices were $2.282/L (petrol), $2.134/L (diesel), and $1.711/L (LPG). Brent averaged $106.686/barrel, ranging from $47.970 to $130.000.

Weekly series are global means across all countries to isolate time dynamics from country-composition noise.

2) Temporal Price Trends & Volatility

Retail fuels tracked Brent closely, with strongest co-movement in diesel (r=1.000) and petrol (r=1.000). Volatility spikes cluster around pandemic-demand disruption and the 2022 energy crisis period.

Top 8-week rolling petrol-volatility weeks (global mean basis) begin around: 2020-04-13, 2020-04-20, 2020-04-06, 2020-04-27.

3) Regional Price Disparities

Regional pricing gaps remained material over time. The largest annual petrol spread was $2.870/L in 2026; in 2026 the spread remained $2.870/L, indicating persistent spatial inequality in retail energy costs.

4) Impact of Subsidies and Taxation on Retail Prices

High-subsidy countries show systematically lower mean consumer prices: petrol $0.709/L vs $3.451/L in low-subsidy systems (79.5% lower), and diesel $0.650/L vs $3.244/L (80.0% lower).

5) Fuel Affordability by Income Level

Income segmentation shows differentiated policy mixes: higher-income groups carry higher average tax shares, while lower-income groups tend to rely more on price suppression mechanisms to contain pump prices.

6) Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Brent remains a dominant transmission channel: petrol and diesel are highly correlated with Brent (r=1.000 and r=1.000). Price stabilization policy must assume strong international pass-through.
  2. Subsidies materially dampen consumer prices: high-subsidy regimes show 79.5% lower petrol and 80.0% lower diesel prices versus low-subsidy regimes on average.
  3. Pass-through is policy-sensitive: subsidy segmentation indicates lower Brent-to-retail slope in high-subsidy settings (petrol slope 0.0067 vs 0.0324), suggesting partial buffering of global shocks.
  4. Regional disparities persist even post-crisis: yearly petrol spread peaked at $2.870/L (2026) and remained $2.870/L in 2026; broad-based equity improvements require regional targeting.
  5. Tax calibration should be counter-cyclical: where tax shares are high, temporary, rule-based excise relief during Brent spikes can reduce household shock without permanently weakening fiscal capacity.
  6. Recommended policy architecture: combine targeted subsidies (vulnerable households, transport-intensive sectors), Brent-indexed tax bands, and automatic stabilization triggers tied to rolling volatility thresholds.
