2. Temporal Price Trends & Volatility

Brent Crude & Retail Fuel Prices (Monthly Averages)

Retail fuel prices tracked Brent Crude with near-perfect correlation when measured at the weekly global average level (r = 1.00 for all three fuel types). At the individual country-week level, the correlation is weaker (r ≈ 0.26), reflecting the wide dispersion introduced by national tax and subsidy regimes.

COVID-19 demand shock (Q2 2020): Brent fell to $47.97/bbl and average retail petrol dropped to $1.09/L in May 2020 — a 23% decline from January 2020.
2022 energy crisis: Brent surged to $130/bbl in April–May 2022, pushing average petrol to $2.78/L — the highest monthly level until March–April 2026, which matched it at the $130 ceiling.

Annual Price Volatility (Cross-Country Std. Dev. of Petrol)

Volatility (measured as cross-country standard deviation) rose sharply from 1.01 in 2020 to 1.63 in 2022, and has remained elevated at ~1.63–1.70 through 2024–2026. The persistent high dispersion reflects structural pricing differences across subsidy regimes rather than temporary shocks.

6. Key Findings & Policy Implications

  1. Brent Crude fully dictates the direction of retail prices, but policy determines the level. Weekly average retail prices correlate at r = 1.00 with Brent — no country escapes global commodity cycles. However, the 170× range between the cheapest ($0.01/L) and most expensive ($6.78/L) retail petrol demonstrates that taxes and subsidies are far more powerful than commodity cost in setting what consumers actually pay.
  2. Subsidies deliver massive price dampening — but at a steep fiscal cost. Very High subsidy countries deliver petrol at $0.15/L versus $3.45/L in Low-subsidy markets (a 95.7% reduction). Policymakers considering subsidy reform should anticipate that even moving from Very High to High subsidy nearly quintuples the consumer price. Phase-out programs need gradual price discovery mechanisms and targeted cash transfers.
  3. Taxation, not commodity cost, drives the Europe–Middle East price gap. The regional spread has widened from $1.61 to $2.87/L over the period, with Europe (avg tax 34.6%) consistently at the top and the Middle East (avg tax 19.6%, high subsidies) at the bottom. European fuel taxes function as de facto carbon pricing; Middle Eastern subsidies function as the opposite.
  4. Price volatility has structurally increased and is not reverting. Cross-country standard deviation rose from 1.01 in 2020 to 1.70 in 2026. This reflects a widening divergence in national pricing policies rather than temporary market stress. Price stabilization strategies must address structural regime differences, not just short-term supply shocks.
  5. Low-income countries face a policy dilemma: low absolute prices but high relative burden. While low-income nations average $1.39/L — the lowest in absolute terms — their average tax rate (22.9%) is comparable to middle-income countries (21.1%). Given lower purchasing power, the effective burden on consumers is disproportionately heavy. Targeted subsidy programs in low-income countries should consider affordability-indexed pricing.
  6. The 2022 energy crisis and 2026 price surge expose subsidy sustainability risks. During peak Brent ($130/bbl), Very High subsidy countries maintained prices at $0.18/L — implying enormous fiscal outlays. Governments with high subsidy commitments should model exposure under sustained $120+/bbl crude scenarios and prepare contingency caps or rationing mechanisms.
