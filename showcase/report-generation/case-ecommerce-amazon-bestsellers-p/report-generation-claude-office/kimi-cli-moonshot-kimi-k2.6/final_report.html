6. Strategic Recommendations

All recommendations are grounded in the dataset and traceable to specific findings above.

1. Do not use price as a proxy for quality. The Pearson correlation of -0.133 definitively shows no positive price-rating relationship. Premium pricing does not signal superior customer satisfaction to the bestseller audience. Price competitively rather than aspirationally.
2. Exploit the Fiction value advantage. Fiction averages $10.85 vs. Non-Fiction’s $14.84 yet scores higher ratings (4.648 vs. 4.595) and generates 1.7× more reviews. Publishers should prioritize Fiction acquisitions — especially in children’s/YA — where lower production costs meet higher engagement.
3. Target the $6–$10 price band. This segment contains 179 bestsellers (the largest cluster) with a strong average rating of 4.649. It represents the "sweet spot" where volume and satisfaction converge. Avoid the $11–$20 band if possible — it shows lower ratings without proportional revenue upside.
4. Monitor the post-2015 market reset. The structural price decline after 2015 (from ~$15 to ~$10) coincided with rising ratings and sustained bestseller counts. This suggests the market has shifted toward value-conscious consumers. Legacy pricing models above $20 should be re-evaluated unless targeting institutional/niche segments (e.g., APA, SAT guides).
5. Invest in series and recurring authors. The top 10 authors account for 95 of 550 appearances (17.3%). Series-driven models (Kinney’s Diary of a Wimpy Kid, Riordan’s mythological universe) create compounding brand equity. Catalog curation should favor authors with multi-title pipeline potential.
6. Leverage review volume as an early signal. Fiction titles with high review counts (e.g., Where the Crawdads Sing at 87,841 reviews) often sustain multi-year bestseller presence. Build pre-launch review generation programs — especially for Fiction — to trigger the algorithmic and social proof dynamics that drive sustained ranking.
