Price vs. User Rating: Correlation Analysis

Higher prices are weakly associated with lower ratings, but the relationship is not strong enough to treat price as a primary satisfaction driver. The Pearson correlation is -0.133 across all 550 observations.

Finding: The downward trendline is shallow. Bestseller status can be achieved across a wide price range, but high prices do not buy higher satisfaction scores.
Genre-specific correlations: Fiction r = -0.103; Non-Fiction r = -0.135. Both are weakly negative, reinforcing that the pattern is not isolated to one genre.

Fiction vs. Non-Fiction: Pricing and Rating Strategies

Fiction delivers the stronger perceived-value profile: lower average price, slightly higher average rating, and materially higher review engagement. Non-Fiction sustains more appearances and higher prices, but does not convert that premium into higher ratings.

Fiction profile: $10.85 average price, 4.648 average rating, and 15,684 average reviews. This points to high engagement at accessible pricing.
Non-Fiction profile: $14.84 average price and 4.595 average rating across 310 appearances. The genre supports premium pricing, but satisfaction trails Fiction by 0.053 rating points.

Year-over-Year Trends in Pricing and Ratings

Temporal movement is the most important management signal in the dataset. Average price peaked in 2009 at $15.40, bottomed in 2019 at $10.08, while average rating rose from its low of 4.532 in 2012 to a high of 4.740 in 2019.

Inflection Points

  • 2012 engagement step-change: average reviews rose by 4,990 year over year, from 8,101 in 2011 to 13,091 in 2012.
  • 2014 rating recovery: average rating increased by 0.068, the first large upward move after the 2012 low.
  • 2015 pricing reset: average price fell by $4.22, the largest one-year price decline in the dataset, from $14.64 to $10.42.
  • 2019 satisfaction peak: average rating reached 4.740 and average reviews reached 15,898, while average price was near the period low at $10.08.

Strategic Recommendations

  • Do not use higher price as a proxy for higher satisfaction. The dataset’s Pearson r of -0.133 and weak negative genre-level correlations show no evidence that higher-priced bestsellers earn better ratings.
  • Prioritize accessible price bands for volume-oriented bestseller strategy. The $6–10 band is the largest segment (179 appearances), and $1–15 captures 72.0% of all bestseller appearances.
  • Use Fiction as an engagement engine. Fiction combines lower average price ($10.85), higher average rating (4.648), and 1.7× Non-Fiction’s average reviews, making it attractive for audience growth and review velocity.
  • Treat Non-Fiction premium pricing selectively. Non-Fiction supports higher average price ($14.84) and more appearances (310), but its lower average rating (4.595) means price premiums should be supported by clear topic authority, utility, or brand strength.
  • Monitor annual price resets and rating resilience together. The 2015 price drop occurred while ratings continued rising; by 2019, ratings and reviews peaked near a low average price. Management should evaluate pricing experiments against both rating and review-volume movement, not price alone.
  • Invest in repeatable author/franchise assets. Top recurring authors demonstrate sustained list presence across multiple titles or repeat years; catalog curation should favor proven repeat engagement where average ratings remain above the dataset average of 4.618.

Methodology and Data Integrity

All statistics, averages, counts, price segments, and Pearson correlation coefficients in this report were computed directly from /app/benchmark/data.csv. The analysis treats each row as one annual bestseller appearance, consistent with the dataset design of 50 books per year from 2009 through 2019.

No external or fabricated values are used. Because duplicate titles across years are intentional, repeat appearances are included in trend, genre, author, and correlation calculations.
