Price vs. User Rating: Correlation Analysis

Quantify and visualize whether higher prices are associated with higher (or lower) customer ratings.

Correlation result (Pearson)

  • Overall Pearson r = -0.133 between Price and User Rating.
  • By genre: Fiction r = -0.103; Non-Fiction r = -0.135.
  • Interpretation: the relationship is weakly negative overall—higher-priced bestsellers do not reliably receive higher ratings.

Management implication: pricing power is more likely explained by brand/format/positioning than by a direct “pay more → rate higher” satisfaction effect.

Fiction vs. Non-Fiction: Pricing and Rating Strategies

At-a-glance comparison

Non-Fiction prices higher on average; Fiction earns slightly higher average ratings and materially higher average review counts.

Key takeaways

  • Price premium in Non-Fiction: average $14.84 vs. Fiction $10.85.
  • Higher satisfaction in Fiction (slightly): average rating 4.648 vs. 4.595.
  • Engagement gap: Fiction averages 15,684 reviews vs. Non-Fiction 9,065.
Value perception: Non-Fiction appears to sustain higher prices without higher ratings, suggesting buyers accept a price premium for perceived utility—but satisfaction is not proportionally higher.

Strategic Recommendations

Actionable guidance grounded strictly in computed findings from the 2009–2019 bestseller dataset.

1) Treat “price” and “rating” as separate levers

  • Overall correlation is weak and negative (Pearson r = -0.133).
  • Action: optimize price for margin/positioning and manage satisfaction via product quality + expectation-setting, not via price lifts alone.

2) Win in the dominant price bands

  • $6–$10 and $11–$15 represent 58.4% of entries combined.
  • Action: prioritize launches and promo strategies that keep competitive offers in these bands while protecting perceived value.

3) Calibrate Non-Fiction’s price premium with value signals

  • Non-Fiction prices higher (avg $14.84) but rates slightly lower (avg 4.595).
  • Action: pair premium pricing with stronger “value proof” (e.g., author credentials, utility framing) to avoid rating erosion.

4) Use inflection years as strategy checkpoints

  • Price volatility peaked in 2015 (-4.22 YoY) and rebounded in 2016 (+2.76).
  • Ratings improved most in 2019 (+0.072 YoY), while prices stayed low.
  • Action: when planning changes, benchmark expected impacts against these historical “shift magnitudes”.

5) Build author franchises—repeat presence is a measurable moat

  • Repeat-author appearances are common (e.g., the top author appears 12 times).
  • Action: prioritize multi-title pipelines and series/brand continuity to maintain bestseller visibility over multiple years.
