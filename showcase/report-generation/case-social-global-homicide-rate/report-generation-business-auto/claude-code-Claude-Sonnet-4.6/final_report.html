Global Homicide Rate Overview

The global intentional homicide rate has followed a complex trajectory over the past three decades. Analyzing the median rate across all reporting countries — a robust measure that resists distortion from extreme outliers — reveals a general declining trend from 1990 to the present, punctuated by periods of stagnation and reversal.

The global median homicide rate fell from 3.35 (1990) to 2.27 per 100,000 (2022), a reduction of approximately 32%. However, the global mean — heavily influenced by high-rate countries in Latin America and the Caribbean — has remained persistently elevated, declining only modestly from ~8.3 in the 1990s to ~7.7 in the 2010s.

The 2000s–2010s transition saw the sharpest improvement: the global average fell 9.6% between these decades. The 2010s–2020s transition showed near-stagnation (+0.2%), suggesting the pace of improvement has slowed considerably. Decade averages: 1990s = 8.27, 2000s = 8.51, 2010s = 7.7, 2020s = 7.71.

Reporting coverage expanded substantially from 94 countries in 1990 to a peak of 162 in 2008, then declined to 84 by 2023 — a reporting gap effect that analysts must account for when interpreting recent trends.

Note: Declining coverage in recent years (especially 2022–2023) means recent global statistics are based on a smaller and potentially non-representative sample of countries.

Regional & Country-Level Comparison

Geographic disparities in homicide rates are stark and persistent. Latin America and the Caribbean consistently records the highest median rates globally — more than 10× higher than Europe. Africa occupies a middle position, while Asia and Europe show the lowest rates among major regions.

Latin America's median homicide rate in 2023 (~17.8 per 100,000) is approximately 20× higher than Europe's median (~0.9), illustrating the profound regional inequality in homicide risk.

03

Gender Disparity in Homicide Victimization

One of the most consistent findings in global homicide data is the pronounced overrepresentation of male victims. Across virtually every country and year in this dataset, males face dramatically higher homicide rates than females — a pattern that persists across all regions and income levels.

Key Finding: In 2022, the global median male homicide rate was approximately 2.86 per 100,000, compared to 1.10 per 100,000 for females — a male-to-female ratio of 2.6×. This ratio has persisted throughout the entire observation period, ranging from 2.5× to nearly 5.7× in select years.

The ratio shows considerable year-to-year variation, partly reflecting changing country composition in reporting. The spike observed in 1993 (ratio ~5.7) reflects a period when only ~44 countries reported sex-disaggregated data, skewing the sample. As coverage expanded post-2000, the ratio stabilized in the 2.6–4.0 range.

Policy Implication: Male homicide overrepresentation cannot be treated as a reason to deprioritize female victimization. Femicide and intimate partner violence require distinct policy responses that go beyond aggregate rate comparisons. Sex-disaggregated data collection remains inconsistent across countries, limiting more precise analysis.
04

Age Group Analysis

Age-disaggregated data reveals that homicide risk is highly concentrated in specific life stages. Young adults face disproportionately high victimization rates, while children and the elderly are comparatively less at risk — though still significant in absolute terms.

Peak Risk Ages 25–44: The 25–29 and 30–44 age groups show the highest median homicide rates for both males and females globally. For males, the median rate in the 30–44 bracket is 4.08 per 100,000, compared to just 2.12 for those 60 and older.

The 15–29 age cohort (spanning brackets 15–17, 18–19, 20–24, 25–29) collectively represents the highest-risk window, consistent with global research linking youth homicide to gang activity, organized crime, and interpersonal conflict in high-inequality settings. The gender gap widens sharply in the 18–29 range, where male rates are 3–4× female rates.

Children aged 0–9 exhibit very low but non-negligible rates (male median: 0.48, female: 0.37), reflecting infanticide and child abuse-related homicides. The relative gender parity in this group contrasts sharply with the divergence in adult age brackets.

Note: Age-disaggregated data has significantly lower coverage than aggregate (ALL) data. Results reflect the subset of countries reporting detailed age breakdowns and may not be globally representative.

06

Data Limitations & Policy Recommendations

Data Limitations

Unbalanced Panel Dataset

The dataset is an unbalanced panel: not all 201 countries report in every year. The reporting set expands from 94 countries (1990) to a peak of 162 (2008), then contracts to 84 (2023). Global averages computed across different subsets of countries are not directly comparable across time.

No Missing Value Imputation

Rows for country-year combinations without available data are simply absent — no interpolation was applied. This is methodologically sound but means analysts should not assume continuity where gaps exist.

United Kingdom Caveat

UK data in this dataset refer to England and Wales only, as reported in the UNODC source. Scotland and Northern Ireland may appear as separate entries. Aggregating these to a "United Kingdom" total requires external population weights.

Territory Double-Counting Risk

Dependent territories (Hong Kong, Macao, Puerto Rico, British Overseas Territories) are reported separately and should not be aggregated with their parent countries. Care is required when constructing national or regional totals.

Small Population Volatility

Small island states and territories with populations under 100,000 can exhibit extreme rate fluctuations driven by a small number of absolute homicides. Rates above 50 per 100,000 in these jurisdictions should be interpreted with caution.

Sex/Age Coverage Gaps

Sex-disaggregated and age-disaggregated data have substantially lower coverage than aggregate (sex=both, age=ALL) records. Gender and age analyses in this report are limited to the reporting subset and may not generalize globally.

Policy Recommendations

  • Prioritize Caribbean and Central American Regional Interventions Latin America and the Caribbean consistently record rates 10–20× higher than Europe and Asia. International cooperation frameworks should prioritize capacity-building, anti-trafficking enforcement, and social investment in these regions, particularly in small island states experiencing rapid rate escalation (Ecuador, Haiti, Trinidad and Tobago).
  • Targeted Youth Violence Prevention (Ages 18–29) The 18–29 age cohort faces the highest homicide risk globally. Evidence-based interventions — including cognitive behavioral therapy programs, economic opportunity initiatives, and hotspot policing — should be scaled in high-rate jurisdictions, informed by the strong age-risk gradient identified in this data.
  • Gender-Responsive Homicide Policy The persistent male-to-female ratio of 2.6× masks distinct underlying drivers. While male homicide is concentrated in public spaces and linked to organized crime, female homicide frequently involves intimate partner and domestic contexts. Separate policy streams addressing femicide prevention, shelter infrastructure, and legal accountability are warranted alongside general homicide reduction strategies.
  • Invest in Reporting Capacity to Close Data Gaps With only 53% of countries providing data in 2022 and coverage declining since 2008, the global picture is increasingly incomplete. UNODC and member states should invest in national civil registration systems and standardized homicide reporting protocols to restore and expand coverage — particularly in sub-Saharan Africa and conflict-affected states.
  • Monitor and Study Successful Outliers Countries like El Salvador, Colombia, and the Philippines achieved dramatic reductions exceeding 65% over the observation period. Systematic evaluation of the policy, institutional, and social factors driving these reductions would generate transferable lessons for other high-burden contexts.
