1. Global Homicide Rate Overview

Over the 34-year observation window (1990–2023), the global landscape of intentional homicide reveals a nuanced trajectory: while the median homicide rate has declined meaningfully — from approximately 3.96 per 100,000 in the 1990s to 2.35 in the 2020s — the mean rate has been more resistant to decline, reflecting the outsized influence of a small number of extremely high-violence countries.

Reporting coverage peaked at 162 countries in 2008 (81% of the 201 countries/territories in the dataset), then declined to 84 countries by 2023 (42%). This shrinking coverage in recent years means that global aggregates for the most recent period should be interpreted with caution, as the composition of reporting countries shifts year to year.

Key finding: The 2000s-to-2010s transition saw the most significant improvement (−9.6% in mean rate). The 2020s data so far shows stagnation, though fewer countries are reporting, making direct comparison unreliable.

The gap between the mean and median — consistently large throughout the period — underscores the skewed distribution of homicide rates globally. A small number of nations with extremely high rates pull the mean significantly above the median. This asymmetry is a critical consideration for policy: global averages mask the concentration of lethal violence in specific regions and countries.

2. Regional & Country-Level Comparison

3. Gender Disparity in Homicide Victimization

Gender disparity in intentional homicide is one of the most consistent and stark findings in this dataset. Across all years and regions, males are overwhelmingly more likely to be victims of intentional homicide than females.

The mean male homicide rate across all observations is 13.95 per 100,000, compared to 2.35 per 100,000 for females — a ratio of approximately 5.9:1. This ratio has remained remarkably stable over three decades, with the median country-level ratio hovering around 3.0–3.5 throughout.

The difference between the mean ratio (5.9) and the median ratio (3.15) reflects the pull of countries with extreme gender disparities. In several high-violence nations, the male rate exceeds the female rate by 10× or more, driven by gang violence, armed conflict, and organized crime that disproportionately affects young men.

Policy implication: While females face lower absolute homicide risk, femicide — the gender-motivated killing of women — is often underreported in aggregate statistics. The relatively stable female rate of ~2 per 100k should not be taken to mean that gender-based violence against women is not a priority. The context of female homicide (intimate partner violence, domestic settings) differs fundamentally from male homicide (street violence, gang-related), requiring distinct policy responses.

4. Age Group Analysis

Homicide risk is not uniformly distributed across age groups. The data reveals a clear age-risk curve that peaks sharply in young adulthood, with males aged 25–29 facing the highest average rate globally.

Critical finding: Males aged 25–29 face a mean homicide rate of 29.4 per 100,000 — roughly 35× higher than the rate for children under 10 (0.83) and 9× higher than females of the same age group. The rate escalates dramatically between ages 15–17 (8.74) and 18–19 (20.33), suggesting that the transition to legal adulthood coincides with a massive spike in lethal violence exposure.

For females, the age profile is flatter: rates rise from approximately 0.5 per 100k in childhood to a peak of about 3.5 at ages 18–19, declining only slightly through middle age. This flatter curve likely reflects the different drivers of female homicide — domestic and intimate partner violence affects women across age groups, unlike the street and gang violence concentrated among young men.

The gender ratio itself shifts dramatically with age. Among children 0–9, the M/F ratio is only 1.5:1 — close to parity. By ages 25–29, it reaches 9.1:1. This widening gap underscores that the mechanisms driving male homicide in young adulthood (organized crime, territorial disputes, firearms access) are qualitatively different from those affecting children and older adults.

6. Data Limitations & Policy Recommendations

Data Limitations

The findings in this report must be interpreted in light of several significant data constraints:

LimitationImpactMitigation
Unbalanced panel Not all countries report every year; the set of reporting countries changes annually. This means "global" averages reflect different country compositions year to year, potentially biasing trend estimates. We report median alongside mean to reduce outlier influence, and note the number of reporting countries with each statistic.
Declining coverage (2020s) Coverage dropped from 162 countries in 2008 to 84 in 2023 (42%). The 2020s data should not be directly compared to the 2000s or 2010s without accounting for this compositional shift. We focus decade comparisons on direction rather than precise magnitudes, and explicitly flag the coverage rate as a KPI.
UK data caveat United Kingdom data refers to England and Wales only. Scotland and Northern Ireland may appear as separate entries, but are not combined into a single UK figure. Users should be aware when using country-level UK comparisons.
Territory inclusion Territories (Hong Kong, Macao, Puerto Rico, overseas French departments, etc.) are included as separate entries. Risk of double-counting with parent countries. Territory status is noted where relevant; small-population territories are flagged when they appear as extreme outliers.
No imputation Missing years are left absent — not interpolated. This preserves data integrity but means trend lines have gaps for many countries. All reported figures are directly from the source data. No values were fabricated or imputed.
Sex/age disaggregation gaps Not all countries report sex- or age-disaggregated data, reducing sample size for the gender and age analyses. We report sample sizes alongside all disaggregated statistics.

Policy Recommendations

1. Prioritize interventions in the Americas and Caribbean. With mean rates of 19+ per 100k — an order of magnitude above Europe and Asia — the Americas region accounts for a disproportionate share of global homicide. Caribbean island states in particular face escalating violence that outpaces their institutional capacity. International cooperation on drug trafficking disruption, weapons control, and community violence intervention programs should be concentrated here.
2. Target young males aged 15–29 with prevention programs. This demographic faces by far the highest risk, with male 25–29 rates averaging 29.4 per 100k. Gang diversion, employment, education, and mentorship programs should be priority investments in high-violence countries.
3. Invest in reporting infrastructure. The decline in global reporting coverage to 42% by 2023 undermines the ability to monitor progress and allocate resources. Capacity building for national crime data systems — particularly in Africa and parts of Asia — is essential for evidence-based policy.
4. Address gender-specific violence through differentiated strategies. The 6:1 male-to-female ratio reflects fundamentally different violence dynamics. Male homicide prevention requires addressing gang recruitment, firearms access, and organized crime. Female homicide prevention requires addressing domestic violence, intimate partner violence, and legal protections for women.
5. Study and replicate success stories. El Salvador (−94%), Colombia (−67%), and several post-Soviet states achieved dramatic reductions. While each context is unique, comparative analysis of successful interventions can inform strategies elsewhere.
6. Monitor emerging crises. Ecuador, Haiti, and several Caribbean nations show rapid deterioration in recent years. Early warning systems and rapid-response frameworks should be established to detect and respond to surging homicide rates before they become entrenched.
