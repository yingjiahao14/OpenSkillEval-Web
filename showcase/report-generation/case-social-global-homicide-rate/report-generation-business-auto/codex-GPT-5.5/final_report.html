Global Homicide Rate Overview

Bottom line: In the reporting-country median, the world appears safer from intentional homicide than in the early 1990s: the median rate fell from 3.35 in 1990 to 1.84 in 2023. The mean is higher and more volatile because high-rate countries exert strong influence, rising from 6.33 to 8.29 over the same endpoints.

Interpretation

The median trend is the more robust global signal in this unbalanced panel. It declines from the elevated early-1990s period and remains below the 1990s median in the 2010s and 2020s. The mean remains around 8.3 in 2023 because a small number of very high-rate countries pull the average upward.

Decade-over-decade averages show a slight increase from the 1990s to 2000s, a decline in the 2010s, and little change in the partial 2020s. In the fixed sample of 100 countries with observations in every decade, mean changes are: 2000s: -1.4%, 2010s: -0.7%, 2020s: -9.9%.

Reporting Coverage and Panel Balance

The dataset is a strongly unbalanced country-year panel. Total-population homicide rates are available for 201 countries or territories at least once, but only 29 have a complete 34-year sequence. The median country has 23 reporting years, while the mean is 21.0.

Coverage peaks and troughs matter for trend interpretation: 2023 includes 84 total-rate country observations, compared with 162 in the best-covered year. Shifts in the reporting set can move the mean and median even when underlying risk is unchanged.

Gender Disparity in Homicide Victimization

Key KPI: In 2023, the median country male-to-female homicide rate ratio is 3.17×. Across countries with paired sex-disaggregated data, mean male and female rates are 14.97 and 2.21 per 100,000, respectively.

The gender gap is persistent across the full time series. Male rates consistently exceed female rates, and the country-level ratio is often above 3:1. This indicates that violence prevention strategies need a strong focus on men and boys as victims, while maintaining targeted responses for women and girls where risks are concentrated.

Age Group Analysis

Age-disaggregated rows are available only for male and female victims, not for `sex = both`. Across all age-specific observations, male risk rises sharply after adolescence, peaking in the 25–29 and 30–44 groups. Female age-specific means are much lower and flatter, with comparatively higher values in adult age groups than in childhood.

Age coverage is narrower than total-rate coverage: 100 countries report at least one age-specific observation, and only 27 countries have any age-specific observation in 2023. The 2023 age sample is heavily shaped by countries that still report detailed age-sex data, so long-run age comparisons should be interpreted as descriptive rather than globally representative.

Data Limitations & Policy Recommendations

Data limitations

  • Unbalanced panel: Countries enter and exit the dataset; absent years are not missing values but omitted rows.
  • Changing composition: Annual global means and medians combine different reporting countries over time.
  • No population weighting: Rates are comparable per 100,000, but this report averages country rates rather than counting victims globally because population denominators are not provided.
  • No region column: Regional language is limited to observed country clusters and should not be treated as formal UN regional aggregation.
  • UK caveat: As documented in the source brief, UK data refer to England and Wales only; Scotland and Northern Ireland may appear separately.
  • Small-population volatility: Very small territories can show extreme rates from small count changes.

Policy recommendations

  • Prioritize high-rate clusters: Direct prevention, investigative, and community-safety resources toward countries with persistently high recent rates, especially the top 2023 group.
  • Target male victimization: Treat the male-to-female ratio as a core monitoring KPI and focus interventions on high-risk male youth and adult groups.
  • Protect improving trajectories: Countries with large declines should be monitored for sustainability and studied for transferable practices, while avoiding causal claims from this dataset alone.
  • Improve reporting continuity: International cooperation should support regular sex- and age-disaggregated reporting, because coverage drops weaken cross-country monitoring.
  • Use medians with means: Executive dashboards should report both metrics so outliers do not obscure the typical country experience.
