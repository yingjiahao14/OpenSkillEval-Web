1. Global Homicide Rate Overview

Over the past three decades, the global intentional homicide landscape has shown a broad downward trajectory in median rates, even as the mean rate has been more volatile. The central question—is the world becoming safer?—can be answered with cautious optimism: the typical country is safer today than in the early 1990s, but the persistence of extremely high-rate outliers continues to elevate the global average.

Figure 1. Global median and mean homicide rates (left axis) alongside the number of reporting countries (right axis), 1990–2023.

The median homicide rate across reporting countries fell from 5.20 per 100,000 in 1993 to 1.84 per 100,000 in 2023—a decline of roughly 65%. However, the mean rate tells a more nuanced story: after peaking at 9.73 in 1994, it stabilized in the 7–8 range from the 2010s onward. The gap between mean and median is a direct consequence of a long right tail: a relatively small number of countries experience homicide rates an order of magnitude above the global norm.

Figure 2. Mean homicide rate by decade, showing the modest rise in the 2000s followed by a decline in the 2010s.

Decade-over-decade comparisons reveal that the 2000s saw a slight increase in the global mean (+2.9% vs. the 1990s), driven in part by expanded reporting from high-violence contexts. The 2010s brought a −9.5% mean decline, while the 2020s-to-date have been essentially flat (+0.1%). This plateau warrants attention: the gains of the 2010s have not clearly continued into the 2020s, and reporting coverage has dropped sharply (from 137 countries in 2019 to 84 in 2023), complicating any assessment of recent trajectories.

Key insight: Among the 58 countries with data in both 1990 and 2023, the median rate fell from 2.70 to 1.28 per 100,000—a 53% improvement. Yet the mean for this same panel rose slightly (6.28 to 7.62), underscoring that deterioration in a subset of nations can offset broad-based progress.

2. Regional & Country-Level Comparison

Geographic patterns in homicide rates are stark. The Americas consistently report the highest median rates of any region, while Europe and Oceania sit at the opposite extreme. Africa’s data remain comparatively sparse, which limits robust regional inference.

Figure 3. Median homicide rate trends by world region. The Americas maintain a consistently elevated trajectory.

In 2023, the Americas recorded a regional median of 17.75 per 100,000—nearly ten times Europe’s median of 0.89. Asia and Oceania clustered in the low single digits (1.57 and 1.46, respectively). Africa’s median of 5.34 was based on only four reporting countries, highlighting a critical data gap rather than a definitive regional estimate.

3. Gender Disparity in Homicide Victimization

One of the most consistent findings across the dataset is the profound over-representation of males among homicide victims. Across 139 countries with matched male and female data in their most recent reporting year, the median male homicide rate is 3.95 per 100,000 compared with 1.39 per 100,000 for females—a 3.3× disparity at the median.

Figure 5. Left: Male vs. female homicide rates by country (most recent year). Right: Distribution of the male-to-female ratio.

The ratio distribution is right-skewed: while half of countries show a ratio below 3.3, a quarter exceed 6.3×, and several outliers surpass 15×. The highest recorded ratios among substantial populations include the United States Virgin Islands (27.2×), Barbados (21.7×), and Venezuela (17.6×). These figures signal not merely elevated male risk, but contexts where female victimization—while absolutely lower—may be driven by distinct etiologies (e.g., intimate-partner violence) that demand targeted policy responses.

Gender Ratio by Age Group

Disaggregating by age reveals that the male-female gap widens dramatically during adolescence and young adulthood. Among 15–17 year-olds, the median male rate is nearly 4× the female rate; in the 25–29 bracket, the ratio is approximately 3.6×. Interestingly, the ratio is narrowest for children under 10 (≈1.3×), suggesting that gender-differentiated exposure to violence emerges primarily in later childhood and adolescence.

Policy implication: Crime-prevention strategies must be gender-informed. Interventions targeting young men in high-violence settings—employment programs, gang diversion, and community policing—are likely to yield disproportionate reductions in overall homicide counts, while parallel efforts are needed to address female victimization pathways, particularly intimate-partner homicide.

4. Age Group Analysis

Because the dataset reports age-disaggregated data only by sex (not for "both" combined), the analysis below presents male and female age patterns separately. This is an important limitation: the reader should not interpret the sum of male and female rates as a total population rate without population-weighting.

Figure 6. Median homicide rate by age group and sex. Young adults (20–44) face the highest victimization risk, particularly males.

For males, the risk profile rises steeply from childhood, peaks in the 30–44 age bracket (median 4.08 per 100,000), and remains elevated through middle age before declining after 60. For females, the pattern is flatter: the 30–44 group also records the highest median (1.39), but the absolute levels are far lower and the gradient across age groups is less pronounced.

These patterns align with criminological evidence that homicide victimization is concentrated among young adult males, often linked to street violence, organized crime, and gang activity. The relative safety of children under 10 and adults over 60—across both sexes—suggests that protective factors associated with age (reduced street exposure, lower criminal-justice system contact) operate globally.

Note on data limitation: Age-group coverage is uneven. Some countries report only broad bands, while others provide granular splits (e.g., 18–19, 20–24). Cross-country age comparisons should therefore be treated as indicative rather than precise.

6. Data Limitations & Policy Recommendations

Data Limitations

This analysis is bounded by the structure and coverage of the underlying UNODC dataset. Readers should bear the following caveats in mind:

  • Unbalanced panel: Not all countries report every year. The set of reporting nations shifts over time, which can introduce composition effects into global and regional averages. For example, the drop in mean rate during the 2010s partly reflects both genuine reductions and changes in which countries submitted data.
  • Coverage decline: Reporting coverage peaked at 137 countries (68.2% of the 201-country universe) in 2019 but fell to 84 (41.8%) by 2023. The post-2020 decline likely reflects pandemic-related disruptions to statistical systems, but it severely constrains confidence in recent global estimates.
  • UK caveat: United Kingdom data refer to England and Wales only. Scotland and Northern Ireland are reported separately where available and should not be double-counted.
  • Territories: Some territories (e.g., Hong Kong, Macao, Puerto Rico) are listed separately. Analysts should avoid aggregating these with their parent states.
  • Sex-disaggregated age data: Age-group breakdowns are available only for male and female categories, not for the total population. Direct cross-country age comparisons are further complicated by varying age-band definitions.

Policy Recommendations

Based on the evidence presented, the following recommendations are offered for policy analysts and public safety stakeholders:

  1. Target the Americas and the Caribbean: The concentration of both high-rate countries and rapid deteriorations in this region demands prioritized resource allocation. Regional cooperation on firearms control, gang intervention, and justice-system strengthening should be scaled.
  2. Invest in male-focused violence prevention: With males victimized at 3–4× the rate of females in the median country, programs targeting young men—employment, mentorship, and hotspot policing—offer high leverage for reducing overall homicide burdens.
  3. Protect fragile recoveries: Countries such as El Salvador and Colombia show that dramatic reductions are possible, but these gains require sustained institutional support. Donors and international bodies should prioritize long-term engagement over short-term project cycles.
  4. Close data gaps: The decline in post-2020 reporting is alarming. International organizations should fund national statistical capacity, particularly in Africa and conflict-affected states, where coverage is thinnest and need for evidence-based policy is greatest.
  5. Adopt gender-responsive homicide prevention: While male victimization dominates aggregate counts, female homicide—often intimate-partner related—requires distinct prevention frameworks (e.g., domestic-violence laws, victim services, and firearm access restrictions for known perpetrators).
Bottom line: The world is becoming safer for the typical country, but not for all. The median homicide rate has fallen by roughly two-thirds since 1993, yet outliers in the Caribbean, Central America, and parts of South America continue to drive the global mean upward. Targeted, evidence-based interventions—especially those focused on young men and fragile states—remain essential to ensuring that global progress is shared equitably.
