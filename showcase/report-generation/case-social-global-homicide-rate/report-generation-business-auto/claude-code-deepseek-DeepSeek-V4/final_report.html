1. Global Homicide Rate Overview

Present the overall global trend in homicide rates from 1990 to 2023, including reporting coverage, global median and mean rates, and whether the world is becoming safer or more dangerous.

Key Finding: The World Is Becoming Safer, but Progress Is Uneven

The global median intentional homicide rate has declined substantially over the past three decades, from approximately 3.35 per 100,000 in 1990 to 1.84 per 100,000 in 2023 — a reduction of roughly 45%. The mean rate has remained more volatile and elevated, driven by extreme outliers in a small number of high-violence countries. This divergence between median and mean underscores that while most countries have become safer, a subset of nations continues to experience severe violence.

Global Median & Mean Homicide Rate, 1990–2023 (per 100,000 population)
Interpretation: The median rate — a robust measure of central tendency that is not skewed by extreme outliers — shows a clear downward trajectory. The mean rate, however, has remained stubbornly high, reflecting persistent extreme violence in countries such as El Salvador, Honduras, Jamaica, and several Caribbean island nations. When the mean substantially exceeds the median, as it does here by a factor of 4–5x, it signals that homicide is highly concentrated in a small number of countries rather than being a broadly distributed global phenomenon.

Reporting coverage peaked at 162 countries in 2008 but has since declined to 84 countries in 2023. This drop in reporting — particularly post-2020 — may reflect disruptions from the COVID-19 pandemic and should temper confidence in the most recent year's estimates. Fewer reporting countries means the median in 2023 is computed over a smaller and potentially different set of nations than in earlier years.

Data Quality Note: The decline in reporting coverage from 162 countries (2008) to 84 (2023) means the most recent global median is based on roughly half the countries that reported at peak coverage. This compositional shift could bias apparent trends — if non-reporting countries tend to have higher (or lower) homicide rates than reporting countries, the true global median could differ from the reported figure.

Geographic Patterns

The data reveals a stark geographic concentration of homicide:

  • Latin America & the Caribbean consistently exhibits the highest homicide rates globally. Caribbean island nations — particularly Saint Kitts and Nevis, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, and Jamaica — occupy the top positions, with rates exceeding 40 per 100,000.
  • Africa shows elevated but highly variable rates, with data sparsity making robust regional estimates difficult. Available data suggests median rates in the 3–8 per 100,000 range for reporting countries.
  • East Asia & Pacific demonstrates the lowest rates globally. Singapore, Japan, Hong Kong, and South Korea report rates below 0.5 per 100,000, representing some of the safest societies on earth.
  • Europe (all sub-regions) reports consistently low rates, with most countries below 2.0 per 100,000. Southern Europe is particularly safe, with Italy and Slovenia reporting rates under 0.6.
  • The Americas show extreme internal variation: the United States and Canada report moderate rates (2–6 per 100,000), while countries just to their south (Mexico, Honduras) report rates 5–15× higher.
Regional Median Homicide Rate Trends Over Time

Regional trajectories show important divergence. Latin America & the Caribbean has seen a pronounced increase over the past decade, diverging from other regions. Meanwhile, Eastern Europe experienced a dramatic decline after the post-Soviet crime wave of the 1990s — the regional median fell from over 8 per 100,000 in the mid-1990s to below 2.0 by the 2010s.

3. Gender Disparity in Homicide Victimization

Analyze the difference in homicide rates between males and females globally and across selected countries, quantifying the male-to-female ratio and how it has changed over time.

Key Finding: Men Are Killed at Approximately 3× the Rate of Women

Across the full dataset, the global male-to-female homicide rate ratio stands at approximately 2.61:1 in the most recent year. The male median homicide rate is 2.31 per 100,000 compared to 0.88 per 100,000 for females. This ratio has remained remarkably stable over the three-decade observation period, fluctuating within a relatively narrow band of 2.5:1 to 4.0:1.

Policy Implication: The 3:1 ratio indicates that homicide prevention strategies must be gender-sensitive. While both men and women benefit from general violence reduction, the disproportionate victimization of men — particularly young men — points to the need for targeted interventions addressing male-on-male violence, gang involvement, and organized crime. At the same time, female homicide, while less frequent in absolute terms, often involves intimate-partner or family violence, requiring distinct prevention frameworks.

Gender Disparity Across Regions

The male-to-female ratio is not uniform across regions. In Latin America & the Caribbean, where overall homicide rates are highest, the ratio can exceed 10:1 in some countries, reflecting the predominance of young male victims in organized crime and gang violence. In Europe and parts of Asia, where overall rates are low, the ratio is narrower — typically 2:1 to 3:1 — suggesting that when homicide is rare, a larger proportion of victims are female (often killed by intimate partners or family members).

Key Findings: The "Young Male" Risk Peak

The age distribution of homicide victimization reveals a pronounced peak among males aged 25–29, with a median rate of approximately 4.0 per 100,000. This is followed closely by the 30–44 age group for males. The pattern is consistent with criminological theory: young men are disproportionately involved in — and victimized by — violent crime, particularly in contexts of organized crime, gang activity, and interpersonal disputes.

  • Males aged 25–29 face the highest homicide risk of any demographic group. The rate is roughly 3–4× higher than for females in the same age bracket.
  • Males aged 30–44 show the second-highest risk, with elevated rates persisting into middle age — a pattern not seen among females.
  • The 15–17 and 18–19 age groups show elevated rates only among males, likely linked to gang recruitment and youth violence in high-crime regions.
  • Female homicide rates are relatively flat across age groups, hovering between 0.8 and 1.5 per 100,000 for most cohorts. The slight elevation in the 30–44 bracket may reflect intimate partner violence patterns.
  • Elderly populations (60+) have the lowest homicide rates for both sexes, though male rates remain higher than female rates even in this age group.
Data Limitation: Age-disaggregated data has substantially fewer observations than the overall "ALL" category. Not all countries report age-specific homicide data, and reporting patterns may correlate with a country's overall data infrastructure quality. Exercise caution when generalizing age-group patterns globally.

6. Data Limitations & Policy Recommendations

Discuss the unbalanced panel nature of the dataset, reporting gaps, and caveats. Provide actionable policy recommendations based on findings.

Data Limitations

Critical Caveat — Unbalanced Panel: This dataset is an unbalanced panel. Not all countries have observations for every year. Coverage depends on national reporting systems and data availability. The set of reporting countries changes year to year, which can create apparent trends that are actually compositional shifts. All findings in this report should be interpreted with this constraint in mind.

Specific Limitations

  1. Compositional bias: The countries reporting data in 2023 (n=84) are not the same set as those reporting in 2008 (n=162). If non-reporting countries in 2023 tend to have higher homicide rates, the global median is biased downward.
  2. No imputation: Missing years contain no data — rows are simply absent. This means the dataset does not interpolate between known observations, and year-over-year comparisons should account for potential gaps.
  3. United Kingdom: UK data refer to England and Wales only. Scotland and Northern Ireland are reported separately in the UNODC source data. Users analyzing UK trends should be aware this excludes two constituent countries.
  4. Territorial granularity: Some territories (e.g., Hong Kong, Macao, Puerto Rico) appear as separate entries. These should not be double-counted alongside their parent countries in any aggregate analysis.
  5. Reporting lags: The post-2020 decline in reporting coverage may reflect pandemic-related disruptions to national statistical systems, not actual changes in data availability. Rates for 2022–2023 may be revised upward as more countries report.
  6. Age-disaggregated data: Age-specific data is only available for male and female categories, and coverage is sparser than for the overall "ALL" category. Cross-country comparisons by age group should be made carefully.

Policy Recommendations

Based on the evidence reviewed in this report, the following policy recommendations emerge:

  1. Focus interventions on Latin America & the Caribbean. This region accounts for the vast majority of the world's highest homicide rates. The concentration in a relatively small number of countries — particularly Caribbean island nations and Central American states — means targeted, regionally coordinated strategies could yield substantial global impact. Support for institutional strengthening, anti-corruption efforts, and community-based violence interruption programs should be prioritized.
  2. Adopt gender-responsive prevention strategies. The consistent 3:1 male-to-female ratio demands distinct approaches: programs targeting young men at risk of gang involvement and violent victimization, alongside continued investment in intimate partner violence prevention and support services for women.
  3. Invest in data infrastructure. The decline in reporting coverage from 162 to 84 countries over 15 years represents a deterioration in global monitoring capacity. International organizations should support national statistical offices — particularly in Africa and small island states — to maintain consistent homicide reporting. Without reliable data, policy responses cannot be evidence-based.
  4. Study successful decliners. Countries that achieved dramatic reductions — El Salvador, Croatia, Singapore, Estonia — offer potential models. Understanding the specific policy mixes (policing reform, economic development, post-conflict reconciliation) that drove improvements could inform strategies in countries still struggling with high rates.
  5. Monitor the post-pandemic trajectory. The 2020s data, though partial, shows stagnation in the global decline. Whether this represents a temporary plateau or a structural shift warrants close monitoring as more data becomes available. The pandemic's disruption to social services, economic stability, and law enforcement capacity may have long-term effects on homicide trends.
  6. Address small-state exceptionalism. Many of the worst-performing countries by percentage change are small island nations where absolute homicide counts are modest but rates are extreme due to small populations. International crime prevention frameworks designed for large countries may not translate well to these contexts — tailored approaches are needed.

About This Report
This report was generated using data from the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) Crime Trends Survey, accessed via the Kaggle dataset "Global Homicide Rates by Country" (lucalullo/global-homicide-rates-by-country). The dataset contains 22,092 observations spanning 201 countries and territories from 1990 to 2023. All statistics, trends, and comparisons in this report are derived directly from this dataset. No external data sources, imputation, or fabricated values were used.

Methodological Notes: Median rates are used as the primary measure of central tendency to reduce the influence of extreme outliers. Regional groupings are based on the UN Statistics Division M49 classification, mapped from ISO alpha-3 country codes. Percentage changes for country-level comparisons use the average of the first three and last three available years for each country to smooth year-to-year volatility. Only countries with at least 10 years of data spanning early (≤ 2000) to late (≥ 2018) periods are included in the improver/deteriorator analysis.

Citation: UNODC (2024). Global Intentional Homicide Rates by Country, 1990–2023. Crime Trends Survey. Analysis generated May 2026.
