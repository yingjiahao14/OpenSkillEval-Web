Section 1: Global Homicide Rate Overview
Time-series of global median and mean homicide rates (1990–2023), sex=both, age_group=ALL. Rates are per 100,000 population.

Is the world getting safer? The global median homicide rate has declined substantially — from 3.35 per 100,000 in 1990 to 1.84 in 2023, a drop of approximately 45% over 33 years. This is a meaningful positive trend sustained across multiple decades.

However, the global mean rate (which is sensitive to high-rate outliers) remains persistently elevated at 8.29 in 2023, nearly unchanged from 8.03 in 2000. This divergence between median and mean signals deep inequality: a small number of high-homicide countries drag the mean up while the typical country has improved substantially.

Reporting coverage peaked at ~80.6% (2018) and has since declined to 41.8% in 2023, reflecting typical data lag. Counts of reporting countries rose from 94 in 1990 to a peak of 162 in 2008, then stabilized in the 130–160 range through the 2010s.

Median Rate: -45% since 1990

The typical country's homicide rate has halved. Most improvement occurred in the 2010s, driven by declining rates in Latin America and Eastern Europe.

Mean Remains High

The mean rate in 2023 (8.29) exceeds the 2000 rate (8.03), reflecting persistent crises in Caribbean and parts of Latin American nations.

2023 Coverage Caveat

Only 84 countries reported in 2023. The 2023 figures should be interpreted with caution as many nations have not yet submitted official data.

Geographic Patterns. The highest homicide rates are overwhelmingly concentrated in the Caribbean and Latin American region: Turks and Caicos Islands (76.3), Saint Kitts and Nevis (64.2), Saint Vincent and the Grenadines (51.3), Jamaica (49.4), Ecuador (45.7), South Africa (43.7), Haiti (41.2), Trinidad and Tobago (40.4), and Saint Lucia (39.0) dominate the top tier. The United States Virgin Islands also appears at 49.9 (2012 data).

Small island territories face distinctive security challenges including limited law enforcement capacity, narcotics transit routes, and concentrated gang activity. South Africa's persistent high rate reflects structural socioeconomic inequality combined with high firearm prevalence.

At the opposite extreme, the safest countries include Singapore (0.07 per 100,000), alongside several territories with zero recorded homicides in their most recent reporting year (American Samoa, Holy See, Monaco, San Marino, Tuvalu, etc.), though these represent very small populations or territories with limited reporting exposure.

A Persistent and Widening Gap. In 1990, the global mean homicide rate for males was 11.19 per 100,000 compared to 2.44 for females — a ratio of 4.58:1. By 2023, the male rate reached 14.76 while the female rate moderated to 2.16, widening the ratio to 6.85:1. This is not a statistical artifact — it reflects deep structural patterns in violence perpetration and victimization.

Interpreting the male rate trajectory: Male rates climbed from 11.19 in 1990 to a peak of 17.11 in 2009, before declining and then rising again to 14.76 in 2023. The surge in the 2000s aligns with drug-related violence escalation across Latin America and the Caribbean. Female rates have remained comparatively stable in the range of 1.8–3.5 throughout the period.

Policy relevance: The predominantly male victimization profile reflects male-dominated participation in gang activity, armed conflict, and criminally-motivated violence. Female homicide victimization, while lower in absolute terms, often reflects intimate partner violence (femicide) — a distinct phenomenon requiring targeted policy interventions.

Ratio Widened from 4.58 to 6.85

The male-to-female ratio increased by 49% between 1990 and 2023, driven by rising male victimization in conflict-affected and high-crime territories.

Female Rate Held Steady

Female rates remained in the 1.8–3.5 band across the entire period. The 2023 female mean of 2.16 is lower than the 1990 value of 2.44.

Male Rates Peaked in 2009

Male mean rate peaked at 17.11 in 2009, correlating with the height of drug-related violence in Mexico, Central America, and the Caribbean.

Young Adult Males Face Dramatically Elevated Risk. Among males, the age groups 25–29 (29.4 per 100,000) and 20–24 (26.5) carry the highest homicide burden globally — rates approximately 35 times higher than children aged 0–9 (0.83). The 30–44 bracket (22.9) and 18–19 bracket (20.3) also show significantly elevated risk, confirming that the core at-risk demographic is young adult males.

Female age group pattern differs markedly. Female victimization peaks in late adolescence and early adulthood (18–19: 3.54; 20–24: 3.08; 25–29: 3.23) rather than following the sharp 20–29 spike seen in males. This is consistent with intimate partner violence being a dominant driver — risks associated with early partnerships rather than street violence.

Children (0–9): Both male (0.83) and female (0.56) rates in the youngest age group are low but non-trivial — child homicide victimization, often at the hands of family members, represents a distinct policy concern requiring child protection frameworks.

Peak Risk: Males 25–29

At 29.4 per 100,000, men aged 25–29 face the highest homicide risk of any demographic group globally — more than 35 times the rate of young children.

Young Adult Cliff

Male risk rises sharply from 1.0 (age 10–14) to 8.7 (15–17) to 20.3 (18–19) — a 20-fold increase across the teenage years.

Female Peak: 18–19

Female victimization peaks at 3.54 in the 18–19 cohort, reflecting early intimate partner violence exposure rather than street-crime dynamics.

El Salvador: The Most Dramatic Reduction on Record. El Salvador recorded a rate of 134.3 per 100,000 in 1994 — among the highest ever documented globally. By 2022, this had fallen to 7.9, a reduction of 126.4 percentage points (−94%). This followed aggressive security crackdowns including mass gang prosecutions beginning in 2016 and intensified from 2022 under President Bukele's emergency security measures. While controversial regarding human rights, the statistical homicide reduction is unambiguous.

Colombia's Long Decline. Colombia's rate fell from 75.0 (1990) to 24.9 (2023), a reduction of 50 per 100,000 (−67%). The trajectory reflects the dismantling of major cartels, the 2016 FARC peace agreement, and sustained institutional strengthening — though rates remain high by global standards.

The Caribbean Crisis. Saint Kitts and Nevis (+579%), Saint Vincent and the Grenadines (+422%), Ecuador (+437%), and Haiti (+694% from 2007 baseline) show alarming deterioration. Ecuador's transformation from a low-homicide nation to a high-crisis state is especially notable, driven by the explosion of drug cartel activity following the fragmentation of Colombian trafficking networks from 2020 onward.

Section 6: Data Limitations & Policy Recommendations
Methodological caveats and evidence-based policy guidance derived from this analysis.

Data Limitations

Unbalanced Panel Data

The dataset is an unbalanced panel: not all countries report data for all years. Coverage ranges from 47% (1990s) to 81% (2018), dropping to 42% in 2023 due to reporting lag. Cross-year comparisons of means and medians are affected by which countries participate in each year.

UK Coverage Scope

Data attributed to the United Kingdom covers England and Wales only and excludes Scotland and Northern Ireland. This understates the overall UK rate by omitting Northern Ireland's historically higher homicide rates during the Troubles era.

Small Territory Volatility

Small-population territories (population <100,000) exhibit extreme rate volatility — a single homicide can shift the rate by many points. Monaco, San Marino, Holy See, and similar entities should be treated as statistical outliers rather than policy benchmarks.

Age Group Data Sparsity

Age-disaggregated data is only available for male and female categories (not "both"), with coverage beginning from 2003 onward for some groups. Conclusions about specific age groups should acknowledge this partial data coverage.

Definition Variation

Intentional homicide definitions vary by jurisdiction. Some nations use police-recorded data; others use vital registration systems. Methodological harmonization by UNODC reduces but does not eliminate cross-national definitional inconsistencies.

Reporting Incentives

Countries with high homicide rates may under-report to avoid international stigma. Countries in conflict or state fragility may simply lack capacity to collect accurate mortality statistics, leading to systematic underestimation in the most affected contexts.

Policy Recommendations

  • 1
    Prioritize young male violence prevention (ages 18–29). The data consistently shows that men aged 20–29 carry the highest homicide burden globally. Prevention strategies targeting gang recruitment, youth unemployment, and drug market involvement in this demographic offer the greatest potential for rate reduction. El Salvador and Colombia provide instructive case studies on what sustained, if controversial, intervention can achieve.
  • 2
    Implement gender-differentiated violence response frameworks. The nearly 7-fold male-to-female homicide ratio reflects fundamentally different underlying violence mechanisms. Female homicide (particularly intimate partner femicide) requires dedicated legal protection frameworks, while male victimization requires street-violence and organized-crime interventions. One-size-fits-all policies risk missing both targets.
  • 3
    Address the Caribbean and Latin American crisis with region-specific strategies. Eight of the top 10 highest-rate jurisdictions are in the Caribbean or Latin America. The concentration of extreme rates in small island territories with drug trafficking transit routes suggests coordinated regional security frameworks — including enhanced maritime interdiction, anti-money-laundering capacity, and judicial system strengthening — are prerequisites for meaningful progress.
  • 4
    Expand and sustain global homicide data reporting infrastructure. The decline in reporting coverage from 81% (2018) to 42% (2023) is a significant data governance failure. UNODC and national statistical agencies should invest in standardized, real-time vital registration systems. The countries with the worst data gaps are frequently those facing the most acute violence challenges.
  • 5
    Replicate success factors from declining nations. Colombia's 67% reduction over 33 years and El Salvador's 94% reduction over 28 years demonstrate that dramatic improvements are achievable. Systematic policy learning — understanding what worked, with what trade-offs, and under which institutional conditions — should inform high-burden country strategies.
  • 6
    Monitor the Ecuador crisis as an early-warning signal. Ecuador's transformation from a low-homicide country (8.5 in 1990) to a high-crisis state (45.7 in 2023) is a case study in how rapidly violence can escalate when organized crime fills governance vacuums. Early-intervention frameworks triggered by rate thresholds (e.g., 15 per 100,000) could enable faster international response in similarly vulnerable nations.
