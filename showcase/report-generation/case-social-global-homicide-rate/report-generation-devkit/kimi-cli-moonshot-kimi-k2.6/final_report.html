Executive Summary

Is the world becoming safer from intentional homicide? The evidence is cautiously optimistic—but highly uneven. Between 1990 and 2023, the global median homicide rate declined from approximately 3.4 to 1.8 per 100,000 population in the most recent reporting year. The 2000s to 2010s saw the most pronounced improvement, with the global mean falling by 9.6%. However, progress has stalled since the 2010s, and the 2020s show essentially flat trends.

Yet averages conceal stark disparities. Males are homicide victims at roughly three times the rate of females globally—a ratio that has remained remarkably persistent across decades. Young adult males (ages 20–29) face the highest risk of any demographic group. At the country level, a small number of Caribbean and Latin American nations continue to experience extreme homicide burdens, while several East Asian and European jurisdictions report rates below 1 per 100,000.

Critical caveat: These findings must be interpreted in light of significant data gaps. In 2023, only 84 of 201 countries and territories (42%) reported homicide data. The unbalanced panel means year-to-year comparisons reflect both real changes and shifts in which countries are reporting.

1. Global Homicide Rate Overview

The trajectory of global homicide rates over the past three decades reveals a story of broad improvement followed by plateau. The chart below tracks both the median and mean homicide rates across all reporting countries from 1990 to 2023.

The median rate is the more robust indicator here, as it is less sensitive to extreme outliers (such as countries experiencing civil conflict or organized-crime surges). The median fell steadily from the early 1990s through the mid-2010s, bottoming near 2.3 per 100,000 around 2019, before ticking down further to 1.8 in 2023—though the 2023 figure is based on a reduced reporting pool.

Reporting Coverage

Coverage peaked in the mid-2010s at roughly 130 countries (≈65% of the panel), but has fallen sharply since 2020, reaching just 42% in 2023. This decline is not random: lower-income and conflict-affected states are disproportionately represented among non-reporters, meaning recent global aggregates may understate true worldwide homicide levels.

3. Gender Disparity in Homicide Victimization

One of the most consistent findings across the entire dataset is the profound gender imbalance in homicide victimization. Males are killed at substantially higher rates than females in virtually every country and year for which sex-disaggregated data exist.

The median male-to-female ratio across reporting countries has hovered between 3:1 and 3.5:1 for most of the past two decades. In 2023, the median ratio was 3.2:1. The mean ratio is higher (around 4.5:1) because a subset of countries exhibit extreme ratios—driven either by very high male victimization or by extremely low female rates.

Key insight: The male-to-female ratio has proven remarkably stable over time. While absolute rates have risen and fallen in many countries, the relative risk faced by men versus women has changed little. This suggests that the underlying mechanisms driving gender disparity—such as male involvement in violent offending networks, gang activity, and street violence—are structurally persistent.

4. Age Group Analysis

Age is a critical but underreported dimension of homicide risk. Because many countries do not systematically disaggregate by age, the analysis below draws on the subset of observations (primarily from the 2010s onward) where age-specific data are available.

The pattern is unmistakable: young adults face the highest homicide risk, and this is overwhelmingly driven by male victimization. For males, median rates rise steeply from childhood, peak in the 25–29 and 30–44 age brackets (median ≈ 4.0 per 100,000 across all reporting years), and then decline gradually into older age. For females, the age gradient is far flatter, with median rates staying below 1.5 per 100,000 across all age groups.

5. Notable Country-Level Trends & Outliers

Beyond snapshots of any single year, the most compelling policy stories lie in how countries have changed over time. The following charts highlight the countries with the largest absolute increases and decreases between their first and most recent observations (minimum 10-year span and 3 data points).

Remarkable Improvements

El Salvador stands as the most dramatic turnaround. From a peak of 138.4 per 100,000 in 1995, the country’s rate fell to 7.9 by 2022—a decline of 94%. Colombia similarly reduced its rate from 85.9 (1991) to 24.9 (2023), a 67% improvement. Other notable declines include the Philippines (down 71%), Russia (down 52%), and Latvia (down 75%).

These improvements are not merely statistical artifacts; they correspond to documented policy and social changes: strengthened criminal justice institutions, demobilization of armed groups, economic stabilization, and targeted violence-reduction programs in urban hotspots.

Alarming Deteriorations

On the other side, several Caribbean and Latin American nations have experienced severe deteriorations. Ecuador rose from 8.5 (1990) to 45.7 (2023). Haiti climbed from 5.2 (2007) to 41.2 (2023). Honduras increased from 9.9 (1990) to 31.4 (2023), though it has moderated from an even higher peak in the early 2010s.

Outlier alert: Small-island Caribbean states exhibit extreme volatility due to small populations. A handful of homicides can produce rate spikes that appear dramatic in per-capita terms. Analysts should treat rates in jurisdictions with populations under 100,000 with particular caution.

Contextual Factors

While this report does not attempt causal attribution, the data patterns are consistent with established criminological research:

  • Improvements often follow periods of institutional consolidation, economic growth, and targeted policing reforms.
  • Deteriorations frequently coincide with narcotics trafficking expansion, gang fragmentation, political instability, and erosion of judicial capacity.
  • Volatility in small states suggests that localized shocks—natural disasters, tourism collapses, or single criminal-group conflicts—can have outsized statistical effects.

6. Data Limitations & Policy Recommendations

Data Limitations

This analysis is bounded by the structure and coverage of the UNODC dataset. Users should bear the following limitations in mind:

  • Unbalanced panel: Not all countries report in every year. The composition of the reporting pool changes over time, which can create spurious trends in global aggregates.
  • Coverage decline: After peaking at roughly 65% of countries in the mid-2010s, coverage has fallen to just 42% in 2023. Recent global figures are therefore less representative than those from earlier periods.
  • UK coverage: Data for the United Kingdom refer to England and Wales only. Scotland and Northern Ireland are reported separately where available and should not be conflated with the UK figure.
  • Territories: Some dependent territories (e.g., Hong Kong, Macao, Puerto Rico) are reported separately. These should not be double-counted with their parent states.
  • No imputation: Missing years were not interpolated. Any trend analysis that treats absent data as zero would be seriously misleading.
  • Small-population volatility: Countries and territories with very small populations can show extreme rate fluctuations from year to year that do not reflect meaningful changes in absolute violence levels.
Recommendation for analysts: When comparing across years, restrict analysis to the subset of countries that report in both periods. When comparing across countries, use the most recent available year but acknowledge that the set of reporting countries may differ.

Policy Recommendations

Based on the evidence presented in this report, the following actions are recommended for policymakers and public safety stakeholders:

  1. Target young adult males. The concentration of risk among males aged 15–34 is the single most consistent finding across countries and years. Violence-reduction investments—employment programs, mentorship, gang diversion, and hotspot policing—should prioritize this demographic.
  2. Learn from improvers. Countries such as El Salvador, Colombia, and Latvia demonstrate that dramatic reductions are possible. Systematic documentation and knowledge transfer of the policies and institutional reforms that drove these declines should be a priority for international cooperation.
  3. Address Caribbean violence. The clustering of the highest national rates in the Caribbean demands regional attention. Small-island states face unique challenges—limited forensic capacity, tourism-driven drug transshipment, and gang fragmentation—that require tailored, regional responses.
  4. Invest in data infrastructure. The decline in global reporting coverage since 2020 is alarming. Donor governments and international organizations should prioritize technical assistance for vital registration, police-record digitization, and UNODC reporting compliance, especially in sub-Saharan Africa and conflict-affected regions where data are sparsest.
  5. Disaggregate by sex and age. Global aggregates obscure critical disparities. National statistical systems should be supported to produce and publish sex- and age-disaggregated homicide data as standard practice.
  6. Monitor post-2020 trajectories. The COVID-19 pandemic and its economic aftermath appear to have disrupted both crime patterns and data collection. Close monitoring of 2024–2026 data will be essential to determine whether the 2010s gains are sustained or reversed.
