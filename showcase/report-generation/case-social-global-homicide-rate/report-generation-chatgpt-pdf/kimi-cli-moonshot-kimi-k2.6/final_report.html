Executive Summary

This report examines three decades of intentional homicide data from the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) to answer a central question: Is the world becoming safer from intentional homicide, and for whom? The evidence points to a nuanced picture. While the global median homicide rate has declined from roughly 3.0 per 100,000 in the mid-1990s to 1.84 in 2023, the global mean remains elevated at 8.29, driven by a small number of extremely high-rate countries. Decade-over-decade analysis reveals a modest improvement from the 2000s to the 2010s (−9.6%), but the 2020s have seen a slight rebound (+0.2%).

Gender disparity remains stark and persistent: males are homicide victims at a median rate roughly 3.2× that of females, a ratio that has changed little over the observation period. Young adults—particularly males aged 18–29—face the highest risk. Meanwhile, several Caribbean and Latin American nations continue to report alarming rates, while a number of Asian and European countries have achieved dramatic long-term reductions.

Data Caveat: This is an unbalanced panel. Only 41.8% of the 201 countries in the dataset reported data for 2023. Coverage peaked in 2019 at 137 countries but has since fallen. Comparisons across time and geography must be interpreted with this limitation in mind.

1. Global Homicide Rate Overview

The global trajectory of intentional homicide is best understood by distinguishing between the median and mean. The median—the rate of the typical reporting country—has trended downward since the mid-1990s, suggesting that the "average" nation has become safer. However, the mean is heavily skewed by outliers, particularly in the Caribbean and Central America, and has fluctuated between 6.8 and 8.5 over the past decade.

The dotted grey line overlays the number of countries reporting each year, highlighting a critical data quality issue. Coverage expanded steadily from the early 1990s, peaked at 137 countries in 2019, but dropped sharply during the COVID-19 era to just 84 countries by 2023. This decline means recent global aggregates may not be directly comparable to earlier years: the composition of reporting nations has shifted, and some high-rate countries may be underrepresented in the latest figures.

Decade-level aggregation smooths out year-to-year volatility. The 1990s and 2000s were comparably violent on average (8.27 and 8.51 respectively). The 2010s registered a meaningful decline to 7.70, but the 2020s—to date—have seen a marginal uptick to 7.71. Whether this represents a genuine reversal or a reporting-artifact remains uncertain given the shrinking panel.

3. Gender Disparity in Homicide Victimization

One of the most consistent findings across the dataset is the profound gender imbalance in homicide victimization. Males are killed at a median rate roughly three times that of females, and in many countries the ratio is far higher. In 2023, the median male homicide rate among reporting countries was 2.31 per 100,000, compared with 0.88 for females.

The male-to-female ratio has remained remarkably stable over three decades, fluctuating between approximately 2.9 and 3.5 at the median. This persistence suggests that the structural drivers of male victimization—gang involvement, firearm violence, and street-level conflict—are deeply entrenched and resistant to broad secular trends. The mean ratio is higher (around 4.3–4.7) because a subset of countries exhibits extreme skew, driven by very high male rates in conflict or gang-affected settings.

It is important to note that while males dominate aggregate victim counts, females may face distinct risk profiles—particularly intimate-partner and family-related homicide—that are not fully captured by overall rate comparisons. The UNODC data do not disaggregate relationship to perpetrator, so this analysis is limited to sex-disaggregated rates only.

4. Age Group Analysis

Age-disaggregated data reveal that homicide is disproportionately a killer of young adults. The 25–29 age group records the highest mean rate globally (16.6 per 100,000), followed closely by 20–24 (14.9) and 18–19 (12.1). Children under 10 and adolescents 10–14 face the lowest risk, although any non-zero rate in these groups is especially concerning from a child-protection perspective.

The risk profile is not uniform across sex. When disaggregated, males aged 18–29 show median rates an order of magnitude higher than their female peers. The following chart compares median rates by age group and sex on a logarithmic scale to accommodate the wide range.

For males, the peak risk window is 25–29 (median 4.0), with elevated rates extending from 18–19 through 30–44. For females, risk is more evenly distributed across age groups, with a modest peak in the 25–29 and 30–44 brackets (median ~1.1–1.4). Elderly populations (60+) face moderate risk relative to youth, but again with a strong male skew.

Policy implication: Violence-prevention programs targeting males aged 15–29—through education, employment, and gang-disengagement interventions—are likely to yield the largest reductions in overall homicide burden.

5. Notable Country-Level Trends & Outliers

Beyond snapshot comparisons, longitudinal analysis reveals dramatic divergences in national trajectories. The following charts highlight countries with the largest percentage changes between their earliest and latest available observations over a span of at least ten years.

Remarkable Success Stories

El Salvador stands out as the most dramatic turnaround: from a peak of 134.3 per 100,000 in 1994 to 7.9 in 2022—a decline of 94%. Singapore and Hong Kong both reduced already-low rates by over 80%, cementing their positions among the world's safest jurisdictions. Italy and Macao also achieved reductions exceeding 80% from 1990s baselines. These cases demonstrate that steep declines are possible across diverse institutional and cultural contexts.

Alarming Deteriorations

On the other end of the spectrum, Haiti saw its rate surge from 5.2 in 2007 to 41.2 in 2023—a nearly sevenfold increase amid political instability and gang proliferation. Saint Kitts and Nevis climbed from 9.5 in 1995 to 64.2 in 2023, while Ecuador and Saint Vincent and the Grenadines both more than quintupled their rates. These deteriorations are concentrated in the Caribbean and parts of Latin America, reinforcing the region's status as the global epicenter of lethal violence.

Volatility

Several small states and territories exhibit extreme year-to-year volatility, driven by low population denominators and stochastic event counts. San Marino, Saint Helena, and Tuvalu show the highest coefficients of variation. For these jurisdictions, single-year figures should be interpreted with caution; multi-year averages are more reliable indicators of underlying risk.

6. Data Limitations & Policy Recommendations

Data Limitations

This analysis is subject to important constraints that affect interpretation:

  • Unbalanced panel: Not all countries report every year. The set of reporting nations changes over time, which can introduce compositional bias into global and regional averages.
  • Coverage decline: Reporting coverage fell from a peak of 137 countries (68% of the panel) in 2019 to just 84 (42%) in 2023. The reasons for this decline—whether delayed statistical processing, resource constraints, or political factors—are unknown from the data alone.
  • United Kingdom caveat: UK data refer to England and Wales only. Scotland and Northern Ireland are reported separately where available and should not be double-counted.
  • Territories: Some territories (e.g., Hong Kong, Macao, Puerto Rico) are listed separately. Analysts should treat these as distinct reporting units.
  • No imputation: Missing years are omitted, not interpolated. Trend estimates for individual countries with sparse data should be treated as indicative rather than definitive.

Policy Recommendations

Based on the evidence presented, the following recommendations are offered for policy analysts and public safety stakeholders:

  1. Target youth and gender-specific interventions. Given the concentration of risk among males aged 15–29, gender-transformative programming, mentorship, and economic inclusion initiatives should be prioritized in high-rate settings.
  2. Invest in Caribbean and Latin American capacity. The clustering of extreme rates and rapid deteriorations in this region demands coordinated international assistance for law enforcement, judicial reform, and violence-prevention ecosystems.
  3. Learn from rapid decliners. Case studies of El Salvador, Singapore, Hong Kong, and Italy should be systematically evaluated to identify transferable policies—whether community policing, firearm regulation, or social investment—that contributed to their success.
  4. Strengthen data infrastructure. The decline in global reporting coverage is itself a risk factor for effective policy. Donors and international organizations should support national statistical and criminal-justice data systems, particularly in fragile states.
  5. Disaggregate further. Future data collection should prioritize relationship-to-perpetrator and mechanism (firearm, sharp object, etc.) variables to enable more targeted prevention strategies, particularly for female victimization.
Bottom line: The world is not uniformly becoming safer. While many nations have achieved remarkable reductions, others—especially in the Caribbean and parts of Latin America—are experiencing severe deteriorations. Gender and age disparities remain entrenched. Closing these gaps requires targeted, evidence-based interventions and a renewed commitment to transparent, timely data.
