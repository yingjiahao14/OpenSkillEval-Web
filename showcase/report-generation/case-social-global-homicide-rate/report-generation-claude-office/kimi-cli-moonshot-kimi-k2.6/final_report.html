Global Intentional Homicide Trends

A Multi-Dimensional Analysis (1990–2023)
United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) Data

Report Date: April 2026  |  Audience: Policy Analysts & Public Safety Stakeholders

Executive Summary

Is the world becoming safer from intentional homicide? The evidence suggests a modest long-term improvement at the global level, but with profound regional and demographic disparities. Over three decades, the global mean homicide rate has declined by approximately 6.7% when comparing the 2020s to the 1990s. However, this headline figure masks a critical divide: the Americas continue to experience homicide rates roughly 20× higher than Europe, and males are killed at nearly 7× the rate of females. Meanwhile, reporting coverage has fallen to just 41.8% of countries in 2023, underscoring the urgent need to strengthen global data infrastructure.

1. Global Homicide Rate Overview

Between 1990 and 2023, the global trajectory of intentional homicide reveals a pattern of relative stability punctuated by regional volatility. The global mean homicide rate—averaged across all reporting countries—stood at 8.27 per 100,000 in the 1990s, rose marginally in the 2000s, then declined by 9.6% during the 2010s. The 2020s have seen a slight rebound, driven in part by deteriorating conditions in parts of the Americas.

The median rate offers a more robust picture, as it is less sensitive to outlier nations with extreme violence. In 2023, the median stood at 1.84 per 100,000, down from roughly 2.9 in the mid-2010s, suggesting that the "typical" country has indeed become safer.

Figure 1. Global mean and median homicide rates with reporting coverage. Shaded area reflects mean. Dashed line shows the share of countries reporting data each year.
Key insight: While the mean rate has been volatile, the median has trended downward since the mid-1990s—suggesting that improvements are widespread rather than driven by a few outlier countries.

2. Regional & Country-Level Comparison

Geographic disparity is the most striking feature of the global homicide landscape. In 2023, the Americas recorded a mean homicide rate of 21.5 per 100,000—more than twenty times the European mean of 1.05. Africa and Asia fell in the middle, though African data remain sparse (only 4 countries reported in 2023).

Figure 2. Mean homicide rate by region in 2023. Number of reporting countries shown in parentheses within data labels.

Over time, the Americas have consistently maintained the highest regional rate, with a noticeable uptick since 2019. Europe has seen a long-term secular decline, while Asia and Oceania have remained relatively stable at low levels.

3. Gender Disparity in Homicide Victimization

One of the most consistent findings across the entire dataset is the profound gender imbalance in homicide victimization. Globally, males are killed at a rate nearly 7 times that of females—a disparity that has persisted, and in some years widened, over the past three decades.

Figure 4. Global mean homicide rates for males and females (age_group="ALL"). The dashed line shows the male-to-female ratio, which averaged 5.8:1 across all years.

In 2023, the male-to-female ratio reached 6.8:1, up from roughly 5:1 in the early 2010s. This reflects both a modest decline in female victimization rates and a relative increase in male victimization in certain high-violence contexts. The ratio is not uniform across countries: in some Latin American nations, male rates exceed 50 per 100,000 while female rates remain below 5, producing ratios well above 10:1.

Policy implication: Crime-prevention strategies must be gender-sensitive. The overwhelming burden of homicide falls on young men, particularly in the Americas, suggesting that targeted interventions—focused on urban violence, gang activity, and firearm access—are critical.

4. Age Group Analysis

Homicide risk is not evenly distributed across the life course. Global averages reveal a clear pattern: risk rises sharply during adolescence, peaks in young adulthood (ages 20–29), and declines steadily thereafter. Children under 14 face the lowest risk, while adults in their late twenties experience the highest.

Figure 5. Global mean homicide rate by age group, averaged across all sex-disaggregated observations.

When disaggregated by sex, the age pattern becomes even more pronounced. Males aged 25–29 face a global mean rate of 29.4 per 100,000—more than 9 times the female rate in the same bracket (3.2). For males, the peak-risk window spans ages 18–44; for females, rates remain relatively flat across adulthood, never exceeding 4 per 100,000 in any age band.

Figure 6. Mean homicide rates by age group and sex. Note the logarithmic scale implied by the large male-female gap in peak ages.
Key insight: Youth homicide is overwhelmingly a male phenomenon. The concentration of risk among males aged 15–29 points to the importance of early intervention programs, education, and employment initiatives in high-violence settings.

5. Notable Country-Level Trends & Outliers

Beyond the global and regional averages, individual country trajectories reveal dramatic stories of both progress and deterioration. To identify meaningful trends, we compared the average rate in each country’s first three available years to the average of its last three available years, restricting the analysis to countries with at least eight observations and excluding very small territories where single incidents can produce volatile rates.

Largest Absolute Increases

Figure 7. Countries with the largest absolute increases in homicide rate (early-period average vs. recent-period average).

Jamaica stands out with an increase of more than 27 points, driven by sustained gang violence and narcotics trafficking. Honduras, Ecuador, and Trinidad and Tobago have also seen substantial deteriorations, reflecting complex interactions of organized crime, institutional weakness, and socioeconomic stress.

Largest Absolute Decreases

Figure 8. Countries with the largest absolute decreases in homicide rate. These represent some of the most significant public-safety improvements globally.

El Salvador recorded the most dramatic improvement, falling from a peak mean above 129 to roughly 15—a testament to aggressive security reforms, though the sustainability and human-rights dimensions of these policies remain debated. Colombia also saw a remarkable decline of over 56 points, reflecting the peace process and reduced paramilitary violence. In Europe, Estonia, Latvia, and Croatia transitioned from post-Soviet-era violence to among the safer countries on the continent.

Caution: Percentage changes can be misleading when baseline rates are near zero (e.g., Iceland’s 674% increase reflects a rise from 0.12 to 0.96—still extremely low). Absolute changes and contextual interpretation are essential.

6. Data Limitations & Policy Recommendations

Data Limitations

This analysis is bounded by the structure and coverage of the underlying UNODC dataset. Analysts and policymakers should bear the following caveats in mind:

  • Unbalanced panel: Not all countries report data for every year. Coverage peaked at roughly 68% of countries in 2019 but fell to just 41.8% by 2023. This decline may reflect reporting delays rather than a true drop in monitoring capacity, but it nonetheless limits the reliability of recent global aggregates.
  • Changing composition: Because the set of reporting countries shifts year to year, global means and medians are not strictly comparable across time. A spike in the global mean could reflect new data from high-violence countries rather than a true worldwide increase.
  • United Kingdom caveat: UK data refer to England and Wales only. Scotland and Northern Ireland are reported separately in the original UNODC source and may appear as distinct entries.
  • Territories: Some territories (e.g., Hong Kong, Macao, Puerto Rico) are included separately. These should not be double-counted with their parent states.
  • No imputation: Missing years were not interpolated. All averages and trends are computed only from observed data.
Transparency note: The male-to-female ratio and age-group analyses are further constrained by the fact that many countries do not report sex- or age-disaggregated data consistently. The ratios presented here reflect only the subset of countries and years where both male and female (or age-specific) rates are available.

Policy Recommendations

Based on the evidence presented in this report, the following priorities emerge for policymakers and public safety stakeholders:

  1. Invest in data infrastructure. The declining share of countries reporting homicide data—particularly after 2020—threatens evidence-based policymaking. International bodies should prioritize technical assistance and standardized reporting frameworks, especially for low-income and conflict-affected states.
  2. Target youth violence in the Americas. The concentration of homicide among young men in Latin America and the Caribbean demands focused investment in violence-prevention programs, community policing, and alternatives to gang recruitment.
  3. Address gender-specific risk. While males bear the overwhelming burden of homicide, female victimization often occurs in distinct contexts (intimate-partner violence, femicide). Disaggregated data collection and gender-sensitive prosecution frameworks remain essential.
  4. Learn from success stories. Countries like El Salvador and Colombia demonstrate that dramatic reductions are possible. Rigorous evaluation of the policies driving these declines—while safeguarding human rights—can yield transferable lessons.
  5. Account for small-state volatility. Small territories can experience extreme rate fluctuations due to low population denominators. Regional aggregates and multi-year averages should supplement single-year snapshots in policy planning.

Methodology: All statistics derived directly from UNODC data as provided in data.csv (22,092 rows, 1990–2023). No external sources were consulted. Global aggregates are unweighted means across reporting countries. Regional classifications are based on ISO code mapping. For full reproducibility, analysts should filter on sex="both" and age_group="ALL" for country-level comparisons, and on sex="male"/"female" for gender-disparity analysis.
