Global Homicide Rate Overview
Overall global trend in homicide rates from 1990 to 2023, including reporting coverage and key trajectory findings.

The global median intentional homicide rate has followed a non-linear trajectory over the 33-year observation window. From a peak in the early 1990s — driven partly by post-Cold War instability and expanding reporting — rates declined through the 2000s and 2010s before partially stabilising in the 2020s. In 2023, the global median rate stood at 1.84 per 100,000, while the mean of 8.29 reflects a right-skewed distribution dominated by high-rate outliers.

Headline finding: The global mean homicide rate declined approximately 9.6% from the 2000s to the 2010s, suggesting measurable progress in public safety — though the 2020s show signs of stagnation and the disparity across regions remains stark.

The number of reporting countries has grown substantially from roughly 40–60 in the early 1990s to over 120 by the 2010s, before declining to 84 in 2023 — reflecting both genuine progress in data collection and reporting cycles. This expansion must be held in mind when interpreting trend data: part of the apparent change in global rates reflects changes in the composition of reporting countries rather than true underlying shifts.

Regional & Country-Level Comparison
Highest and lowest homicide rate countries, geographic patterns, and regional disparities.

Regional disparities in homicide rates are among the most persistent patterns in the data. The Americas consistently record the highest median rates — typically 10–20× those of Europe and Asia — driven by concentrated violence in the Caribbean, Central America, and parts of South America. In contrast, Europe and Asia maintain the lowest medians, typically under 2.0 per 100,000.

Americas outlier: The regional median for the Americas in 2023 was approximately 16.2 per 100,000 — over 18 times higher than Europe's median of 0.89. This single region accounts for a disproportionate share of global homicide burden and warrants targeted policy attention.

Gender Disparity in Homicide Victimization
Male-to-female homicide rate ratio globally and its evolution over time.

Homicide victimization is profoundly gendered. Across all years and geographies, male victims outnumber female victims by a ratio of approximately 3–4:1. In the most recent year with disaggregated data (2023), the global median male-to-female ratio stood at 3.17. This disparity is driven by multiple overlapping factors: higher male involvement in organized crime, interpersonal conflict, and gang violence.

Gender disparity KPI: The male-to-female ratio of 3.17 means that for every female homicide victim, approximately 3.2 males are killed. This ratio has remained relatively stable over the observation period, suggesting structural rather than cyclical drivers.

While the absolute rates for both sexes have generally trended downward in line with the global pattern, the ratio has remained persistent — suggesting that the underlying social and structural factors driving male victimization have not been fundamentally disrupted by crime reduction policies. Female homicide victimization, though lower in overall volume, may include a higher proportion of intimate-partner violence, which has distinct policy implications.

Age Group Analysis
Which age groups face the highest homicide risk, and whether youth or elderly populations are disproportionately affected.

The UNODC dataset provides age-disaggregated rates for a subset of countries and years (2003–2023), with data available primarily in male and female breakdowns rather than combined totals. Analysis of male rates — which are more complete — reveals a clear age-risk gradient:

Youth and young-adult peak: The 25–29 and 30–44 age groups consistently show the highest homicide rates across reporting countries, with rates approximately 5–12× higher than children (0–9) and markedly higher than those aged 60+. Young men in particular face acute risk.

Data Limitations & Policy Recommendations
Caveats on data quality and actionable recommendations for policy analysts.

Data Limitations

Unbalanced Panel

Countries differ substantially in how many years they report. A simple average of all-country rates in any year reflects the composition of reporting countries that year, not a stable global benchmark. Trend interpretation requires awareness of which countries enter and exit the reporting pool.

No Interpolation

Missing years are absent from the dataset rather than imputed. This means consecutive-year analysis can mask structural breaks. Countries with sparse data (fewer than 5 years) should be treated cautiously in trend comparisons.

UK Coverage Note

United Kingdom data in this dataset refer to England and Wales only. Scotland and Northern Ireland may appear as separate entries. Analysts should not double-count UK totals or use GBR figures as representative of the full United Kingdom.

Small-Population Territories

Micro-territories (e.g., Turks and Caicos Islands, Montserrat, Saint Helena) can show extreme per-capita rates from a single event. These should be contextualised with absolute counts, not treated as comparable to sovereign state rates.

Age Group Coverage

Age-disaggregated data is available only for a subset of countries and years (primarily 2003 onward), and only in male/female disaggregation — not combined totals. The age analysis in Section 4 should be interpreted as a directional indicator, not a comprehensive global picture.

Reporting Incentives

UNODC data depend on national reporting, which may understate homicides in conflict zones, failed states, or regions with limited judicial infrastructure. High-burden countries are disproportionately likely to have missing data, potentially understating the true global mean.

Policy Recommendations

1. Prioritise the Americas

Regional median rates 18× higher than Europe demand proportionate international cooperation, judicial reform, and violence prevention investment — particularly in the Caribbean and Andean subregions showing worsening trends.

2. Target Young Male Victimization

The 18–35 male cohort drives the overwhelming majority of homicide victimization. Youth-focused programmes — employment, diversion, community policing, and social services — should be the primary channel for rate reduction.

3. Strengthen Reporting Infrastructure

Only 42% of tracked countries reported in 2023. Expanding UNODC CTS participation — particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa and conflict-affected Asia — is essential for evidence-based global monitoring.

4. Investigate Stagnation in the 2020s

The near-zero change (+0.2%) in mean homicide rates from the 2010s to 2020s warrants investigation. COVID-era social disruption, reduced policing, and economic stress may have reversed previous gains in vulnerable countries.

5. Study El Salvador's Trajectory

The documented 94% decline in El Salvador's homicide rate — among the steepest in the dataset — offers a case study for policy learning, even accounting for the methodological and rights-related debates around enforcement approaches.

6. Gender-Sensitive Approaches

The persistent 3–4:1 male-to-female homicide ratio suggests that gender-neutral policing strategies are insufficient. Male-targeted violence prevention must be integrated alongside domestic/intimate partner violence programmes to address both dimensions.
