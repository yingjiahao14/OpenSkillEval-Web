1. Global Homicide Rate Overview

The global median intentional homicide rate has followed a notable long-term downward trajectory since the 1990s, though the path has been uneven. In the early 1990s, the global median hovered around 3.4 per 100,000 (with only 94 countries reporting in 1990). By 2023, the global median had fallen to 1.84 per 100,000 across 84 reporting countries.

The mean rate (dashed red line) runs consistently above the median, reflecting a right-skewed distribution where a small number of high-violence countries pull the average upward. The interquartile range (shaded band) has narrowed modestly since the late 2000s, suggesting some convergence among countries.

Key Finding: The world has become safer from intentional homicide over the past three decades, with the global median rate declining substantially. However, this aggregate picture masks significant regional and demographic disparities explored in the sections below.

3. Gender Disparity in Homicide Victimization

One of the most striking findings in this dataset is the persistent and substantial gender gap in homicide victimization. Globally, the median male homicide rate is approximately 3.2 times the median female rate — a ratio that has remained relatively stable over the past three decades despite overall declines in absolute rates.

The top panel shows that both male and female homicide rates have declined over time in absolute terms. However, the male rate has consistently been several times higher than the female rate. The bottom panel reveals that the male-to-female ratio has fluctuated within a band of roughly 2.4× to 3.5×, with no clear long-term convergence.

Policy Implication: The persistent gender gap — with men facing 3× the homicide risk of women at the global median — suggests that violence prevention strategies should account for gender-specific risk factors. Young men in particular face disproportionate risk, as the age-group analysis in Section 4 confirms.

6. Data Limitations & Policy Recommendations

Data Limitations

This analysis draws on UNODC Crime Trends Survey data, which has several important limitations that should inform the interpretation of findings:

  1. Unbalanced Panel Structure. The dataset is not a complete panel — countries enter and exit reporting across years. In 1990, only 94 countries reported, compared to 84 in 2023. This means early-period aggregates reflect a smaller and potentially different sample of countries than later periods, and trend comparisons should be interpreted with this compositional shift in mind.
  2. No Imputation. Missing years were not interpolated. Gaps in national time series mean that year-to-year comparisons for a given country may reflect reporting artifacts rather than genuine changes in homicide rates.
  3. United Kingdom Data. UK figures refer to England and Wales only, excluding Scotland and Northern Ireland, which may be reported separately in the source data.
  4. Territory Reporting. Some sub-national territories (e.g., Hong Kong, Macao, Puerto Rico) are reported separately. Care should be taken not to double-count these with their parent countries in regional or global aggregations.
  5. Measurement Heterogeneity. National definitions, recording practices, and data quality vary across countries and over time within countries. Homicide statistics in countries with weak statistical systems or active conflict may be particularly unreliable.

Policy Recommendations

Based on the findings of this analysis, we offer the following evidence-informed recommendations for policy analysts and public safety stakeholders:

  1. Target Young Male Populations. The analysis confirms that young men face the highest homicide victimization risk by a wide margin. Violence prevention and intervention programs should be designed with this demographic as a primary focus, including street-outreach programs, youth employment initiatives, and conflict-resolution training.
  2. Prioritize High-Burden Regions. With regional median rates varying by an order of magnitude, international cooperation and resource allocation should be directed toward the most affected regions. Capacity-building for national statistical systems in these areas would also improve the evidence base.
  3. Improve Data Infrastructure. Reporting coverage has improved but remains incomplete. Strengthening national vital registration and criminal justice data systems — particularly in low-income and conflict-affected countries — would enhance the global community's ability to monitor and respond to homicide trends.
  4. Disaggregate to Diagnose. The gender and age disparities documented here demonstrate the value of disaggregated data. Policymakers should demand and fund disaggregated reporting (by sex, age, geography, and mechanism) to enable targeted interventions rather than one-size-fits-all approaches.
  5. Monitor Reversal Risks. While the global trend is downward, several countries have experienced significant increases. Early-warning monitoring systems that track homicide rates at sub-national levels could help identify emerging crises before they escalate.
