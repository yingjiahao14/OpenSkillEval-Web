Global Homicide Rate Overview

Over the three decades from 1990 to 2023, the global median intentional homicide rate has shown a modest but meaningful decline — from 3.35 per 100,000 in 1990 to 1.84 per 100,000 in 2023 among reporting countries. The mean rate has been more volatile, reflecting the outsized influence of high-rate countries, but also trends downward in the most recent decade.

The world is, on balance, becoming somewhat safer from intentional homicide — but progress is uneven, geographically concentrated, and partially obscured by declining reporting coverage in recent years. The 2023 figures draw on only 84 countries, versus 162 in 2008, which introduces a systematic bias: countries that report more consistently tend to be higher-income nations with lower rates.

02

Regional & Country-Level Comparison

Geographic disparities in homicide rates are stark. Latin America & the Caribbean stands apart from all other regions with a median rate of 14.1 per 100,000 — more than thirteen times the European & Central Asian median of 1.09. Sub-Saharan Africa ranks second at 5.4. By contrast, East Asia & Pacific and the North Africa & Middle East region report the lowest medians after Europe.

The top-rate countries are concentrated in the Caribbean and Central/South America. Small island states such as Turks and Caicos Islands (76.3), Saint Kitts and Nevis (64.2), and Jamaica (49.4) record rates comparable to active conflict zones. At the other extreme, Singapore, Qatar, and Japan report rates below 0.25 per 100,000.

Gender Disparity in Homicide Victimization

One of the most consistent findings in this dataset is the profound and persistent gender gap in homicide victimization. Males are murdered at roughly 3.3 times the rate of females (median M/F ratio across countries with both sexes reported). This ratio has remained remarkably stable over three decades, fluctuating between approximately 2.6× and 5.7× depending on the year and sample composition.

The aggregate male rate (median: ~3.6 per 100,000 in recent years) dwarfs the female rate (~1.0 per 100,000). In high-burden countries like Saint Kitts and Nevis, Venezuela, and the US Virgin Islands, male rates can be 15–27× higher than female rates. However, female victimization is not trivial — it likely includes a significant proportion of intimate partner and domestic violence, which may itself be under-reported in countries with weak institutional protections.

Policy implication: The consistently high M/F ratio (3–4×) indicates that male victimization — not gender-based violence against women — drives the global homicide burden. Effective prevention must address the specific drivers of male-on-male lethal violence: gang activity, organized crime, and socioeconomic deprivation. Simultaneously, femicide and intimate partner violence warrant dedicated tracking and intervention given likely under-reporting.
04

Age Group Analysis

Homicide risk rises sharply at adolescence and peaks in young adulthood. Among males, the highest median rates are found in the 30–44 (4.08), 25–29 (3.99), and 20–24 (3.44) age bands — the years overlapping most heavily with labor market entry, peer network formation, and potential gang or organized crime involvement. The rate declines substantially after age 45 and is lowest in the 10–14 cohort.

Among females, the pattern is broadly similar but compressed: rates are low across all age groups, peaking modestly in the 30–44 band (1.39). Notably, the 0–9 age band shows elevated relative risk for young children — likely reflecting infanticide and intra-family violence — both for males (0.48) and females (0.37).

Youth (15–29) are disproportionately victimized across virtually all high-burden countries, underscoring the urgency of prevention programs targeting this cohort.

05

Notable Country-Level Trends & Outliers

Country-level trajectories reveal both remarkable successes and alarming deteriorations. El Salvador stands out as the single most dramatic improvement: a rate of 134.3 in 1994 fell to 7.9 by 2022 — a 94% decline, driven by sustained law enforcement and gang-suppression efforts. Colombia similarly reduced its rate from 75.0 (1990) to 24.9 (2023), a two-thirds reduction over three decades. Sri Lanka (−73%) and Philippines (−71%) also demonstrate substantial long-term progress.

Conversely, several Caribbean and Central American nations have seen sharp escalations. Haiti's rate surged 694% from 5.2 (2007) to 41.2 (2023), coinciding with state collapse and gang proliferation. Saint Kitts and Nevis rose 579% and Ecuador 437%, reflecting the spread of organized crime networks. Costa Rica's 299% increase is notable for an otherwise stable democracy, pointing to drug-route dynamics.

Data Limitations & Policy Recommendations

⚠ Data Limitations

  • Unbalanced panel: Not all countries report data for every year. The sample of reporting countries shifts substantially across time — the 1990s data come from ~94–107 countries; peak coverage reached ~162 countries around 2008; by 2023 only 84 countries reported. All cross-year comparisons must account for this compositional change.
  • Declining recent coverage: The apparent stabilization or slight uptick in mean rates after 2020 may partially reflect a smaller, more heterogeneous reporting pool, not genuine worsening. Interpret post-2020 trends cautiously.
  • United Kingdom caveat: UK data in the UNODC source refer to England and Wales only; Scotland and Northern Ireland may appear as separate entries. Double-counting risk exists.
  • Territories vs. sovereign states: Small territories (Puerto Rico, Hong Kong, Macao, Guam, US Virgin Islands) are reported separately and should not be aggregated with parent-country figures.
  • No interpolation: Missing years were left absent — rows do not exist for years with no data. Analysts computing trends should account for gaps rather than treating adjacent observations as continuous.
  • Definitional variation: "Intentional homicide" definitions can vary slightly between countries and reporting periods, affecting strict comparability.
  • Age group sub-categories (non-ALL): Only male and female sex categories are reported for specific age groups; the "both" aggregate is only available for the ALL group in many countries. Combine male/female medians for age-group analysis.

1. Prioritize Caribbean and Central American Crime Surge

The Caribbean sub-region shows the highest rates globally and significant recent deterioration in several island states. International cooperation — including targeted anti-gang and anti-trafficking programs, judicial capacity building, and regional policing intelligence-sharing — should be treated as an urgent priority. Countries such as Haiti, Ecuador, Saint Kitts and Nevis, and Trinidad and Tobago warrant specific monitoring and support frameworks.

2. Invest in Youth Violence Prevention

Young males aged 15–44 are disproportionately victimized across all regions. Evidence-based interventions — gang deterrence programs, vocational training, cognitive behavioral therapy, urban redesign — have documented effectiveness in reducing youth homicide. High-burden countries should integrate age-targeted strategies into national crime reduction plans.

3. Strengthen Gender-Disaggregated Reporting

While the M/F ratio is consistently high, not all countries report sex-disaggregated data (Afghanistan, for instance, provides only the "both" category for many years). Universal adoption of sex-disaggregated reporting would enable more precise tracking of femicide and intimate partner violence, which may be systematically under-recorded in aggregate figures.

4. Scale Up Data Coverage for Monitoring

Reporting coverage below 50% in 2023 severely limits the ability to assess recent global trends. UNODC and international partners should incentivize and support statistical capacity in low-income countries to ensure that global monitoring is not inadvertently biased toward wealthier, safer nations.

5. Learn from Success Stories

El Salvador (−94%), Colombia (−67%), Sri Lanka (−73%), and the Philippines (−71%) demonstrate that dramatic, sustained reductions are achievable. Cross-national policy learning — understanding what institutional, economic, and social mechanisms drove these improvements — should be a research and cooperation priority for the international community.
