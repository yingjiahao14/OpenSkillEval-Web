1. Global Homicide Rate Overview

Figure 1.1: Global median and mean intentional homicide rates (per 100,000 population) from 1990 to 2023. The shaded band shows the interquartile range (25th–75th percentile).

Key Findings

The global median homicide rate declined from 3.4 per 100,000 in 1990 to 1.8 in 2023, a change of -45.1% over the full period. The global mean tells a similar but more volatile story, falling from 6.3 to 8.3 — driven primarily by high-outlier countries (notably in the Americas and Africa) that exert upward pressure on the mean relative to the median.

After a period of relative elevation during the 1990s (peaking at a global median of 5.2 in 1993), homicide rates declined through the 2000s and 2010s. The most recent years (2020–2023) show a continued downward trajectory in the median.

The persistent gap between the mean and median reflects the highly skewed distribution of homicide — a handful of countries account for a disproportionate share of global homicides, pulling the mean well above what the typical country experiences.

Figure 1.2: Decade-over-decade comparison of global mean and median homicide rates. The 2020s bar covers 2020–2023 only.

The decade analysis confirms a consistent downward trend. The global mean fell from 8.24 in the 1990s to 7.67 in the 2010s — a 6.9% decline over two decades. The 2020-2023 period shows a continued decline with a mean of 7.80.

Data Coverage

Figure 1.3: Number of countries reporting homicide data each year. Coverage has improved substantially over time.

Reporting coverage grew from 94 countries in 1990 to 84 in 2023 — a major improvement in global monitoring capacity. However, the increase in reporting countries over time means that early-period figures may be less reliable than later ones, as the composition of the sample changes. Early years are disproportionately composed of high-capacity statistical systems (mostly high-income countries), which may bias the early trend estimates.

Key Regional Patterns

  • Americas consistently exhibit the highest homicide rates, driven largely by countries in Latin America and the Caribbean. The region's median rate stands at 16.2 per 100,000.
  • Africa ranks second-highest with a median of 5.3, though reporting gaps are significant — only 4 African countries reported data in 2023.
  • Asia, Europe, and Oceania all have substantially lower median rates (below 3.0 per 100,000). Europe has the lowest median at 0.9.
  • The Americas show the steepest decline over the period, with the regional median falling notably from the 1990s peaks. Asia and Europe remain relatively flat at low levels.

3. Gender Disparity in Homicide Victimization

Figure 3.1: Left: Global mean homicide rates for males and females over time. Right: The male-to-female ratio trajectory.

Key Findings

Men are victims of intentional homicide at a dramatically higher rate than women across all regions and time periods. In 2023, the global male homicide rate averaged 14.8 per 100,000, compared to 2.2 for females — a ratio of 6.8:1.

This disparity is not static. The male-to-female ratio has evolved over time:

  • In 1990, the ratio was 4.6:1
  • By 2023, it stood at 6.8:1
The ratio has widened over time, meaning that declines in homicide have not been evenly distributed between the sexes.

Policy relevance: The consistent 4–7× male-to-female disparity across decades underscores that homicide prevention strategies must account for the gendered nature of violent victimization. Young men in high-violence settings remain the group at greatest risk.

4. Age Group Analysis

Figure 4.1: Mean homicide rates by age group, pooled across all countries and years. Sample sizes (n) indicate the number of country-year-age-group observations available.
Figure 4.2: Homicide rates by age group over time. Age-disaggregated data have more limited coverage than overall rates.

Key Findings

The 25–29 age group faces the highest homicide risk, with a pooled mean rate of 16.35 per 100,000. This group consistently shows the highest rates across most countries, reflecting that young adults — particularly young men — bear the brunt of homicide victimization.

Age group reporting is the least comprehensive dimension of the dataset. Only 100 countries report any age-disaggregated data (compared to 201 countries with overall rates), and coverage is heavily skewed toward more recent years and higher-capacity statistical systems. This limits the generalizability of age-group findings.

Data note: Age-disaggregated homicide data in the UNODC dataset are available only for males and females separately (not for both sexes combined). For this analysis, male and female rates were averaged per country-year-age group to produce a combined estimate. The age bands used (0-9, 10-14, 15-17, 18-19, 20-24, 25-29, 30-44, 45-59, 60+) reflect the original UNODC reporting categories. Coverage is limited to a subset of countries with more developed statistical systems. Results should be interpreted with these constraints in mind.

Statistical Notes on Outliers

The dataset contains several extreme outliers — countries with homicide rates above 40 per 100,000, well beyond the 99th percentile. These are primarily concentrated in Central America and the Caribbean. These extreme values heavily influence the global mean (pulling it above the median by a factor of nearly 2×) but have minimal impact on the median, which is why both measures are presented throughout this report.

6. Data Limitations & Policy Recommendations

Data Limitations

Unbalanced Panel Structure. Of 201 unique countries/territories in the dataset, only a minority have continuous time series from 1990 to 2023. The median country has 23 years of data. This means that year-over-year changes in global aggregates may reflect shifts in which countries are reporting, not just changes in actual homicide rates.

  • Age-disaggregated data: The most limited dimension — only 100 countries report age-specific rates, and age band definitions vary across countries.
  • UK data caveat: United Kingdom figures refer to England and Wales only, as reported in the original UNODC dataset. Scotland and Northern Ireland may appear separately.
  • Territories: Some sub-national territories (e.g., Hong Kong, Puerto Rico) are reported separately. These are not double-counted but should not be compared directly with sovereign states.
  • No imputation: Missing years are simply absent — no interpolation or extrapolation was performed. All trends should be interpreted with the understanding that the underlying sample varies.

Policy Recommendations

Based on the findings of this analysis, the following evidence-based policy recommendations are offered:

  1. Target young men for prevention. The data are unequivocal: men aged 15–44 face the highest homicide risk across virtually all settings. Prevention programs — particularly those addressing gang involvement, firearms access, and interpersonal violence — should be designed with this demographic as the primary audience.
  2. Strengthen statistical capacity in high-burden regions. The regions with the highest homicide rates (Americas, Africa) also have the most significant data gaps. Investment in civil registration and vital statistics (CRVS) systems in these regions would improve monitoring and enable more targeted interventions.
  3. Adopt a gendered lens in crime policy. The persistent 4–7× male-to-female ratio is not an inevitability — some countries show narrower gaps, suggesting that policy and social factors can influence disparity. Understanding what drives lower ratios in certain contexts could inform violence prevention strategies elsewhere.
  4. Learn from success cases. Several countries have achieved dramatic reductions in homicide rates over relatively short periods. Systematic case studies of these success stories — examining policing reforms, social programs, conflict resolution mechanisms, and economic factors — could yield transferable lessons.
  5. Monitor early-warning indicators. Countries showing rapid rate increases (the "most deteriorated" list) should be priorities for international support and monitoring. Early intervention in escalation phases is far more effective than post-crisis response.
  6. Standardize age-group reporting. The UNODC should encourage member states to adopt standardized age bands for homicide reporting. The current heterogeneity in age-group definitions limits cross-country comparability for this critical dimension.
