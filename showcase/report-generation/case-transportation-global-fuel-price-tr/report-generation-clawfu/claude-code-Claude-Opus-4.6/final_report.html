1970s: The 1970s began with oil at just $1.21/bbl and ended above $39. The OPEC embargo of October 1973 triggered the first major price shock, with prices jumping from $2.70 to $13.00 in a single quarter — a ~380% increase. The Iranian Revolution in 1979 drove a second shock, pushing prices past $40 for the first time.
1980s: Prices peaked at ~$40 in early 1980 before a long decline driven by demand destruction, non-OPEC supply growth, and Saudi Arabia's decision to defend market share. The 1986 crash saw prices fall from ~$27 to under $10 in mid-1986 — a collapse of over 60%% in six months.
1990s: The 1990s were the most stable decade, with prices oscillating between $10 and $35. Iraq's invasion of Kuwait (August 1990) caused a short-lived spike. The Asian Financial Crisis in 1997–98 pushed prices to decade lows near $10/bbl, before a recovery in 1999.
2000s: The 2000s saw the most dramatic price trajectory in the dataset. Driven by surging Chinese demand and constrained supply, prices rose from ~$25 in 2000 to an all-time peak of $132.83 in July 2008. The subsequent crash during the Global Financial Crisis brought prices down to ~$41 by December 2008.
2010s: The 2010s were defined by the U.S. shale revolution. Prices remained above $90 through mid-2014 before OPEC's decision not to cut production triggered a crash to under $30 by early 2016. Prices partially recovered but never sustained above $80 for long.
2020s: The 2020s began with the COVID-19 demand shock that sent prices to $21.04 in April 2020. The recovery was swift, amplified by Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 which pushed prices back above $110. By early 2026, prices have settled in the $60–88 range.

Outlook & Conclusions

Key takeaways from 56 years of crude oil price data and observations on the current trajectory.

1. Structural regime changes define oil markets. The dataset reveals at least five distinct pricing regimes: pre-embargo ($1–4), OPEC-era ($10–40), post-Cold War ($10–35), commodity supercycle ($25–133), and the post-shale/energy transition era ($20–117). Each transition was driven by fundamental shifts in supply structure, demand composition, or geopolitical alignment.
2. Volatility is the constant. Despite long periods of apparent stability (e.g., 1990s), the oil market has consistently generated extreme moves. The standard deviation of $30.61 represents 77% of the mean price — a coefficient of variation that underscores the risk inherent in energy market exposure.
3. Recent data suggests a stabilizing, lower-price environment. 2024–2026 prices in the $58–88 range represent a significant moderation from the 2022 peaks. This is consistent with expanded U.S. production, OPEC+ management challenges, and the early stages of energy demand diversification.
4. The CAGR of 7.93% masks enormous path dependency. While the long-run return appears moderate, the journey from $1.21 to $87.84 included drawdowns exceeding 70%, rallies exceeding 300%, and multi-year periods of both sustained gains and sustained losses.
