Executive Overview

The dataset traces crude oil from a low-price 1970 starting point of $1.21 to $87.84 in Mar 2026, a compound annual growth rate of 7.93% over 56.16 elapsed years. The nominal series is not a smooth trend: it is a sequence of regimes shaped by abrupt repricing events, long plateaus, collapses, and recoveries.

The all-time monthly high is $132.83 in Jul 2008; the dataset minimum is $1.21 in Jan 1970. This creates a full observed range of $131.62. Dispersion is high: the sample standard deviation is $30.61, with the median ($29.11) materially below the mean ($39.80), indicating a right-skewed distribution created by the high-price 2000s, 2010s, and early 2020s.

The biggest absolute month-over-month move is a −$26.96 decline in Oct 2008, from $99.66 to $72.69 (−27.06%). The largest percentage month-over-month increase is +217.07% in Jan 1974, a defining jump in the 1970s regime shift.

Long-Term Price Trajectory (1970–2026)

Full monthly series, annotated

Annotations identify historically recognized market events at monthly observations available in the dataset. Numeric labels are the exact monthly crude oil prices from the data.

The long-run chart shows three broad regimes. First, prices were extremely low and stable at the beginning of the 1970s before the 1973–74 repricing. Second, the series cycled between elevated but mean-reverting levels from the late 1970s through the 1990s, including the 1986 collapse and Gulf War spike. Third, after 2000 the amplitude of price cycles widened substantially: prices rose to the 2008 high, fell during the financial crisis, rebuilt through the early 2010s, declined during the 2014–16 adjustment, then experienced a pandemic trough and 2022 energy shock.

Outlook & Conclusions

Recent trend context, not a forecast

The 2020s segment is incomplete, ending in March 2026. Within the data available, the decade spans from a pandemic-era trough of $21.04 in Apr 2020 to a recent-decade peak of $116.80 in Jun 2022. The partial-decade mean is $71.75, with a standard deviation of $18.80; that keeps recent prices structurally higher than pre-2000 history but below the 2010s average.

Two conclusions matter for analysts. First, crude oil’s long-run nominal path is upward, but regime shifts and shocks dominate short- and medium-horizon interpretation. Second, the dataset shows that major repricing episodes are asymmetric: upside shocks can reset the price level for years, while downside shocks such as 2008 and 2020 can be exceptionally abrupt. The historical baseline therefore supports scenario analysis built around regimes and volatility clusters rather than linear extrapolation.
