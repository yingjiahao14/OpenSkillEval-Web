1Executive Overview

This report analyzes 675 monthly crude oil price observations from January 1970 through March 2026, spanning 56.2 years of global energy market history. Over this period, crude oil transformed from a low-cost, stable commodity priced near $1.21/bbl to a volatile, geopolitically sensitive asset that has traded as high as $132.83/bbl (July 2008).

The dataset reveals a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 7.93%, with the price expanding over 110x from start to finish. However, this growth has been anything but linear. The time series is characterized by violent shocks—the 1973 OPEC embargo, the 1979 Iranian Revolution, the 2008 speculative peak, the 2020 pandemic crash—separated by extended regimes of relative stability.

The 2010s recorded the highest average price at $76.45/bbl, driven by the unprecedented 2008 super-spike and the sustained $100+ environment of 2011–2014. Overall price volatility, as measured by the standard deviation of monthly prices, stands at $30.61—a figure that underscores the extraordinary dispersion of outcomes over the dataset's lifetime.

Key Finding The largest single-month percentage move was +217.1% in January 1974, while the steepest decline was -39.6% in March 2020. These extremes illustrate the asymmetric risk profile inherent in crude oil markets.

2Long-Term Price Trajectory (1970–2026)

The chart below presents the complete monthly crude oil price series from January 1970 to March 2026. Key geopolitical and economic events are annotated to contextualize the major inflection points. The trajectory broadly divides into five structural phases:

Phase 1 (1970–1973): A period of extreme stability, with prices hovering near $1–$3/bbl under the administered pricing of the Seven Sisters era.
Phase 2 (1973–1985): The OPEC-driven revolution, with two major price shocks lifting crude from $4 to over $39/bbl.
Phase 3 (1986–2003): A long regime of moderate, range-bound pricing ($10–$35/bbl), punctuated by the Gulf War spike and Asian crisis trough.
Phase 4 (2003–2014): The commodity super-cycle, peaking at $132.83/bbl in July 2008 and sustaining triple-digit prices through 2014.
Phase 5 (2014–present): The shale era and beyond, characterized by lower equilibrium prices, the COVID shock, the post-pandemic recovery, and the Ukraine war-driven spike.

1970s: The OPEC Revolution

The decade began with crude at $1.21/bbl and ended above $39/bbl. The 1973 Arab oil embargo quadrupled prices virtually overnight, and the 1979 Iranian Revolution doubled them again. The decade's standard deviation of $8.89 reflects this extraordinary transformation from a stable, low-cost commodity to a geopolitically weaponized resource.

1980s: Correction and Collapse

After peaking near $40/bbl in early 1980, prices entered a prolonged decline as demand destruction, new non-OPEC supply (North Sea, Alaska), and Saudi Arabia's decision to abandon its swing producer role combined to crash prices to below $10/bbl in mid-1986. The decade average of $25.55/bbl masks the dramatic collapse from the early-decade highs to the late-decade lows.

1990s: Range-Bound Stability

Apart from the brief Gulf War spike to $34.50 in October 1990, the 1990s were the most stable decade in the dataset with a mean of $18.19/bbl and the lowest standard deviation among full decades at $3.84. The Asian Financial Crisis drove prices to the dataset's post-1970s low of $10.41/bbl in December 1998.

2000s: The Super-Cycle

Surging demand from China and other emerging economies, combined with supply constraints, propelled the decade's average to $49.17/bbl. The decade encompasses both the all-time peak of $132.83/bbl (July 2008) and the subsequent crash to $41.34 during the Global Financial Crisis, producing the highest decade-level standard deviation of $25.64.

2010s: Shale, Oversupply, and Rebalancing

The 2010s carried the highest mean of any decade at $76.45/bbl, buoyed by the $100+ environment of 2011–2014. The US shale revolution then fundamentally altered the supply landscape, contributing to the 2014–2016 price collapse that saw crude fall below $30/bbl. Prices recovered into the $50–$75 range by decade's end.

2020s: Pandemic, Recovery, and Normalization

The decade opened with the COVID-19 demand shock crashing prices to $21.04/bbl in April 2020. The subsequent recovery was turbocharged by Russia's invasion of Ukraine, which drove prices to $116.80/bbl in June 2022. Since then, prices have progressively normalized, with the most recent observations (early 2026) in the $60–$88 range. The decade's average stands at $71.75/bbl across 75 observations to date.

6Outlook & Conclusions

Recent Trajectory (2023–2026)

After the Ukraine war-driven spike of mid-2022, crude oil prices have entered a normalizing trend. The most recent 12 months of data show prices fluctuating between approximately $58 and $88/bbl, with a general downward bias through 2025 before a partial recovery in early 2026. The March 2026 print of $87.84/bbl represents a sharp uptick from the preceding months, though it remains well below the 2022 highs.

Structural Takeaways

1. Mean reversion is powerful but slow. Every major price shock in the dataset was eventually unwound, but the timeline ranged from months (2020 COVID recovery) to over a decade (1980s correction). Mean reversion does not imply predictability.

2. Supply shocks create regimes, not spikes. The 1973 embargo, 1979 revolution, and post-2003 super-cycle each established new, sustained price levels rather than temporary dislocations.

3. The shale revolution permanently altered the supply elasticity. Post-2014, the US shale industry acts as a partial price ceiling, ramping production when prices rise and slowing when they fall. This has compressed the upper end of the price distribution relative to the 2008 peak.

4. Volatility clusters. High-volatility periods (visible in the rolling standard deviation chart) tend to persist for 12–24 months, not weeks. Risk management must account for regime-based volatility, not a single constant.

Data Limitations

Note on Forward-Looking Data The dataset extends through March 2026. Observations beyond the current date may represent forecasts or projections rather than realized prices. Additionally, all prices are in nominal US dollars; no inflation adjustment has been applied, meaning the real purchasing-power-adjusted growth is lower than the nominal CAGR reported here.
