Executive Overview

The monthly crude oil series rises from $1.21 in Jan 1970 to $87.84 in Mar 2026, a 7.93% compound annual increase across 56.2 years. The long-run profile is not a smooth growth path: it is a sequence of distinct regimes shaped by supply shocks, macro cycles, demand collapses, and post-shock recoveries.

  • The all-time dataset high is $132.83 in Jul 2008; the low is $1.21 in Jan 1970, producing a $131.62 full-period range.
  • The 2010s show the highest decade-average price at $76.45, narrowly above the partial 2020s average of $71.75 and materially above the 2000s average of $49.17.
  • The largest absolute one-month move is $26.96 in Oct 2008, when prices fell from $99.66 to $72.69.
  • The largest percentage one-month move is +217.1% in Jan 1974, a jump from $4.10 to $13.00.

Structural Reading of the Full Series

The pre-1974 segment is low and compressed, with prices at $1.21 throughout 1970 and still below $5.00 until late 1973. The first major regime break arrives in January 1974, when the monthly series rises to $13.00, the dataset’s largest MoM percentage increase.

The 1980s begin from an elevated base, but the decade contains a major downward reset: by February 1986, the price is $15.92, down -35.5% from the prior month. A comparatively subdued 1990s regime follows, with an average of $18.19 and the lowest decade standard deviation outside the pre-shock 1970s base effects.

The 2000s and 2010s represent the high-price era. The peak occurs in July 2008, followed by the largest 12-month dollar decline in July 2009 (-68.16 USD/bbl versus July 2008). The 2020s contain both the COVID-era trough of $21.04 and a recovery to $116.80 in June 2022.

Outlook & Conclusions

The latest observations show a partial 2020s regime that remains high by historical standards: $71.75 on average through March 2026, above every full decade except the 2010s. Within that partial decade, the minimum is $21.04 and the maximum is $116.80, a $95.76 range that confirms continued macro and supply-demand sensitivity.

For analysts, the central historical lesson is regime dependence. Low nominal prices before 1974 make percentage moves appear extreme; the 2000s–2020s high-price environment makes dollar moves more consequential for producers, consumers, and inflation pass-through. Current and future oil-market moves should therefore be benchmarked against both the full-series distribution and the relevant structural regime rather than against a single long-run average.
