1. Executive Overview

This report analyzes 675 monthly crude oil price observations from January 1970 to March 2026, spanning more than five decades of global energy market history. Prices are expressed in nominal US dollars per barrel (USD/bbl).

Over the full period, crude oil prices rose from $1.21 in January 1970 to $87.84 in March 2026, reflecting a compound annual growth rate of 7.93%. The trajectory, however, is far from linear — the dataset captures distinct pricing regimes shaped by geopolitical crises, supply-side transformations, and demand shocks.

Key Findings: Crude oil prices traversed a range of $131.62 (from $1.21 to $132.83). The 2010s recorded the highest average price of any decade. The single largest month-over-month price swing occurred during the 1973 oil embargo, and the most extreme percentage moves cluster around geopolitical disruptions and the COVID-19 demand collapse.

2. Long-Term Price Trajectory (1970–2026)

The full time series reveals a commodity whose price has been anything but stationary. The chart below shows the complete monthly price history with annotations at critical inflection points.

Three broad regimes are visible:

  1. Low-price era (1970–1973): Prices held near $1.88/bbl, reflecting the pre-OPEC dominance period when posted prices were administratively set and supply was abundant.
  2. Volatile upward era (1973–1985): The 1973 oil embargo and 1979 Iranian Revolution triggered two step-function price increases, pushing nominal prices above $30/bbl — a 20× increase from the start of the decade.
  3. Post-modern era (1986–2026): Prices have oscillated within a wide band ($9.62–$132.83), driven by a complex interplay of OPEC policy, financial speculation, shale supply, and macroeconomic cycles.

1970s — The Oil Crisis Decade

Average price: $9.70/bbl | Volatility (σ): $8.89

The 1970s transformed oil from a cheap, stable commodity into a geopolitical weapon. The OPEC oil embargo of October 1973 quadrupled prices overnight (from $2.70 to $4.10 in a single month, then to $13.00 by January 1974). The Iranian Revolution of 1979 pushed prices above $40/bbl by decade's end. The decade's price range of $39.54 was unprecedented at the time.

1980s — Glut and Collapse

Average price: $25.55/bbl | Volatility (σ): $8.35

The early 1980s saw prices peak above $39/bbl as the Iran-Iraq war disrupted supply. However, conservation efforts, new non-OPEC production (North Sea, Alaska), and Saudi Arabia's decision to increase output led to a supply glut. The 1986 price collapse saw crude fall from the mid-$20s to below $15/bbl in a matter of months. The rest of the decade saw prices range-bound in the mid-teens to low-$20s.

1990s — Low Volatility, Gradual Rise

Average price: $18.19/bbl | Volatility (σ): $3.84

The 1990s were the calmest decade in oil price history (standard deviation of just $3.84). The Gulf War caused a brief spike in mid-1990, but prices quickly normalized. The Asian financial crisis of 1997–1998 pushed prices to a low of $10.41/bbl, prompting OPEC to coordinate production cuts that set the stage for the subsequent bull market.

2000s — The Great Bull Market and Crash

Average price: $49.17/bbl | Volatility (σ): $25.64

Driven by surging Chinese demand, tight spare capacity, and financial inflows into commodity indices, prices staged a historic rally from the mid-$20s in 2002 to an all-time high of $132.83/bbl in July 2008. The 2008 financial crisis then triggered the largest absolute price collapse on record — prices fell below $40/bbl within five months, a decline of over 70%.

2010s — Shale Revolution and Price War

Average price: $76.45/bbl | Volatility (σ): $24.31

The 2010s opened with prices recovering above $100/bbl as global demand rebounded. The US shale revolution began to shift the supply landscape fundamentally. In late 2014, Saudi Arabia abandoned its role as swing producer, triggering a price war designed to squeeze high-cost shale producers. Prices collapsed from over $100 to the mid-$40s. The remainder of the decade saw a gradual recovery, with prices stabilizing in the $50–70 range.

2020s — Pandemic, War, and Transition

Average price: $71.75/bbl | Volatility (σ): $18.80

The COVID-19 pandemic triggered the most extreme demand shock in oil market history. In April 2020, prices briefly traded in negative territory for WTI futures (though this dataset reflects nominal spot/benchmark prices which show a low of $21.04/bbl). The subsequent recovery was remarkably swift. Russia's invasion of Ukraine in early 2022 then pushed prices back above $110/bbl. Since mid-2023, prices have moderated toward the $60–80 range as demand growth slows and supply diversifies.

4. Price Volatility & Major Shocks

The rolling 12-month standard deviation chart identifies three distinct volatility regimes:

  • 1973–1975: The oil embargo created the first major volatility spike as prices quadrupled.
  • 2008–2009: The financial crisis produced the highest absolute volatility on record, with prices swinging over $90/bbl in a 12-month window.
  • 2020–2022: The pandemic crash and subsequent Ukraine war spike represent the most recent high-volatility episode.

6. Outlook & Conclusions

Key Takeaways

  1. Oil prices are shock-driven. The five decades of data show that the largest and most consequential price moves are triggered by exogenous events — embargoes, wars, revolutions, financial crises, and pandemics. Structural forecasting is inherently limited by the unpredictability of these events.
  2. The era of stable, low prices ended in 1973 and has not returned. Pre-1973 prices averaged ~$1.88/bbl. Since then, the mean has been $42.71/bbl — a regime change of roughly an order of magnitude.
  3. Volatility is structural, not cyclical. The rolling standard deviation shows no tendency to permanently decline. Each new decade has produced its own volatility episodes, suggesting that oil market participants must structurally account for wide price swings.
  4. The 2020s reflect a new equilibrium in formation. Recent data (2023–2026) shows prices settling in the $60–85/bbl range with moderate volatility. This is consistent with a market balancing diversified supply (shale, renewables transition) against still-growing demand in emerging economies.
  5. Nominal prices overstate long-term appreciation. The CAGR of 7.93% over 56 years reflects nominal dollar terms. Adjusted for cumulative US CPI inflation over the same period, the real price appreciation is substantially lower — reminding analysts that much of the apparent price growth reflects monetary depreciation rather than commodity scarcity.

Recent Trends (2024–2026)

The most recent data shows crude oil prices trending downward from the mid-$70s in late 2024 toward the low $60s by early 2025, before a notable spike to $87.84 in March 2026. This recent uptick warrants monitoring, as it may reflect renewed supply constraints or geopolitical risk premia re-entering the market.
