Core finding: high prices are not evenly distributed across the sample. The median monthly price is $29.11, while the mean is $39.80, and the 90th percentile is $88.14. This right-skewed distribution reflects episodic periods of extreme price elevation, especially the 2000s and early 2010s.

Long-Term Price Trajectory (1970–2026)

The full series shows four broad regimes: low nominal prices before the 1973–1974 repricing, elevated but unstable prices through the 1980s, comparatively range-bound 1990s pricing, and a post-2000 regime with structurally higher levels and larger cyclical drawdowns.

Level shift: prices rose from $1.21 in Jan 1970 to $13.00 in Jan 1974; the Jan 1974 observation is 525.0% above Jan 1973, the largest YoY percentage increase in the dataset.
Peak and collapse: the all-time dataset high is $132.83 in Jul 2008. By Dec 2008, the price was $41.34, a fall of $91.49 from the peak.
Recent regime: the 2020s average through Mar 2026 is $71.75, below the 2010s average of $76.45 but above every pre-2010 decade.
Annual extremes: the highest annual averages are 2012 ($105.01), 2013 ($104.08), 2011 ($104.01); the lowest are 1970 ($1.21), 1971 ($1.69), 1972 ($1.82).

Decade-by-Decade Breakdown

Decade averages reveal the structural elevation of crude prices after 2000. The 2010s record the highest decade mean at $76.45, while the 2000s show the highest decade-level dispersion at $25.64 standard deviation.

Structural interpretation:
• The 1970s have the largest first-to-last percentage rise (3,185.1%), dominated by oil-shock repricing from a low base.
• The 1980s decline 50.4% from first to last observation, marking a major reversal from the prior decade.
• The 2000s nearly triple from first to last (195.9%) despite the 2008 collapse, demonstrating a powerful commodity supercycle before the drawdown.

Price Volatility & Major Shocks

The largest absolute monthly shock is a -26.96 USD/bbl move in Oct 2008, while the largest percentage monthly shock is 217.1% in Jan 1974. These two maxima differ because percentage moves are base-sensitive: the 1974 repricing starts from a very low nominal level, while 2008 volatility occurs at much higher prices.

Outlook & Conclusions

The data through Mar 2026 places the latest price, $87.84, above the long-run mean and median and above the 2025 annual average of $63.81. The final month also records one of the largest monthly increases in the dataset: +23.37 USD/bbl (36.2%) from Feb 2026.

Analyst takeaway: the historical context suggests that crude oil prices move through regimes rather than along a smooth trend. Major breaks are visible around the 1970s oil shocks, the mid-1980s collapse, the 2000s supercycle and 2008 crisis, the 2014–2015 downturn, the COVID-era shock, and the 2022 energy-price surge.

Current and future movements should therefore be benchmarked against both level and volatility history. A price in the high-$80s, such as Mar 2026, is elevated relative to the full-sample mean but not unprecedented relative to the 2008 peak, the 2010s high-price regime, or the 2022 shock. The 2020s-to-date average of $71.75 indicates that the recent decade remains structurally high versus pre-2000 history, despite the COVID trough at $21.04.

Data Notes

The report uses only /app/benchmark/data.csv, which contains two columns: Date and Crude_Oil_Price. Dates are parsed as monthly observations and prices are treated as nominal USD per barrel. The source brief notes that later observations approaching 2026 may include projected or estimated values; the report does not independently classify actual versus forecast months because that distinction is not encoded in the CSV.

All KPIs, charts, decade aggregations, and shock calculations are computed directly from the CSV. Month-over-month and year-over-year changes are calculated from consecutive monthly observations and 12-month lags, respectively. Standard deviations are sample standard deviations.
