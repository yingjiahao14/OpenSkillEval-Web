Indexing Lag Note: PubMed records accumulate over time — 2024 entries have had longer to be fully indexed, producing an inherent volume advantage (~80% of records). 2025 and 2026 counts should be interpreted as lower bounds, not absolute measures. Additionally, 575 records (14.7% of 2024) carry an "Unknown" publication month and are excluded from monthly trend charts.
Publication Volume by Year
2024 dominates with 3,924 records (80.3% of the sample) — a real-world artifact of indexing latency. The 2025 and 2026 cohorts will grow as indexing catches up, making comparisons across years best performed on rates rather than raw counts.
Monthly Output (Known Months)
January 2024 shows an anomalous spike of 1,665 publications — consistent with a dataset where records indexed throughout 2024 were retroactively assigned to "January" when the exact month was unclear. Later months trend down through mid-year then recover in Q4 2024 and into 2025–2026.
2025–2026 Monthly Trend (Excluding the Jan 2024 artifact)
Excluding the Jan 2024 spike, both 2025 and early 2026 show a clear upward trajectory. December 2025 (94 records) and the Q1 2026 ramp (51 → 76 → 98) signal accelerating indexing of recent publications, consistent with the dataset's recency effect. March 2026 is the highest single month since January 2024.

Key Takeaways & Strategic Implications
Synthesized findings for research policy, funding prioritization, and institutional strategy. Ordered by estimated strategic impact.
Priority Finding 1 — Open Access

OA Adoption Has Crossed a Tipping Point

The 2025 open-access rate of 74.1% — up 10 percentage points from 2024 — signals that open publishing is becoming the majority norm rather than the exception. Seven of the top 15 journals by volume are OA mega-journals or preprint servers. Policy mandates (NIH, EU Horizon) appear to be producing measurable structural shifts. Funding agencies should treat OA compliance infrastructure as table-stakes, not optional.

Priority Finding 2 — Geographic Power

Asia-Pacific Bloc Controls ~35% of Global Output

China alone accounts for 22.1% of the dataset, with the Asia-Pacific region (China, Japan, India, South Korea, Australia) collectively contributing roughly 35%. India's YoY share growth from 3.3% (2024) to 8.0% (2025) is the strongest national acceleration in the dataset. Strategic funders should model engagement frameworks that assume Asia-Pacific parity, not marginal contribution.

Priority Finding 3 — Topic Momentum

Post-COVID Research Decline Creates Funding Gaps

COVID-19 dropped 28% in normalized topic share from 2024 to 2025, after dominating the dataset as the single largest topic. This creates a reallocation opportunity — and risk. Research infrastructure built up during the pandemic (rapid-response platforms, surveillance networks, global consortia) may be underutilized. Funders should evaluate whether pandemic-era capacity can be redeployed toward emerging infectious disease preparedness.

Priority Finding 4 — Collaboration Norms

High-Impact Research Is Multi-Author by Default

With a median of 6 authors per paper and a mean of 7.1 across nearly 5,000 records, solo authorship is the exception. Preprints — the most collaborative format at median 9 authors — also carry the highest OA rates. Grant structures designed around individual PI models may misalign with how biomedical science is actually organized. Team-science funding mechanisms deserve increased emphasis.

Priority Finding 5 — Emerging Topics

MicroRNAs & Lung Neoplasms Show Durable Growth

After filtering for minimum threshold (≥5 records in 2024), MicroRNAs show an 18% normalized growth rate into 2025, and Lung Neoplasms / MRI show modest but consistent gains. These signals are modest in this sample size but consistent with broader field-level trends in RNA therapeutics and precision oncology imaging. They represent credible investment targets for translational research pipelines.

Priority Finding 6 — Data Quality

Indexing Lag Structurally Distorts Year-over-Year Comparisons

The 80/15/5 split across 2024/2025/2026 is a PubMed indexing artifact, not a reflection of real output. 2025 and 2026 counts are lower bounds and will grow. Any policy decision benchmarking "2025 output decline" against 2024 would be methodologically unsound. Internal datasets should be refreshed quarterly and normalized rates should be preferred over raw counts in all annual comparisons.
