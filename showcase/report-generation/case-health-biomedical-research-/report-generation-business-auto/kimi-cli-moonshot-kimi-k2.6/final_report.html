1. Publication Volume & Temporal Distribution

The dataset spans January 2024 through March 2026, with a pronounced temporal skew: 80.3% of records are from 2024, 15.1% from 2025, and only 4.6% from 2026. This distribution is a natural artifact of PubMed indexing lag — older publications accumulate more indexed entries over time — and must be interpreted accordingly.

Data Quality Note: 15.4% of records (754 abstracts) have an unknown publication month. Monthly trend analyses exclude these records. Comparisons across years should use normalized rates or proportions rather than raw counts.

Monthly Publication Volume

The January 2024 spike (1,665 abstracts) dominates the series and likely reflects a bulk indexing event or backfill rather than a true seasonal surge. Excluding this outlier, monthly volumes in 2024 decline from ~380 in Q1 to ~40–70 in Q4. 2025 shows a more stable monthly pattern (26–94 abstracts), while early 2026 data (Jan–Mar) suggests a possible uptick, though the small sample size (225 total) precludes firm conclusions.

3. Research Topic & MeSH Keyword Analysis

MeSH indexing is incomplete for newer publications: 47.1% of records lack a major topic assignment. Among the 2,583 indexed records, COVID-19 remains the dominant major topic (43 abstracts), followed by Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (24) and Breast Neoplasms (17).

Indexing Lag Bias: Because MeSH terms are assigned retrospectively, 2025–2026 publications are disproportionately unindexed. Topic trend comparisons should therefore focus on indexed subsets and use normalized shares rather than raw counts.

When normalized by total publications per year, a few topics show relative share growth from 2024 to 2025:

  • Post-Traumatic Stress Disorders — share grew from 0.08% to 0.27%

Top MeSH Keywords

The most frequently indexed MeSH terms reflect the demographic and methodological focus of the corpus: Humans (1,842), Female (707), Male (606), Animals (537), and Adult (340). Notable keyword trends include a relative decline in Humans (41.9% share in 2024 → 21.4% in 2025) and Animals (12.5% → 5.7%), which may again reflect indexing lag rather than a true methodological shift.

4. Open Access Adoption & Journal Landscape

The overall open-access rate across the dataset is 65.1%. However, this figure masks significant temporal and geographic variation — a critical consideration for policy analysts tracking publishing norms.

Open Access Rate Over Time

Monthly OA rates are volatile, ranging from 23.5% (March 2026) to 88.2% (January 2026). The 2025 annual rate of 74.1% exceeds 2024's 64.1%, but the March 2026 drop to 23.5% — driven by a small sample (98 abstracts) — underscores the instability of early-year metrics. The December 2024 peak (85.9%) and January 2026 spike (88.2%) may reflect seasonal submission or editorial-batch effects at fully OA journals.

OA Rate by Research Type

Iran leads OA adoption among major contributors at 86.9%, followed by Japan (77.4%) and the USA (76.4%). China, despite its volume dominance, has the lowest OA rate among the top 10 at 55.5% — a policy-relevant gap. By research type, Preprints are 100% open access by design, while Randomized Controlled Trials (43.1%) and Meta-Analyses (36.8%) show the lowest OA penetration.

Leading Journals

The volume leaders reveal a bifurcated journal landscape. Fully OA titles — Scientific Reports (82), Heliyon (76), Cureus (69), PLOS ONE (37) — dominate the top ranks. In contrast, subscription-heavy outlets such as The Science of the Total Environment (2% OA), Zootaxa (0% OA), and Food Chemistry (0% OA) contribute significant volume without open accessibility.

Secondary classifications include Research Support, Non-U.S. Gov't (9.5%), Review (8.3%), and Case Reports (4.2%). Collaboration Patterns

The median author count is 6 (mean: 7.1). The distribution is right-skewed: 16.3% of papers have 1–3 authors, 26.2% have 4–5, 22.1% have 6–7, and 19.4% have 8–10. Large collaborations (21+ authors) are rare (1.7%).

By research type, the highest median author counts appear in Clinical Trials Phase II (28), Practice Guidelines (18), and Clinical Trials Phase III (18) — reflecting the multi-site, multi-disciplinary nature of late-stage therapeutic research. Preprints show a median of 9 authors, suggesting that early dissemination is increasingly a team activity.

6. Key Takeaways & Strategic Implications

1
Temporal skew is structural, not anomalous. With 80% of records from 2024, any cross-year comparison must be normalized. Funding strategists should treat 2025–2026 figures as directional signals rather than definitive baselines. The January 2024 volume spike (1,665 abstracts) is likely an indexing artifact and should be excluded from seasonality models.
2
China's volume dominance contrasts with a below-average OA rate. At 22.1% of output but only 55.5% OA, China represents both the largest source of biomedical research and the biggest opportunity for open-access policy leverage. Interventions here — institutional mandates, APC subsidies, or national licensing deals — would have outsized impact on global accessibility.
3
Open access is rising but volatile. The 2025 OA rate (74.1%) exceeds 2024 (64.1%), yet month-to-month swings are extreme (23%–88%). Policy analysts should use rolling quarterly averages rather than single-month snapshots. The March 2026 drop warrants monitoring as the year progresses.
4
Topic momentum is shifting away from pandemic-era priorities. COVID-19's normalized share declined from 2024 to 2025. Emerging relative-growth areas include COPD, PTSD, and electroencephalography research. Funding bodies should note that infectious-disease urgency may be giving way to chronic-disease and neuroscience priorities in the indexed literature.
5
MeSH indexing lag creates a blind spot for real-time topic tracking. With 47% of records unindexed, major-topic analysis under-represents the most recent research. Funding strategists should supplement PubMed MeSH data with preprint servers, grant databases, and altmetrics for forward-looking topic intelligence.
6
The journal landscape is polarized. A small set of fully OA mega-journals (Scientific Reports, Heliyon, Cureus, PLOS ONE) captures a disproportionate share of volume. Subscription journals with high output (e.g., The Science of the Total Environment) contribute minimally to open accessibility. Portfolio evaluations should weight accessibility alongside impact factor.
