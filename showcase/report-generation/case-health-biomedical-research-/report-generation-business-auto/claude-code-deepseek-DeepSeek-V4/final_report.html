Biomedical Research Landscape 2024–2026
Publication Trends, Topic Dynamics, and Open Access Patterns

A comprehensive trend analysis of 4,886 biomedical research abstracts indexed in PubMed from 2024 to early 2026, examining temporal publication patterns, geographic contributions, dominant research topics, and open-access adoption.

Key Observations

  • 2025 stabilization: Monthly volumes in 2025 range from 26–94 (median 45), showing a more even distribution after the January surge effect.
  • Q1 2026 trajectory: Early 2026 shows an upward monthly trend (51→76→98) that may indicate accelerating index catch-up, though the sample is too small for confident projection.

The OA rate fluctuates month-to-month (range: 23.5%–88.2%), with higher volatility in low-volume months. The overall trend shows a modest upward drift, with 2025 averaging 69.2% vs. 2024's 57.2% among months with known dates.

OA Rate by Country (Top 12)

OA adoption varies significantly by country. Iran (86.9%), USA (76.8%), Japan (77.4%), and UK (78.0%) lead in OA rates, while China (55.5%) and Unknown-affiliation records (25.0%) trail.

Top Journals by Publication Volume

The journal landscape is highly fragmented: 2,055 distinct journals across 4,886 publications (mean 2.4 papers/journal). The top 15 journals account for only 11.2% of all publications. Open-access megajournals (Scientific Reports, Heliyon, Cureus, PLOS ONE) dominate the top positions by volume, each operating at or near 100% OA. Traditional subscription-model journals (e.g., Food Chemistry, Journal of Hazardous Materials) show OA rates near 0%.

Research Type Distribution & Collaboration Indicators

Research Type Composition

Journal Articles overwhelmingly dominate (4,864, 99.6% of records — note: publications may carry multiple type labels). Reviews (406) and Case Reports (204) form the next tier. Preprints (41) represent 0.8% of the sample, concentrated almost entirely in 2024.

OA Rate by Research Type

Preprints are 100% OA by definition. Case Reports show the highest OA rate among traditional types (80.4%), while Meta-Analyses (36.8%) and Randomized Controlled Trials (43.1%) are less likely to be OA — possibly reflecting their concentration in traditional subscription journals.

Author Count Distribution (Collaboration Intensity)

The median publication has 6 authors (mean 7.1), with a long tail extending to 115 authors on large consortia studies. The modal range is 6–10 authors (37.7% of publications), consistent with modern team-science norms in biomedicine.

Median Authors by Research Type

Preprints lead in collaboration intensity (median 9.0 authors), followed by Systematic Reviews (7.0) and Meta-Analyses (7.0). Case Reports have the smallest teams (median 5.0), consistent with their nature as focused clinical observations.

Collaboration proxy: Author count serves as a rough proxy for research collaboration intensity. Higher medians in preprints, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses reflect the inherently collaborative nature of these research modes. The overall median of 6 authors across all types indicates that team science is the norm in biomedical research.
Section 6

Key Takeaways & Strategic Implications

The following findings are synthesized from the full analysis above, prioritized by strategic relevance for research policy and funding decisions.

  1. Priority 1 — Data Infrastructure
    PubMed indexing lag is the dominant signal in temporal analysis. With ~80% of indexed records from 2024 and progressively thinner coverage through 2026, raw publication counts should never be interpreted as output trends. Funding agencies and research evaluators should use normalized, rate-based metrics and acknowledge that real-time publication monitoring requires sources beyond PubMed (e.g., preprint servers, institutional repositories).
  2. Priority 2 — Open Access Policy
    Open access adoption is high but uneven — 65.1% overall, ranging from 25% to 87% by country. The 10-percentage-point gap between China's OA rate (55.5%) and the US/UK/Japan cluster (~77%) has strategic implications for global research accessibility. Funders with OA mandates should monitor country-level compliance rates, as national OA policies appear to be a stronger predictor of adoption than journal-level factors alone.
  3. Priority 3 — Research Prioritization
    COVID-19 research is transitioning from acute response to long-term sequelae. While COVID-19 remains the top MeSH topic, its normalized keyword prevalence is declining sharply. Adjacent topics — environmental health, air pollution, and health systems research — show accelerating keyword growth, suggesting the field is pivoting from pathogen-focused research to systemic health impacts.
  4. Priority 4 — Geographic Concentration
    China and the US together produce one-third of indexed biomedical research, but the concentration is declining. The top 10 countries account for 52.4% of all publications. However, with 1,126 distinct country affiliations represented (including non-standardized names), the long tail of global contributors is substantial. Emerging research nations merit dedicated tracking as indexing data matures.
  5. Priority 5 — MeSH Indexing Gap
    Nearly half (47.1%) of publications lack MeSH indexing, concentrated in 2025–2026. This creates a systematic blind spot for topic-based research intelligence. Policy analysts relying on MeSH for landscape monitoring should supplement with keyword and text-mining approaches for recent publications, and budget for a 12–18 month indexing lag in PubMed-based topic analyses.
  6. Priority 6 — Journal Fragmentation
    The biomedical journal landscape is highly fragmented (2,055 journals for 4,886 papers). OA megajournals (Scientific Reports, Heliyon, Cureus, PLOS ONE) lead in volume, but collectively represent only ~5.4% of publications. This fragmentation means that journal-level policies have limited reach — systemic changes in OA adoption, data sharing, and research quality standards require funder-level mandates, not journal-level initiatives.
  7. Priority 7 — Collaboration Norms
    Team science is the default mode: median 6 authors per paper, with preprints and systematic reviews showing the largest teams. Funding models that assume small-lab, single-PI research are misaligned with the dominant collaboration pattern. Grant mechanisms should accommodate large, multi-institution teams as the baseline, not the exception.

Limitations & Caveats

  • Sample size: This analysis uses 4,886 records — a representative sample of the full 126,832-record dataset. Margin of error for percentages is approximately ±1.4% at 95% confidence.
  • Temporal skew: The 2024-heavy distribution is a PubMed indexing artifact. Year-over-year comparisons should use normalized rates and acknowledge the immaturity of 2025–2026 data.
  • Country standardization: Country names were consolidated for known variants (e.g., "China" ← "PR China", "People's Republic of China"), but 1,126 unique country strings remain, suggesting residual non-standardization.
  • MeSH coverage: Only 52.9% of records have MeSH terms. Topic analysis reflects the indexed subset and may underrepresent very recent research.
  • Language bias: 99.8% of records are in English. Non-English biomedical research is not represented in this dataset.
