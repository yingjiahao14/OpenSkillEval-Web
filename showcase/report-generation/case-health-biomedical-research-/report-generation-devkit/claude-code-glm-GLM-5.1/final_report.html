2. Publication Volume & Temporal Distribution

The temporal distribution of publications reveals a pronounced skew toward 2024, which accounts for 80.3% of all records. This is a known artifact of PubMed's indexing pipeline: older publications accumulate more fully indexed entries over time, while recent publications (especially from late 2025 and early 2026) are still being processed. Accordingly, raw count comparisons across years should be interpreted as reflecting indexing lag rather than actual research output decline.

Note: 754 records with unknown publication months are excluded from monthly trends. The Jan-2024 spike (1,665 publications) likely reflects both a genuine seasonal pattern and backfill indexing.

Quarterly Trends

The quarterly view smooths monthly volatility and reveals that once the Q1 2024 indexing backlog is excluded, publication volumes remain relatively stable from Q2 2024 through Q4 2025, ranging from 119 to 545 records per quarter. The Q4 2025 uptick (169 records) and strong Q1 2026 showing (225 records) may signal either a genuine acceleration or continued indexing catch-up.

Methodological note: Any analysis of growth or decline over this window must account for the fact that 2024 data has had substantially more time to accumulate MeSH indexing, DOIs, and other metadata. The apparent decline from 2024 to 2026 is primarily an artifact, not a real-world contraction in research output.

3. Geographic Landscape of Biomedical Research

China and the USA dominate the dataset, together contributing 33.8% of all publications. The top 10 countries account for 53.3% of total output, while the remaining 46.4% is distributed across over 90 nations — indicating a long tail of smaller contributing countries.

Country names were consolidated (e.g., "PR China" and "People's Republic of China" mapped to "China"; "USA" and "United States" mapped to "USA"). Hong Kong and Jiangxi Province records were attributed to China. 144 records had unknown country affiliations.

7. Key Takeaways & Strategic Implications

1. Indexing lag distorts temporal interpretation — normalize accordingly.
The 80%/15%/5% split across 2024/2025/2026 is primarily a PubMed indexing artifact, not a signal of declining research output. Policy analysts should use rates and normalized metrics rather than raw counts when comparing years. The apparent steep drop after Q1 2024 is largely backfill-related.
2. China's research volume is dominant, but its OA rate lags behind peers.
China contributes 23.0% of all publications — more than double the USA's 11.0% — yet its open-access rate (54.5%) is the lowest among top-15 producers. This gap is a significant policy lever. Funders and institutions pushing for open science should prioritize engagement with Chinese research institutions and publishers to close this gap.
3. COVID-19 research is declining rapidly; emerging topics are diversifying.
COVID-19 remains the #1 major topic overall, but with a -16.9% average monthly decline rate, it is clearly on the wane. Fast-rising topics include E. coli research (+44.4% MoM), long noncoding RNA (+33.3%), and non-small-cell lung carcinoma (+16.7%). Funding strategists should monitor these areas for sustained momentum.
4. Open access is trending upward, but progress is uneven.
The overall OA rate of 65.1% represents substantial adoption, with 2025 reaching 74.1%. However, this progress is driven by fully OA mega-journals (Scientific Reports, Heliyon, Cureus) rather than systemic change across all journals. Traditional subscription-based journals still account for a large share of closed-access publications.
5. The geographic research base is slowly diversifying.
The top 10 countries' share has declined from 54.3% (2024) to 48.4% (2026), suggesting that research output is gradually spreading to a broader set of nations. India's rising share (3.3% to 8.0% in 2025) is particularly notable and may warrant increased funding attention.
6. Collaboration intensity varies significantly by country and research type.
Systematic reviews, RCTs, and preprints show median author counts of 8, indicating high-collaboration research designs. Italy leads in team size (median 8), while India and Iran operate with smaller teams (median 5). Funders seeking to promote multi-institutional research should consider these baselines when designing collaboration incentives.

8. Methodology & Data Notes

Data Source

The dataset comprises 4,886 biomedical research abstracts sourced from NCBI PubMed, published between January 2024 and March 2026. Data was obtained from Kaggle (kanchana1990/biomedical-research-abstracts-20242026) and underwent prior QA/QC including deduplication by PMID and title, garbage text removal, abstract length filtering (50–1,000 words), and date-range scope filtering.

Data Cleaning Applied

  • Country consolidation: Variants such as "PR China", "People's Republic of China", "P R China", and "Jiangxi Province" were mapped to "China"; "United States" and "United States of America" mapped to "USA"; "Republic of Korea" mapped to "South Korea"; "Hong Kong" mapped to "China".
  • Unknown months: 754 records with pub_month = "Unknown" (pub_month_num = 0) were excluded from monthly and quarterly trend analyses but included in yearly aggregates.
  • Missing MeSH data: 2,303 records (47.1%) lack keywords and major_topic values, consistent with indexing lag for newer publications. Topic analyses are restricted to the 2,583 records with available major_topic data.
  • Research type parsing: The research_type field contains semicolon-separated values. Individual types were extracted for frequency analysis; the first non-funding-related type was used as the "primary type" for per-record classification.

Limitations

  • The dataset represents a subset of PubMed and may not be fully representative of all biomedical research output globally.
  • Temporal comparisons are confounded by PubMed's indexing lag, which disproportionately affects 2025 and 2026 records.
  • Country attribution is based on the first author's affiliation only, which may undercount multi-national collaborations.
  • Emerging topic growth rates are computed from small monthly counts (single-digit publications per topic per month) and are therefore subject to high volatility. They should be interpreted as directional signals rather than precise estimates.
  • The 2026 data covers only January–March, making year-level comparisons with 2024 (full 12 months) misleading without normalization.
