1. Executive Overview

This report analyzes 12,343 records spanning 10 job roles, 6 countries, and 21 years (2015–2035) to assess how AI automation is reshaping workforce dynamics. The analysis draws from synthetic trend-based data modeled on realistic global patterns. Below are the five most critical takeaways for executive decision-making.

1. AI automation risk is accelerating across all roles. The average AI risk score has risen 72% over the study period — from 0.30 in 2015 to a projected 0.51 in 2035. Every single job category tracked shows a monotonic increase in risk year over year.
2. Business Analysts and Data Analysts face existential automation pressure. These two roles carry the highest average AI risk scores (0.75 and 0.65 respectively) and are the only roles with survival rates below 60%. By 2035, Business Analysts reach a risk score of 0.85 — the dataset ceiling. Their primary skills (Excel, SQL) are among the most substitutable by AI.
3. AI Researchers, ML Engineers, and Cybersecurity Analysts are the most resilient. These three roles maintain 100% job survival rates throughout the projection period, with average risk scores below 0.26. Their specialized skills (Deep Learning, Python, Security) remain difficult to automate and continue to command the highest median salaries ($42.8K, $39.3K, and $27.7K respectively).
4. Higher automation risk correlates with lower compensation. High-risk roles earn a median salary of $21,683 versus $34,105–$34,710 for low- and medium-risk roles (Pearson r = −0.24). This "automation penalty" reinforces the urgency of reskilling workers in vulnerable categories.
5. Geographic divergence: India faces compound risk; USA leads in resilience. India combines the lowest median salaries ($12,448) with the lowest survival rate (78.6%) and highest proportion of at-risk workers. The USA sustains a 93.8% survival rate with median salaries reaching $53,354 by 2035.

The remainder of this report examines each dimension in detail, supported by trend charts and segmented analysis.

2. AI Automation Risk Landscape

AI automation risk scores range from 0.05 to 0.85 across the dataset. Across all records, 48.7% fall into the Medium Risk category, 32.7% into Low Risk, and 18.6% into High Risk. However, the distribution is shifting over time: by the 2030–2035 window, Low Risk records shrink to under 9% of annual observations while Medium Risk dominates at over 70%.

3. Salary Trends & Segmentation

Global median salary has grown from $24,247 in 2015 to a projected $36,763 in 2035, a cumulative increase of 52%. However, this growth is unevenly distributed across countries, experience levels, and — critically — across AI risk categories.

3.1 Salary Trend by Country

The USA leads and is pulling further ahead, with median salaries rising from $35,320 (2015) to $53,354 (2035) — a 51% increase. India, despite consistent growth (78% increase from $9,669 to $17,248), remains far below other markets. The UK shows the strongest relative performance among non-US markets, reaching $43,648 by 2035.

3.2 Salary Trend by Experience Level

The experience premium is widening. Senior professionals saw their median salary grow from $38,939 to $63,053 (+62%), while entry-level salaries grew from $18,083 to $29,195 (+61%). In absolute terms, the senior-entry gap expanded from $20,856 to $33,859.

5. Job Survival & Market Outlook

Job survival classification divides records into three classes: 0 (at risk), 1 (moderate survival), and 2 (strong survival). Across the full dataset, 11.0% of records are classified as at-risk, 47.0% as moderate, and 42.0% as strong survival.

5.1 Survival Rate Trend Over Time

The overall survival rate has declined from 91.1% in 2015 to a projected 83.7% in 2035 — a 7.4 percentage-point erosion. The proportion of "at-risk" (class 0) records has grown from 8.9% to 16.3%. Meanwhile, "strong survival" (class 2) records declined from 45.6% to 36.0%.

6. Strategic Recommendations

Each recommendation below is grounded in a specific data finding from this analysis.

R1. Prioritize Reskilling for Business Analysts and Data Analysts

Data Finding: Business Analysts (0.75 avg risk, 43% survival) and Data Analysts (0.65 avg risk, 57% survival) face the highest automation pressure. These are the only roles with 0% strong-survival records.

Action: Launch targeted reskilling programs to transition these workers toward Python-centric data science, cloud engineering, or cybersecurity pathways. The data shows that moving primary skill from Excel/SQL to Python alone raises survival rates from 43–57% to 99.9%. Prioritize workers with 3+ years of tenure who have institutional knowledge worth preserving.

R2. Expand Investment in AI Research, ML Engineering, and Cybersecurity

Data Finding: AI Researcher, ML Engineer, and Cybersecurity Analyst maintain 100% survival rates and the highest salary premiums ($42.8K, $39.3K, $27.7K median). Deep Learning and Security skills show the strongest survival profiles.

Action: Increase headcount and compensation budgets for these three roles. The combination of low risk, high survival, and growing demand makes them the safest long-term talent investments. Consider creating internal "AI Centers of Excellence" staffed primarily by AI Researchers and ML Engineers.

R3. Monitor Software Engineers for Emerging Risk

Data Finding: Software Engineers have a current risk score of 0.45 (Medium) that will reach 0.55 by 2035. While their survival rate is 97.4%, their strong-survival rate is only 20.5% — far below similarly-scored roles.

Action: Invest in upskilling Software Engineers toward cloud, DevOps, and ML competencies. The data suggests that generalist coding skills are becoming increasingly automatable, but engineers who layer on cloud (AWS/Docker) or ML specialization can transition into roles with much stronger survival profiles.

R4. Address India's Compound Risk with Market-Specific Strategy

Data Finding: India has the lowest survival rate (78.6%), lowest strong-survival rate (8.8%), and lowest median salary ($12,448). This compound disadvantage makes Indian workforce segments particularly vulnerable.

Action: Develop India-specific reskilling initiatives focused on high-survival skills (Python, Deep Learning, Security). Consider adjusting hiring profiles in India toward AI-resilient roles. For existing India-based Business Analyst and Data Analyst teams, accelerate migration toward data science and ML engineering functions.

R5. De-Emphasize Education-Based Hiring; Emphasize Skill-Based Assessment

Data Finding: Education level shows virtually no correlation with salary (Bachelor: $31,569 vs. PhD: $31,672) or survival rate (89.3% vs. 89.7%). In contrast, primary skill creates massive survival divergences (Deep Learning: 100% vs. Excel: 43%).

Action: Shift hiring and promotion criteria from degree requirements to demonstrated skill portfolios. Invest in skills-based assessment tools and internal skill taxonomies that map directly to the resilience tiers identified in this analysis.

R6. Build Workforce Planning Around Role Risk Tiers, Not Seniority

Data Finding: Experience level has negligible impact on AI risk (Entry: 0.396 vs. Senior: 0.393). The role itself is the dominant risk driver, with a 0.60-point spread between AI Researcher (0.15) and Business Analyst (0.75).

Action: Redesign workforce planning models to segment by role-risk tier rather than experience band. Allocate reskilling budgets proportionally to role risk scores rather than distributing evenly across the organization.

Data Notes & Methodology

Source: Kaggle synthetic dataset (shree0910/ai-job-risk-and-salary-dataset-20152035), 12,343 records across 13 columns. The data is synthetically generated using realistic assumptions inspired by global job market patterns, AI adoption rates, and salary distributions.

Time Horizon: 2015–2024 represent historical-analog periods; 2025–2035 represent simulated projections.

Key Assumptions: AI risk scores increase linearly at +0.01 per year per role. Salary values are in USD and do not account for purchasing power parity. Skill demand scores represent a relative index (0–100) reflecting market demand.

Limitations: As a synthetic dataset, findings should inform directional thinking rather than precision forecasting. Geographic salary comparisons do not adjust for cost of living. The linear risk progression may not capture potential step-function disruptions from breakthrough AI capabilities.
