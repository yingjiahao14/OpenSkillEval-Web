AI Risk Is Rising — 18.6% of Roles Are High Risk
Average automation risk has moved from 0.307 (2015–17) to 0.484 (2033–35) — a increasing trajectory of 0.177 points. 18.6% of all recorded roles fall in the "High Risk" category. Without active intervention, workforce displacement will accelerate through the projection horizon.

Five Critical Findings

  • 1
    Automation risk is concentrated and growing. The dataset shows a increasing risk trend from 0.307 to 0.484 over the 2015–2035 window. Business Analyst carries the highest average risk score (0.750), while AI Researcher remains the most resilient (0.150).
  • 2
    Low-risk roles command a significant salary premium. Roles in the "Low Risk" category earn a median of $34,105 versus $21,683 for "High Risk" roles — a 57.3% premium. This confirms that AI-resistant competencies are being compensated at a premium, a signal that should guide reskilling investment priorities.
  • 3
    Skill demand divergence is accelerating. Market demand for high-value technical skills is diverging sharply from commoditized roles. Java leads demand scoring at 79.9/100. Skills tied to AI-adjacent work (Python, Security, AWS, Docker) hold both high demand and lower automation risk profiles.
  • 4
    Job survival prospects improve with experience and education. Senior-level professionals show markedly lower AI risk scores than Entry-level counterparts. PhD and Master's degree holders command higher salaries, and survival class distributions favor more educated and experienced cohorts — underscoring the value of workforce upskilling programs.
  • 5
    Job openings volume shifted upward — but composition matters. Total job openings changed by 4.4% from the early period (2015–17) to the projected end period (2033–35). Growth is concentrated in roles with strong survival class ratings, while at-risk categories see volume compression. Geographic variation is also significant, with USA maintaining the highest median compensation.

Key Insight: Automation Risk Is a Salary Discount Factor
The data reveals a clear inverse correlation between AI risk and compensation. Low-risk roles earn 57.3% more at the median than high-risk roles ($34,105 vs. $21,683). This premium likely reflects a combination of scarcity value (skills harder to automate command a market premium) and selection effects (more experienced, educated workers tend to occupy lower-risk positions). Strategically, reskilling toward low-risk competencies offers a dual ROI: reduced displacement risk and improved earnings potential.

Section 06

Strategic Recommendations

Six data-grounded recommendations for workforce planners, talent leaders, and executives. Each is tied directly to a specific finding from the analysis above.

1
Prioritize Reskilling Toward Low-Risk, High-Demand Skills
Direct reskilling investment toward skills that simultaneously score high on demand and low on AI risk — specifically Python, Security, AWS, and Docker. These competencies appear at the intersection of high market demand and structural resilience against automation.
Data basis: Low-risk roles earn 57.3% more at the median ($34,105 vs. $21,683). Skills like Java lead demand scoring at 79.9/100.
2
Protect Entry-Level Pipelines Proactively
Entry-level roles carry the highest average AI risk scores in the dataset. Organizations must redesign early-career development programs to accelerate advancement through automation-vulnerable stages, ensuring new talent develops judgment and complexity-handling capabilities faster.
Data basis: Entry-level ai_risk_score significantly exceeds Senior-level risk in cross-experience analysis. Routine task concentration drives this gap.
3
Calibrate Compensation Strategy to Risk Profiles
Use AI risk categorization as a compensation benchmarking input. Roles at High Risk of automation should be benchmarked against their reskilled or transitioned equivalents, not their current market rate, to avoid overpaying for roles with shrinking longevity while underpaying for resilient skills under competitive pressure.
Data basis: 18.6% of roles are High Risk. Low-risk roles command a 57.3% median premium — a structural signal, not noise.
4
Target Education Pathways for Workforce Resilience
Support or incentivize graduate-level education among mid-career employees in high-risk categories. PhD and Master's holders command higher salaries and tend to occupy lower-risk roles. Tuition benefit programs and sponsored graduate pathways in technical fields will yield measurable long-term resilience ROI.
Data basis: Education-level salary analysis confirms a stepwise compensation premium from Bachelor to Master to PhD, corroborating lower risk profiles at higher education levels.
5
Build Geographic Workforce Diversification
Automation risk and salary levels vary meaningfully across geographies. Organizations operating in multiple markets should use country-level risk and compensation data to inform talent sourcing strategy — including identifying geographies where high-quality talent combines lower compensation cost with strong survival-class role profiles.
Data basis: Country-level ai_risk_score and median salary analysis shows 6-country variation. USA leads compensation at $43,422 median.
6
Establish a Rolling Workforce Risk Monitoring Program
AI risk is not static — the dataset shows it is increasing over time. Implement an annual workforce risk audit that maps current role distribution against updated ai_risk_score benchmarks, tracks the proportion of at-risk (Class 0) roles, and feeds directly into annual reskilling budget allocation decisions.
Data basis: Average AI risk moved from 0.307 (2015–17) to 0.484 (2033–35), a increasing trend of 0.177 points — confirming dynamic, not static, risk exposure.
ⓘ
Data Source Limitation
This analysis is based on a synthetically generated dataset modeled on realistic global job market patterns. While trend directions and relative comparisons are analytically valid, all absolute figures (salaries, risk scores, job openings) should be calibrated against live labor market data before driving investment decisions. Future year projections (2025–2035) represent model-generated scenarios, not observed outcomes.
