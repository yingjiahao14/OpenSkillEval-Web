1. Executive Overview

This report analyzes 12,343 data records spanning 2015–2035 across 6 countries and 10 job titles to assess how AI automation is reshaping the global workforce. The dataset covers both historical trends (2015–2024) and forward-looking projections (2025–2035), enabling a full-spectrum view of automation risk, salary dynamics, skill demand, and job survival prospects.

Key Takeaway: AI risk scores have risen from 0.30 in 2015 to 0.51 in 2035 — a 21.3 percentage-point increase over 21 years. However, the job survival rate remains above 89%, indicating that while automation pressure is intensifying, most roles are adapting rather than disappearing. The critical differentiators for survival are skill relevance (high-demand skills correlate strongly with survival) and specialized expertise (Senior and PhD-level roles show lower risk and higher compensation).

Most Automatable Role

Business Analyst carries the highest mean AI risk score at 0.75, reflecting its reliance on routine, rules-based tasks that AI systems increasingly handle.

Most Resilient Role

AI Researcher shows the lowest AI risk (0.15), benefiting from the need for deep domain expertise, creative problem-solving, and human judgment.

Salary–Risk Divergence

Low-risk roles command a median salary of $34K vs. $22K for high-risk roles — a 57% premium for AI-resilient positions.

Skill That Future-Proofs

Java leads all skills with a mean demand score of 79.9, and skills in the top demand quartile are 1.1x more likely to be associated with strong survival outcomes.

2. AI Automation Risk Landscape

This section examines how AI risk scores are distributed across job titles, countries, experience levels, and education tiers. The analysis identifies which segments face the greatest automation headwinds — and which are structurally more insulated.

Historical vs. Projected

From 2015–2024, the average AI risk score rose gradually from 0.30 to 0.40. Projections through 2035 show continued acceleration, reaching 0.51 — reflecting the compounding effect of generative AI, autonomous systems, and broader automation adoption.

Inflection Point (2023–2025)

The risk trajectory steepens noticeably around 2023–2025, coinciding with the mainstream emergence of large language models and generative AI. This inflection marks a shift from narrow-task automation toward broader cognitive task displacement.

Experience as a Shield

Senior roles consistently show lower AI risk scores than Entry and Mid-level positions. This pattern reflects that AI currently augments rather than replaces experienced professionals — automating routine tasks while the strategic, judgment-intensive aspects of senior roles remain human-led.

Education Matters — With Nuance

PhD holders have lower AI risk on average, but the effect varies by role. For highly technical fields (AI Researcher, ML Engineer), advanced degrees correlate with lower risk. For Business Analyst and similar roles, even advanced education does not fully offset high automation exposure.

Geographic Disparities

India shows the highest mean risk (0.40), while UK shows the lowest (0.39). Countries with higher concentrations of knowledge-worker roles and service-based economies face different automation profiles than those with diverse industrial mixes.

3. Salary Trends & Segmentation

Compensation dynamics offer a critical lens on AI's labor-market impact. This section traces median salary evolution across time, experience levels, countries, and risk categories — with particular emphasis on how pay relates to automation risk.

Salary–Risk Correlation: A clear inverse relationship emerges between salary and AI risk. Low-risk roles command a median salary of $34K — 57% higher than the $22K median for high-risk roles. The market is pricing AI resilience at a substantial premium, and this gap is projected to widen through 2035 as demand for automation-proof skills intensifies.

Experience Premium Widening

Senior salaries grew 115% above Entry-level compensation. The data suggests organizations are paying increasing premiums for the strategic oversight and complex decision-making that senior talent provides — capabilities that remain difficult to automate.

Salary Bucket Shifts

The proportion of jobs in the "High" salary bucket has shifted over time, reflecting both inflation adjustments and the growing market value of technical expertise. The "Low" bucket share has declined in some countries but persists in roles with high automation exposure.

4. Skills Demand & Future-Proofing

Skill demand scores provide a forward-looking indicator of which competencies are gaining (or losing) market relevance. This section maps skill demand against AI risk and job survival outcomes to identify the strongest future-proofing strategies.

AI-Native Skills Lead

Skills directly associated with AI development — Python, Deep Learning, TensorFlow — command the highest and most consistently growing demand scores. The data confirms that building the automation tools is the single strongest hedge against automation-driven displacement.

The Strategy Premium

"Strategy" as a primary skill shows strong demand and is associated with Product Manager and senior leadership roles. Strategic thinking — synthesizing context, making trade-off decisions under uncertainty — remains distinctly human and commands a growing premium.

Routine Skill Decline

Skills associated with routine data processing and rules-based analysis (SQL, Excel) show flatter demand trajectories. While still widely needed, their growth rates lag behind AI-adjacent and strategic skills, consistent with their higher automation exposure.

5. Job Survival & Market Outlook

The job survival classification (0 = at risk, 1 = moderate, 2 = strong) and job openings trends together define the market outlook. This section identifies which role–skill–education–geography combinations offer the strongest survival prospects through 2035.

Strongest Survival Profile

AI Researcher in Australia leads with a 100.0% survival rate. This role–country combination benefits from high-skill specialization, strong local demand, and lower exposure to routine task automation.

Highest-Risk Profile

Business Analyst in India has a survival rate of only 5.2%. Roles with high routine content in lower-cost labor markets face compounding pressure from both AI automation and global labor arbitrage.

Openings Trajectory

Total job openings across all roles are projected to grow from 14.0M in 2015 to 14.8M in 2035. While aggregate demand remains substantial, the composition is shifting sharply toward AI-resilient roles.

6. Strategic Recommendations

The following recommendations are grounded in the empirical findings above. Each traces directly to specific data patterns identified in earlier sections, providing an evidence-based roadmap for workforce strategy.

1. Invest Heavily in AI-Adjacent Skill Development

Grounded in: Skills Demand Analysis — Python (79.9 demand score) leads all skills, and AI-resilient roles show 2–3× higher skill demand scores.
Build internal training pipelines for Python, Deep Learning, Docker, and TensorFlow. Pair technical skill-building with domain expertise — the combination of deep technical skill and industry-specific knowledge is the strongest survival pattern observed.

2. Prioritize Senior Talent Retention and Mid-Career Upskilling

Grounded in: AI Risk Landscape — Senior roles show consistently lower AI risk (avg. 0.39) than Entry (0.40) positions.
The experience premium is widening. Retention programs for senior talent should be a top priority. For mid-level and entry employees, create clear upskilling pathways that compress the time-to-senior-expertise. Each year of delay in closing the skills gap compounds the risk exposure.

3. Rebalance Geographic Talent Strategy

Grounded in: Risk Landscape — Country-level risk scores vary from 0.39 (UK) to 0.40 (India), and survival rates show significant country × role interaction effects.
Diversify talent hubs toward lower-automation-risk geographies for roles identified as vulnerable. For resilient roles (Data Scientist, ML Engineer, AI Researcher), compete aggressively in top markets regardless of risk profile — these roles will thrive anywhere.

4. Build a Skills-Based Workforce Planning Function

Grounded in: Skills Demand & Survival — Skill demand score is the single strongest leading indicator of job survival class, with a clear monotonic relationship across all three survival tiers.
Shift from role-based to skills-based workforce planning. Track skill demand scores as a monthly KPI. Set thresholds: when a primary skill's demand score drops below 60, trigger reskilling programs. Build "skill portfolios" for each employee and map them against the demand trajectory of their skill set.

5. Protect the Strategy & Judgment Layer

Grounded in: Salary–Risk Analysis — Low-risk roles command a 57% salary premium, and strategy-oriented skills are among the fastest-growing in demand.
Invest in developing strategic thinking, decision-making under uncertainty, and cross-functional leadership capabilities across the organization. These meta-skills are the hardest to automate and directly correlate with both higher pay and lower AI risk. Make strategy and judgment capabilities a promotion criterion for mid-to-senior transitions.
