The data, sourced from Kaggle's AI Job Risk and Salary Dataset, is synthetically generated but modeled on realistic global job market trends. 2. AI Automation Risk Landscape

This section analyzes the distribution and trajectory of AI risk scores across roles, countries, and experience levels. The ai_risk_score ranges from 0 (no automation threat) to 1 (full automation expected).

2.1 Overall Risk Distribution

Across all 12,343 records: 48.7% fall into Medium Risk, 32.7% into Low Risk, and 18.6% into High Risk. The overall mean risk score stands at 0.40.

2.2 Risk Score Trend Over Time

The average AI risk score has risen steadily from 0.30 in 2015 to 0.51 in 2035—a 72% increase over two decades. The acceleration is particularly notable from 2024 onward, coinciding with projected rapid AI capability gains.

2.3 Risk by Job Title

Risk varies dramatically across roles. Business Analysts (0.75) and Data Analysts (0.65) face the highest average risk, while AI Researchers (0.15) and Cybersecurity Analysts (0.25) face the lowest. The gap between the most and least exposed roles is 0.60 points—a 5x difference.

2.4 Risk Trajectories by Role (2015–2035)

All 10 roles show linear, upward-sloping risk trajectories. Business Analysts' risk climbs from 0.65 to 0.85 by 2035. Even the most resilient role—AI Researcher—sees its risk score increase from 0.05 to 0.25, though it remains far below the average.

2.5 Risk by Country

Geographic risk variation is minimal. India (0.40) and USA (0.40) lead by narrow margins, while the UK (0.39) sits lowest. This near-uniformity suggests that AI automation risk is not a regional phenomenon but a global structural shift.

2.6 Risk by Experience Level

Experience level has negligible impact on automation risk. Mid-level (0.399), Entry (0.396), and Senior (0.393) professionals face virtually identical risk profiles. Seniority does not insulate workers from AI disruption.

2.7 Risk by Education Level

Similarly, education level shows no meaningful differentiation: Master's (0.397), PhD (0.396), and Bachelor's (0.395) holders all face nearly equivalent risk. The driver of automation risk is the nature of the role, not the educational credential.

3. Salary Trends & Segmentation

This section examines how compensation has evolved across time, geography, roles, and experience—and crucially, how salary levels relate to AI automation risk.

3.1 Overall Salary Trend (2015–2035)

The overall median salary has grown from $24,247 in 2015 to $36,763 in 2035—a 52% increase over 21 years, averaging roughly 2.0% compound annual growth. Growth has been uneven: relatively flat from 2015–2021, then accelerating from 2022 onward.

3.2 Salary by Country

The USA commands the highest median salary ($43,422), roughly 3.5x India's ($12,448). All six countries show upward salary trends, with the USA and UK growing most aggressively in absolute terms.

3.3 Salary by Job Title

AI Researchers earn the highest median salary ($42,845), followed by ML Engineers ($39,327) and Product Managers ($37,947). Data Analysts ($18,692) and Business Analysts ($23,082) earn the least. Notably, these lowest-paid roles are also the highest-risk for automation.

3.4 Salary by Experience Level

Seniority drives compensation strongly. Senior professionals earn a median of $48,318—2.1x the Entry-level median of $22,924. Mid-level sits at $32,996.

3.5 Salary by Education Level

Education level shows almost no impact on median salary: PhD ($31,672), Bachelor's ($31,569), and Master's ($31,434) holders earn within $238 of each other. In this dataset, the role and experience level matter far more than formal education for compensation.

3.6 Salary × Automation Risk Correlation

This is among the most critical findings in the report. There is a negative correlation of −0.24 between salary and AI risk score. The data shows a clear inverse relationship:

High Risk roles earn 37% less than Low Risk roles. The median salary for High Risk positions ($21,683) is substantially below Medium Risk ($34,710) and Low Risk ($34,105). This means that the workers most likely to be displaced by AI are also the ones least financially prepared to weather a transition.

3.7 Salary Trends by Role (2015–2035)

Among the top-performing roles, Cloud Engineers show the strongest salary growth trajectory (from $29,249 to $43,608, +49%), while AI Researchers climb from $33,753 to $49,023 (+45%). Even high-risk Business Analysts see salary growth (from $18,012 to $26,457, +47%), but from a much lower base.

4. Skills Demand & Future-Proofing

This section identifies which primary skills carry the highest market demand, how that demand is evolving, and how skill choice correlates with job survival prospects.

4.1 Average Skill Demand Scores

Skill demand scores are remarkably flat across the nine primary skills, ranging from 78.9 (Security) to 79.9 (Java). This near-uniformity suggests that market demand for these core technology skills remains broad-based rather than concentrated.

4.2 Skill Demand Trends Over Time

Year-over-year demand scores show no clear directional trend for any individual skill. All nine skills fluctuate within a narrow band of roughly 75–85 throughout the 21-year period. The market is not abandoning any of these skills; demand remains stable.

4.3 Skill × Job Survival

While demand scores are flat, the relationship between skills and job survival is stark. The chart below shows the percentage of records achieving “strong survival” (class 2) versus “at risk” (class 0) for each primary skill.

Deep Learning is the most future-proof skill. 88.9% of Deep Learning records achieve strong survival with 0% at-risk. Python follows at 57.9% strong survival. These skills are primarily associated with AI Researcher, ML Engineer, and Data Scientist roles.
Excel and SQL carry the highest displacement risk. Excel has 57.0% at-risk and 0% strong survival. SQL shows 43.5% at-risk and 0% strong survival. These skills anchor the Business Analyst and Data Analyst roles, which face the highest automation exposure.

6. Strategic Recommendations

The following recommendations are grounded directly in the data findings presented in this report. Each recommendation traces to specific evidence from the analysis.

  • 1. Launch urgent reskilling programs for Business Analysts and Data Analysts.

    These two roles represent the most acute displacement risk in the dataset. A phased transition program should move these professionals toward AI-augmented analytics, data science, or product management roles where survival rates are materially higher.

    Evidence: Business Analysts face 0.75 avg risk score, 57% at-risk rate, and 0% strong survival. Data Analysts face 0.65 avg risk, 43.5% at-risk rate, and 0% strong survival. No education level mitigates this risk. (Sections 2.3, 5.2, 5.5)
  • 2. Invest heavily in AI/ML talent pipelines.

    AI Researchers and ML Engineers represent the strongest long-term talent investments by every metric: lowest risk, highest survival, highest salaries, and growing demand. Organizations should expand hiring, develop internal talent ladders, and create competitive retention packages for these roles.

    Evidence: AI Researchers: 0.15 avg risk, 88.9% strong survival, $42,845 median salary. ML Engineers: 0.25 avg risk, 71.2% strong survival, $39,327 median salary. (Sections 2.3, 3.3, 5.2)
  • 3. Prioritize Deep Learning and Python in technical development programs.

    These two skills are the strongest predictors of job resilience. Upskilling existing workforce in these areas—particularly reskilling Excel- and SQL-dependent professionals—offers the highest ROI for displacement mitigation.

    Evidence: Deep Learning: 88.9% strong survival, 0% at-risk. Python: 57.9% strong survival, 0.1% at-risk. Excel: 0% strong survival, 57% at-risk. SQL: 0% strong survival, 43.5% at-risk. (Section 4.3)
  • 4. Strengthen Cybersecurity and DevOps as AI-resistant workforce pillars.

    Both roles maintain 0% at-risk rates and above-50% strong survival across the entire time horizon. As AI adoption scales, demand for secure infrastructure and automation operations will grow. These roles should feature prominently in workforce planning.

    Evidence: Cybersecurity Analyst: 0.25 avg risk, 53.2% strong survival. DevOps Engineer: 0.30 avg risk, 55.9% strong survival. Both show 0% at-risk rates. (Sections 2.3, 5.2)
  • 5. Apply a global rather than regional lens to workforce strategy.

    AI automation risk is remarkably uniform across all six countries analyzed. Organizations should not assume that certain geographies are insulated from automation. Reskilling and talent strategy should be applied globally with role-specific, not geography-specific, targeting.

    Evidence: Country risk scores range only from 0.393 (UK) to 0.401 (India)—a spread of just 0.008 points. All countries show similar ~0.2-point increases from 2015 to 2035. (Section 2.5)
  • 6. Address the pay-risk compounding effect.

    The negative salary-risk correlation (−0.24) means that displaced workers from high-risk roles will have fewer financial resources to fund their own transitions. Employer-funded reskilling, transition stipends, and bridge compensation should be part of workforce transformation planning, particularly for lower-paid, high-risk roles.

    Evidence: High Risk median salary ($21,683) is 37% below Low Risk ($34,105). Business Analysts in India earn a median of ~$7,500–$9,000 while facing 0.75+ risk scores. (Sections 3.6, 3.2)
  • 7. Monitor Software Engineers as an emerging risk category.

    Software Engineers currently sit in the moderate zone with only 20.5% strong survival and a rising risk trajectory (0.35 in 2015 to 0.55 in 2035). While not yet in the danger zone, this role—one of the largest in the tech workforce—warrants active monitoring and proactive upskilling toward AI-augmented development.

    Evidence: Software Engineer risk rises from 0.35 to 0.55 (+0.20 over the period), with only 20.5% strong survival and 2.6% at-risk. The Java/Master's combination drops to 18.1% strong survival. (Sections 2.4, 5.2, 5.5)

Methodology & Data Notes

Data Source: Kaggle AI Job Risk and Salary Dataset (2015–2035), containing 12,343 synthetically generated records modeled on realistic global job market assumptions. The dataset covers 10 job titles, 6 countries, 3 experience levels, 3 education levels, and 9 primary skills across a 21-year time horizon.

Limitations: As a synthetic dataset, results should be interpreted as illustrative trend projections rather than observed market data. Future years (2025–2035) are modeled projections, not forecasts. Salary values are in USD and do not adjust for purchasing power parity. The near-perfect linear risk trajectories per role reflect the dataset's construction methodology.

Analysis: All statistics (means, medians, correlations, distributions) are computed directly from the raw dataset with no imputation or transformation. Survival rates are calculated as the proportion of records with job_survival_class ≥ 1.
