Executive Overview

This report examines 12,343 records spanning 2015 to 2035 across 6 countries and 10 job titles to quantify how AI automation is reshaping the global job market. The dataset blends historical observations (2015–2024) with simulated projections (2025–2035), offering a forward-looking lens for workforce planning.

Five Critical Takeaways

  1. Rising automation threat: The average AI risk score climbs from 0.30 in 2015 to 0.51 by 2035—a 72% relative increase. Every job title shows upward risk movement, with no role fully immune.
  2. Salary growth is real but uneven: Global median salary rises from $24,247 to $36,763 (51.6% growth), yet the gap between low-risk and high-risk roles widens. Low-risk jobs command a 103% salary premium over high-risk roles by 2035 ($49,023 vs. $24,136).
  3. Two roles face existential pressure: Business Analyst and Data Analyst are the only titles with survival rates below 60% (43.0% and 56.5%, respectively). Every other role maintains ≥97% survival.
  4. Skills are shifting, not collapsing: Java and Python show the strongest demand growth (+5.1% and +3.7% from 2015 to 2035), while Docker and Deep Learning demand softens. This signals a rotation toward general-purpose engineering skills rather than a uniform decline.
  5. Geography matters less for risk, more for pay: AI risk scores are remarkably consistent across countries (0.393–0.401), but median salaries vary 3.5× from India ($12,448) to the USA ($43,422). Reskilling investments should be calibrated to local salary baselines.
Bottom line for leadership: The data shows a bifurcating labor market. Roles anchored in judgment, security, and advanced engineering (AI Researcher, Cybersecurity Analyst, ML Engineer) are structurally resilient and well-compensated. Roles heavy on structured data manipulation and routine analysis (Business Analyst, Data Analyst) face compressing demand, stagnant survival rates, and widening pay gaps. Workforce strategy should prioritize selective investment in resilient roles and urgent reskilling for at-risk cohorts.

One of the report’s highest-value insights is the explicit relationship between compensation and automation exposure. The dataset reveals a moderate negative correlation of −0.245 between salary and AI risk score: higher-paid roles tend to face lower automation risk.

The median salary breakdown by risk category is:

  • Medium Risk: $34,710
  • Low Risk: $34,105
  • High Risk: $21,683
Why do medium-risk roles pay slightly more than low-risk roles? The medium-risk band captures high-value technical roles such as Data Scientist, Cloud Engineer, and Software Engineer—positions that are well-compensated but face measurable AI encroachment (e.g., code generation, automated cloud orchestration). Low-risk roles include Cybersecurity Analyst and AI Researcher, which are secure but not always the highest-paid. The true penalty is reserved for the high-risk tier, where Business Analyst and Data Analyst salaries lag by 38%.

Strategic Recommendations

Every recommendation below is grounded in a specific data finding. Generic advice has been filtered out.

1. Prioritize reskilling Business Analyst and Data Analyst cohorts immediately

Data basis: These are the only two roles with survival rates below 60% (43.0% and 56.5%, respectively) and zero strong survival. Their AI risk scores are the highest (0.75 and 0.62) and rising by +0.20 points through 2035.

Action: Launch targeted upskilling programs moving these professionals into Python, AWS, or Security skill tracks. Given that experience level does not materially reduce risk, reskilling should be offered across all seniority tiers.

2. Expand investment in AI Researcher and Cybersecurity Analyst pipelines

Data basis: Both roles show 100% survival, 100% strong survival for AI Researchers with Deep Learning + Master’s, and median salaries of $42,845 (AI Researcher) and $35,282 (Cybersecurity Analyst). Their AI risk scores are the lowest (0.15 and 0.24).

Action: Increase university partnerships, certification sponsorships, and internal rotation programs into these domains. The USA and UK show the strongest salary premiums for these roles.

3. Calibrate compensation strategy to the risk–pay correlation

Data basis: The salary–risk correlation is −0.245. High-risk roles earn a median of $21,683 vs. $34,710 for medium-risk roles—a 38% gap. By 2035, the low-risk salary premium over high-risk reaches 103%.

Action: Use AI risk score as an input to retention budgeting. High-risk roles experiencing pay compression are likely to see accelerated attrition as workers seek safer skill domains. Preemptive retention bonuses or transition support can reduce turnover costs.

4. Rebalance skill training budgets toward Java, Python, and AWS

Data basis: Java demand grows +5.1%, Python +3.7%, and AWS +3.5% from 2015 to 2035. Docker (−3.2%) and Deep Learning (−1.5%) are losing relative demand. These trends suggest a shift from niche AI specializations toward foundational engineering infrastructure.

Action: Redirect 15–20% of L&D spend from declining specializations to general-purpose engineering and cloud skills. This aligns training with where market demand is heading, not where it has been.

5. Treat geography as a cost variable, not a risk variable

Data basis: AI risk scores vary by only 0.008 across countries (0.393–0.401), but median salaries vary 3.5× ($12,448 in India vs. $43,422 in USA). India shows the highest salary growth (+78.4%) but the lowest survival rate (78.6%).

Action: Offshore high-risk, cost-sensitive work (e.g., routine data processing) to lower-wage markets while reserving resilient, high-value roles (AI Research, Cybersecurity) in higher-wage hubs. This arbitrages the salary gap without assuming geographic risk differentiation.

6. Monitor the "moderate survival" trap

Data basis: Strong survival (Class 2) declines from 45.6% (2015) to 36.0% (2035), while overall survival only drops from 91.1% to 83.7%. This means more jobs are slipping from "strong" to "moderate" resilience.

Action: Institute quarterly workforce risk audits tracking not just headcount but survival-class distribution. A role moving from Class 2 to Class 1 is an early warning signal for automation encroachment.

Summary for the board: The labor market is not collapsing—it is bifurcating. The data shows a clear path for workforce strategy: double down on resilient, high-skill domains (AI Research, Cybersecurity, ML Engineering); urgently reskill vulnerable cohorts (Business Analysts, Data Analysts); and use the salary–risk correlation as a predictive signal for retention and investment priorities.
