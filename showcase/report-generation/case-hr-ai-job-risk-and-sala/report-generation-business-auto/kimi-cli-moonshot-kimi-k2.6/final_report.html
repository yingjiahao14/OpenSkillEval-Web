AI Automation Risk Landscape

Analyze the distribution of AI risk scores and risk categories across job titles, countries, and experience levels. Identify the roles and regions most and least vulnerable to automation.

The dataset reveals a clear hierarchy of automation vulnerability. Business Analysts and Data Analysts dominate the high-risk category, accounting for 100% and 75.9% of their respective role populations. Conversely, AI Researchers, Cybersecurity Analysts, and ML Engineers are exclusively low- or medium-risk, with zero records in the high-risk category.

By 2035, every role in the dataset shows a higher AI risk score than in 2015. The rate of increase is remarkably consistent—each role's risk score rises by exactly 0.01 per year—suggesting the dataset models a linear diffusion of automation capability across occupational domains.

Risk by Experience Level

Experience level shows minimal variation in average AI risk (Entry: 0.396, Mid: 0.399, Senior: 0.393). However, the consequences of risk differ dramatically by experience, as survival rates diverge sharply.

High Risk Distribution

Business Analysts account for 1,312 of all high-risk records (57% of BA records), while Data Analysts contribute 982 (43.5% of DA records). No other role exceeds 3% high-risk representation.

Critical Finding: Geography does not materially alter AI risk scores—all six countries cluster tightly around the global mean (0.393–0.401). This indicates automation risk is primarily role-driven, not region-driven. Workforce strategists should prioritize role-based reskilling over geographic relocation as a mitigation strategy.

Salary Trends & Segmentation

Examine salary evolution over time by country, job title, experience level, and education level. Highlight salary bucket distributions and how compensation correlates with AI risk.

Global median salary increased from $24,247 in 2015 to $36,763 in 2035—a 52% nominal rise. However, the trajectory is non-linear: salaries dipped during 2020–2021 (likely reflecting pandemic-era modeling) before resuming growth. By 2034, the median peaked at $39,801 before a slight correction in 2035.

The country spread is substantial. The USA maintains the highest median salary throughout the period ($43,422 overall), while India remains the lowest ($12,448). The USA–India salary gap is 3.5×, though India's median grew 78% over the period versus the USA's 51%.

Salary × Automation Risk Correlation

A critical finding for workforce strategists: there is a negative correlation (−0.245) between salary and AI risk score. Roles with higher automation exposure tend to command lower compensation. This relationship is stark when comparing risk categories:

Job Survival & Market Outlook

Analyze job survival class distributions and job opening trends over time. Determine which combinations of role, skill, education, and geography yield the strongest job survival prospects through 2035.

The overall job survival rate (classes 1 and 2 combined) declined from 91.1% in 2015 to 83.7% in 2035. The "strong survival" class (2) fell from 45.6% to 36.0%, while the "at risk" class (0) nearly doubled from 8.9% to 16.3%. This is the most consequential trend for workforce planning: not only are more roles at risk, but fewer roles offer strong resilience.

Survival by Job Title

Four roles achieve 100% survival (no at-risk records): AI Researcher, Cybersecurity Analyst, ML Engineer, and DevOps Engineer. At the other extreme, Business Analyst has a 57.0% at-risk rate with zero strong survival records, and Data Analyst has 43.5% at risk with zero strong survival.

Strategic Recommendations

Synthesize findings into actionable recommendations for workforce planning, reskilling investment, and talent acquisition strategy.

1

Redirect Reskilling Budget from Excel/SQL-Only Programs to Python and Security

Business Analysts (57% at risk) and Data Analysts (43.5% at risk) show zero strong survival records. Workers in these roles whose primary skill is Excel or SQL should be prioritized for reskilling into Python, Security, or Deep Learning competencies. The data shows these target skills are associated with 100% survival rates in their respective roles.

2

Prioritize Senior-Level Retention in High-Risk Roles

Experience is the strongest buffer against job loss: senior workers have a 94.5% survival rate versus 83.9% for entry-level. Even within high-risk roles like Business Analyst, senior experience improves outcomes. Retention programs for senior staff in transitioning roles are higher-ROI than entry-level hiring in those same roles.

3

Expand Cybersecurity and AI Research Hiring Pipelines

Cybersecurity Analyst and AI Researcher roles show 100% survival, 0% high-risk classification, and strong salary growth (59.6% and 45.2% respectively). Cybersecurity job openings also grew fastest (+49.1% 2015→2035). Talent acquisition should aggressively build pipelines in these domains, including partnerships with universities and certification providers.

4

Address the India Talent Risk–Compensation Gap

India has the lowest survival rate (78.6%) and median salary ($12,448) of all six countries, with 21.4% of its workforce at risk. While India's salary growth is strong (+78%), the absolute gap and survival deficit suggest a need for targeted upskilling investments and potential geographic rebalancing of high-risk functions.

5

Use Salary–Risk Correlation to Benchmark Compensation Strategy

The negative salary–risk correlation (−0.245) and the $13,000 median gap between high-risk and low-risk categories indicate the market is already pricing automation exposure. Organizations should audit their compensation bands: if high-risk roles are not paying a meaningful premium over low-risk alternatives, talent will migrate. Conversely, low-risk roles may command a "resilience premium" that should be factored into workforce cost models.

6

Monitor the Declining "Strong Survival" Rate as an Early Warning

The proportion of roles classified as "strong survival" (class 2) fell from 45.6% (2015) to 36.0% (2035). This 9.6 percentage point decline is a leading indicator of structural labor market compression. Workforce planning teams should track this metric quarterly—if it drops below 35%, it signals that reskilling capacity is not keeping pace with automation diffusion.
