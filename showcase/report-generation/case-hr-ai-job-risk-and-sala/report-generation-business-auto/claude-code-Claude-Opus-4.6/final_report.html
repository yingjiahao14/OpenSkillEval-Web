Critical Takeaways

  1. Automation risk is accelerating. The average AI risk score has risen from 0.295 in 2015 to a projected 0.508 by 2035 — a 72% increase. Business Analysts (0.75) and Data Analysts (0.65) face the steepest threat, while AI Researchers (0.15) and Cybersecurity Analysts (0.25) remain well-insulated.
  2. Salaries are rising, but unevenly. Median compensation has grown ~51.6% over the study period, yet the gap between high-risk and low-risk roles is widening. Low-risk AI/ML roles command significantly higher pay than high-risk analytical roles.
  3. Job survival is eroding at the margins. The overall job survival rate has declined from 91.1% to 83.7% by 2035. Senior professionals maintain a 94.5% survival rate compared to 83.9% for entry-level workers — experience is a significant buffer against automation.
  4. Skill demand is converging on technical competencies. Python, AWS, and Java show the strongest demand growth over the period. Deep Learning and Security skills maintain consistently high demand, signaling durable value in specialized technical domains.
  5. Total job openings remain relatively stable (14.0M in 2015 vs 14.8M in 2035), but the composition is shifting — openings for high-risk roles are contracting while AI/ML and cybersecurity positions expand.
Bottom line: AI is not eliminating jobs en masse, but it is systematically reshaping which roles, skills, and experience profiles command premium value. Organizations that invest in reskilling toward AI-adjacent and security-focused competencies will capture disproportionate talent advantages.

Key Findings — Automation Risk

  • Business Analysts carry the highest average risk score (0.75), more than 5× that of AI Researchers (0.15). Data Analysts follow at 0.65, making traditional analytical roles the most vulnerable category.
  • Risk is rising across all roles, but the rate differs sharply. High-risk roles are seeing accelerating scores, while roles like AI Researcher and ML Engineer are rising from a much lower base.
  • Geographic variation is modest. India (0.401) has the highest average risk, and the UK (0.393) the lowest — a spread of less than 0.01. Automation risk is a function of role, not geography.
  • Experience provides partial protection. Senior professionals average lower risk exposure than entry-level workers, but the gap narrows in highly automatable roles.
  • Education level shows minimal differentiation in risk scores, suggesting that the type of work (role/skill) matters more than credential level for automation exposure.

Key Findings — Salary Trends

  • Overall median salary has grown 51.6% from $24,247 (2015) to $36,763 (2035), reflecting both market inflation and increasing technical requirements.
  • AI Researchers command the highest median pay ($42,845), while Data Analysts earn the lowest ($18,692) — a 2.3× gap between the highest and lowest paid roles.
  • The salary-risk correlation is inverse and significant. Low-risk roles consistently earn more than high-risk roles, and this gap is widening over time. By 2035, low-risk positions command a substantial premium over high-risk roles.
  • The USA leads in absolute compensation, followed by Australia and Canada, though all six countries show upward salary trajectories.
  • Senior professionals earn substantially more than entry-level counterparts, and this experience premium is growing over time as technical specialization deepens.
Salary × Automation Risk: There is a clear inverse relationship — roles with lower AI risk scores command higher and faster-growing salaries. This suggests the market is already pricing in automation risk, creating a widening compensation gap that penalizes workers in vulnerable roles. Strategic reskilling toward low-risk, high-compensation roles offers a dual benefit.

Key Findings — Job Survival & Market Outlook

  • AI Researchers have the highest survival rate, followed by ML Engineers and Cybersecurity Analysts. Business Analysts and Data Analysts show the lowest survival rates, consistent with their high automation risk.
  • Total job openings remain relatively stable but their composition is shifting. Emerging tech roles (AI, ML, Cloud) are gaining share while traditional analytical roles contract.
  • Senior experience is the strongest survival buffer. Survival rates by experience level — Senior: 94.5%, Mid: 88.6%, Entry: 83.9% — show that accumulated expertise and institutional knowledge provide meaningful insulation from automation.
  • Country-level survival variation is minimal, suggesting automation impact is driven by role and skill profile rather than geographic location.

6. Strategic Recommendations

1. Prioritize Reskilling for High-Risk Roles Toward AI-Adjacent Competencies

Business Analysts (risk: 0.75) and Data Analysts (risk: 0.65) should be the primary targets for reskilling programs. Transition paths toward ML Engineering, Data Science, or Cloud Engineering offer the strongest risk-to-reward improvement — moving workers from >0.65 risk scores to <0.35 while accessing higher salary bands.

Evidence: Business Analysts and Data Analysts have 5× and 4.3× higher risk than AI Researchers; low-risk roles command 2× higher median salaries.

2. Invest in Python, Cloud (AWS), and Security Training at Scale

Python, AWS, and Security skills are associated with the highest survival rates and demand scores. Organizations should build enterprise-wide training pipelines for these competencies. Java and AWS show the strongest demand growth trajectories (+3.9 and +2.7 points), making them high-ROI training investments.

Evidence: Python-skilled roles average >88 demand score; AWS and Security skills are tied to survival rates >90%; Java and AWS demand growth leads all tracked skills.

3. Build Structured Career Ladders for Entry-Level Technical Talent

Entry-level workers face a 10.6 percentage-point survival disadvantage versus seniors (83.9% vs 94.5%). Structured mentorship, progressive skill certification, and accelerated experience paths can close this gap. Focus entry-level programs on high-survival skills (Deep Learning, Security, Python) to maximize early-career resilience.

Evidence: Senior survival rate is 94.5% vs Entry at 83.9%; experience level is the strongest demographic predictor of job survival.

4. Rebalance Talent Acquisition Toward AI-Resilient Roles

Shift new-hire allocation toward AI Researchers, ML Engineers, Cybersecurity Analysts, and Cloud Engineers — roles with risk scores below 0.30 and survival rates above 90%. Reduce proportional hiring for roles scoring above 0.50 unless those roles are being actively redesigned around AI augmentation.

Evidence: AI Researchers (risk: 0.15) and Cybersecurity Analysts (risk: 0.25) combine low automation exposure with strong and growing job openings.

5. Adopt a Global Workforce Strategy With Role-Based (Not Geography-Based) Risk Management

Automation risk variation by country is negligible (0.393–0.401), but role-level risk varies by 5×. Workforce planning should prioritize role transformation over geographic arbitrage. Salary optimization across countries remains relevant (USA leads), but risk mitigation must center on role and skill composition.

Evidence: Country-level risk spread is <0.01 while role-level risk spans 0.15 to 0.75. Geographic survival rate variation is similarly narrow.

6. Establish Ongoing AI Impact Monitoring Dashboards

The data shows risk scores accelerating nonlinearly — the 2015–2025 increase is smaller than the projected 2025–2035 increase. Organizations need real-time monitoring of automation exposure by role, team, and business unit to detect inflection points early and trigger reskilling programs before roles become non-viable.

Evidence: Average risk score increased from 0.295 (2015) to 0.508 (2035), with acceleration visible in the 2025–2035 projected period.
