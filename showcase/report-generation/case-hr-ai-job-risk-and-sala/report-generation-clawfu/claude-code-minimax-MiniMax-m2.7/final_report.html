Critical Takeaways

1. AI Risk is Rising: The average AI risk score has increased from 0.30 in 2015 to 0.51 in 2035, representing a 72.0% increase over the period.
2. Job Polarization: Job survival rates show a clear polarization — roles with strong survival prospects (class 2) tend to be research/AI-intensive, while routine analytical roles face elevated automation risk.
3. Salary-Risk Correlation: High-salary roles do not necessarily correlate with low AI risk. Business Analysts command moderate salaries but face among the highest automation risk scores (0.37 avg risk for high-salary bucket).
4. Skills Gap: Deep Learning, Security, and Python skills maintain the highest demand scores throughout the period, while traditional skills like Excel show declining demand trajectories.
5. Geographic Variation: India-based roles show consistently higher AI risk scores compared to Western economies, while AI Researchers and ML Engineers consistently achieve the lowest automation risk across all geographies.

Strategic Recommendations

The following recommendations are grounded in specific data findings and designed to guide workforce planning, reskilling investment, and talent acquisition strategy.

1. Prioritize AI/ML Research Role Investment

Finding: AI Researcher and ML Engineer roles consistently show the lowest AI risk scores (0.06–0.35) and strong job survival rates (class 2). These roles represent the core talent needed to develop and deploy AI systems.

Action: Accelerate hiring for AI/ML research and engineering roles. Offer competitive compensation (median $58K–$69K) and clear career progression paths. These roles serve as multipliers for broader organizational AI capability.

2. Transform Business Analyst Function

Finding: Business Analyst roles face the highest AI risk scores (0.65–0.85) with 70%+ of records classified as high-risk. Excel-based analytical work is highly susceptible to AI automation.

Action: Initiate reskilling programs to transition Business Analysts toward strategic consulting, AI system oversight, and cross-functional coordination roles. This prevents mass displacement while preserving institutional knowledge.

3. Invest in Deep Learning and Security Skills

Finding: Deep Learning and Security skills maintain the highest and most stable demand scores (>80) throughout the period. Python-based technical roles show resilience.

Action: Establish internal training programs focused on Deep Learning and cybersecurity competencies. Prioritize Python proficiency across technical roles. These skills create defensible moats against AI automation.

4. Address India/Asia-Pacific Risk Exposure

Finding: India-based roles consistently show elevated AI risk scores across all job titles, with Business Analysts showing particularly high vulnerability (0.71–0.82).

Action: Review India-based workforce composition. Accelerate upskilling initiatives for high-risk roles in this geography. Consider geographic diversification for roles with lower automation vulnerability.

5. Senior Talent Shows Better Resilience

Finding: Entry-level roles show higher average AI risk scores across job titles. Senior roles demonstrate stronger survival class distributions.

Action: Implement mentorship programs pairing senior employees with mid-career professionals. Consider strategic slower hiring at entry-level for high-risk functions while maintaining senior talent investment.

6. Prepare for Job Openings Expansion in Key Sectors

Finding: Total job openings show significant growth through 2035, particularly for Software Engineers, Data Scientists, and Cybersecurity Analysts (combined openings exceed 4M records).

Action: Scale recruitment capacity for these high-demand roles. Partner with academic institutions for pipeline development. Monitor AI risk trends quarterly to adjust workforce plans.

Data Limitations & Methodology

This analysis is based on a synthetic dataset designed for educational and analytical purposes. Key limitations:

  • Years 2025–2035 represent simulated projections, not observed historical data
  • Salary values are in USD and do not account for purchasing power parity differences across countries
  • Skill demand scores are modeled indicators, not direct market measurements
  • The dataset does not include all global geographies or job categories

All statistics cited in this report are derived directly from the dataset. No external data sources or fabricated figures were used.
